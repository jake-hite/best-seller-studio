## Logline

A dorky alchemist who'd rather log humidity readings than talk to people has to invent a cure that doesn't exist to save her dying sister — and the only person who's ever solved this exact problem is the woman her matriarchy's own courts quietly call a cautionary tale.

## Back-Cover Blurb

Wren Wickern is not the kind of woman Aldermere's Riverbed Council expects to matter. Her sister Mari is: luminous, self-sacrificing, the kind of healer the matriarchy holds up as the proof its emotional magic works exactly as intended. Wren just logs the readings, minds the ledger, and talks to a half-alive wyrm scale named Bartholomew more easily than she talks to most people.

Then Mari overextends past safe recovery, and the slow, irreversible slide toward Dissolution begins — and the guild's own register has no cure for what's happening to her.

The one person who's done this before, and survived it, is Vesper Reed: brilliant, ruined, and severed — by her own hand — from the obligation that should bind her feelings to anyone else's good. The Court calls her a warning. Wren is increasingly certain she's the only teacher left who can show her what an open hand can actually hold.

To save her sister, Wren will have to become the one thing her whole life has been quietly built to avoid: open, on purpose, to the full weight of someone else's pain — without the buffer, the ledger, or the careful distance that have always kept her safe.

*The Open Hand* is a secondary-world fantasy about a matriarchy with a real mechanism and a real cost, and about the particular courage of a woman who was never supposed to need any.

## Editorial Synopsis (may reveal ending)

Aldermere's matriarchy is mechanically derived from the Permeance, the setting's magic system: emotional openness literally fuels the ability to "open" toward another person's feelings, and the gender-coded cultural expectation that women are more emotionally fluent has calcified into who holds institutional power. Wren Wickern, a Material-opener (alchemist) rather than a Person-opener, occupies the least prestigious, gender-mixed specialization — by design, the path the culture doesn't watch closely.

Wren's sister Mari, a healer who has spent three years over-giving to patients with no edges to her own schedule, begins sliding toward Saturation and then Dissolution — a terminal failure state of the magic system — after a Bleed event (Ch.9). Wren's search for a cure leads her to Vesper Reed (Ch.11), a magistrate-turned-pariah who once nearly died saving a Council member's life and, in the aftermath, invented the Severance: a way to sever feeling from moral obligation while still feeling everything in full resolution. The Court treats Vesper as a cautionary tale; the reform faction (Iris Hale) treats her as a buried asset.

Wren's own invention — the "buffer," opening toward a saturated wyrm scale to interpose it between herself and a flooding person, later refined into "seeding" (Ch.12) — lets her hold Mari's condition at bay but not cure it. The midpoint (Ch.11) and Pinch Two (Ch.16, a near-Dissolution event) escalate the stakes and bring Wren and Vesper into close, charged collaboration. The Plot Turn Two hinge (Ch.18) reveals the Severance's hidden mechanism: it quiets obligation but leaves the feeling itself unusable as fuel for high-stakes rescue work, meaning every major rescue Vesper has performed in 26 years required borrowing someone else's still-obligated feeling — and that her past "mercy" toward four young practitioners (offering them Severance after they'd already fueled a rescue for her) was never as separable from her own need as she told herself.

In the climax (Ch.19), Wren refuses both the buffer (too cautious) and Severance (too costly to her sister and to herself), and instead opens fully — unbuffered, on purpose — using a synthesis she calls the "cupped hand": holding the full weight of Mari's flood without either freezing against it or being replaced by it. Mari is fully restored, at the cost of a permanent, voluntary porousness Wren will carry from now on. In the same scene, Wren also opens, briefly and without calculation, toward Vesper herself — the first time in 26 years anyone has felt Vesper plainly, without asking anything of her in return. The denouement (Ch.20) shows the new equilibrium: Mari learning to say no without guilt, Vesper stepping back from solo rescue work toward documentation and the reform faction, Wren returning to her ledger by choice rather than avoidance, and a slow-building, undeclared relationship with Bram left intentionally unresolved and unhurried.

## Cover Brief

**Genre signal**: secondary-world fantasy, adult/upper-YA crossover, single-POV, low body count, high interiority. Should read as warm and character-forward, not grimdark — closer to *Uprooted*/*Spinning Silver* (Naomi Novik) and T. Kingfisher's adult fantasy than to epic-fantasy cover conventions (no swords, no armor, no battle silhouettes).

**Visual direction**: an alchemist's workbench or ledger motif (humidity gauges, a brass scale-cradle, glass retorts) rather than a heroic figure — the book's central image is competence-with-tools, not combat-readiness. The open-hand / closed-fist / cupped-hand motif (the book's load-bearing symbol) is a strong, simple central image: a single hand, cupped, with something faintly glowing or water-like pooled in the palm, suits both the title and the thematic content without spoiling plot.

**Tone cues for the artist**: warm color palette (amber, brass, river-green) over cool/dark grimdark palettes; one small, specific, slightly funny detail somewhere in the composition (a dye-beetle, a humidity gauge, a half-alive wyrm scale) to signal the book's dork-coded humor alongside its emotional seriousness.

**Audience**: adult and upper-YA crossover readers fatigued with combat-coded "strong female characters," who want competence without warrior posturing and a matriarchy built on a real mechanism rather than a costume change.
