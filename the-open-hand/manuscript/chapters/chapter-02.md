# Chapter Two — The Lowmarket Fever

Lowmarket smelled like it always did in summer, which was river silt and lamp oil and, underneath both, the particular sweetish staleness of a ward that had been running too many sickrooms for too many weeks without enough hands to keep the channels properly flushed. Confluence had been built, three centuries back, by people who understood the Permeance well enough to know that a city full of feeling things needed running water the way a body needed a pulse — every ward had its channel, every channel fed the river, every flush of current was supposed to carry off the accumulated residue of a hundred small griefs before it could pool and sour. Lowmarket's channel had been silted half-shut since spring. There hadn't been coin to dredge it, or there had been coin and no House willing to spend its name dredging a poor ward's gutters, and either way the residue here had nowhere to go, and Wren felt it the second she crossed the ward line the way you'd feel a held breath in a room — nothing dramatic, nothing dangerous to a Material-opener with her hands in her pockets, just a low, constant, unpleasant pressure, like standing too close to a fire that wasn't yours.

The heat didn't help. It was the thick, breathless kind of summer afternoon that sat on a city rather than passing through it, and Wren's shirt had gone damp between her shoulder blades before she'd covered two streets, her braid sticking unpleasantly to the back of her neck, her own pulse loud in her ears in a way that had nothing to do with resonance and everything to do with simple, undignified exertion. She could feel a blister starting on her left heel, the boots she'd grabbed without thinking the wrong pair for running, and she noted it the way she noted most physical complaints that weren't actively stopping her — a fact worth knowing, not worth stopping for — and kept moving.

She ran the last three blocks, which she would not, later, describe as running, because running implied a kind of useful urgency, and what she had actually done was move very fast in a straight line while doing the long, practiced work of not thinking about anything at all — not the boy's face, not the word *collapsed*, not the specific arithmetic of how long it had been since Mari had looked anything other than fine. Her case slammed against her hip with every stride, the strap she hadn't taken the half-second to adjust properly biting a thin line into her shoulder that she'd find, later, gone an angry red. Her lungs had started to burn somewhere around the second block, a stitch building under her ribs that she breathed around rather than slowed for, and her own heartbeat had climbed into her throat and stayed there, an unwelcome, insistent drum she couldn't talk herself out of the way she could a flinch at the cradle, because this wasn't a want she could starve. This was just a body doing what a body did when it was frightened and moving as fast as it knew how.

She passed a man selling river-reed baskets who called out her family name without quite meaning to, the way you'd call out at a passing cart you recognized, and didn't stop, and passed two boys diverting a thin trickle of greywater away from a doorstep with the bored, competent ease of children who had grown up doing exactly this kind of small unglamorous maintenance work their whole lives, and didn't stop for them either, and the closer she got to the healing house the thicker the unpleasant pressure in the air became, until it sat against her like weather — a thickness behind the eyes, a faint metallic taste at the back of her throat that had nothing to do with running, the specific signature of too much unflushed feeling sitting too long in too small a space. She passed a woman scrubbing a doorstep who looked up, registered Wren's face, and looked back down again without a word, the particular tact of a ward that had seen enough running people this summer to know better than to ask any of them to stop and explain themselves.

Lowmarket itself looked the way it always looked under stress: patched, resourceful, unglamorously dignified. Somebody had strung wet laundry between two windows directly over the silted channel, on the theory, Wren supposed, that the channel's smell could hardly get worse for the addition of damp linen, and a row of stalls that should have been shuttered for the day stood open anyway, their owners calling out prices in voices pitched to carry over a noise that wasn't there — the ordinary clatter and argument of a market square that had simply, collectively, decided not to stop functioning just because its favorite healer had gone down. Wren had grown up two wards over, in a house with a proper channel and clean water running past the back step, and she had never, until this exact summer, fully understood what it meant that Lowmarket didn't have that, that an entire neighborhood's worth of people went about their ordinary days breathing air that pressed against them like this, every day, with no House anywhere troubling itself to notice.

She found the healing house by the crowd outside it, which was its own kind of answer before she'd asked a single question. Lowmarket loved Mari with the specific fierce gratitude of a ward that had had very little reason to expect anyone competent to stay, and the dozen or so people gathered at a careful, respectful distance from the door were not there to gawk. They were there the way you stood outside a house where someone you loved was sick, which was to say: uselessly, and because leaving felt worse. Someone had brought a stool for the oldest woman in the group to sit on. Someone else had set down, near the door, an offering Wren recognized instantly for what it was even before she registered the specific shape of it — a small clay dish of river water, fresh-drawn, the old Lowmarket habit of leaving running water near a sickroom door for luck, or for the Permeance, or for both, nobody much distinguished between the two down here. An old woman near the front had a child asleep against her shoulder and a look on her face Wren recognized from her own mirror that morning, four years back, the morning their father didn't wake up — not grief yet, just the waiting room before grief, where you still believed, with your whole exhausted body, that the right news might still arrive.

A man near the back was wringing his cap in both hands, over and over, the brim gone soft and shapeless from the same motion repeated for what must have been an hour, and a girl no older than nine sat cross-legged on the cobbles drawing something in the dust with a stick, with the fierce, deliberate concentration of a child who has been told to be good and quiet and has decided the only way to manage it is to give her hands something else to do entirely. Nobody was crying. That, more than anything, told Wren how serious it actually was — a Lowmarket crowd that loved Mari as much as this one did would have been making noise if the news had been merely bad. This kind of quiet was the kind people kept for news they were still waiting to receive.

"She's family," someone said, low, as Wren pushed through, and the crowd parted the way crowds did for that word, and didn't ask her anything else. Someone else — she didn't see who — pressed a hand briefly to her shoulder as she passed, not stopping her, not slowing her down, just a single flat human gesture of *go, we're with you, go*, and Wren let it pass through her without catching, the way she'd trained herself to let most things pass through her at a run, because there would be time to feel the kindness of strangers later, and there was not time now. The girl with the stick looked up as Wren passed, her drawing in the dust some careful, looping shape Wren didn't have time to identify, and said nothing, only watched her go with the particular solemn attention children gave to adults clearly living through something the children themselves weren't yet old enough to be told the name of.

The door itself stood half open, and someone had propped it that way deliberately, Wren registered, with a stone set against its base — an old Lowmarket habit, leaving a sickroom door cracked for air and for luck both, the same impulse as the dish of river water, the ward's whole patchwork theology of small gestures meant to keep a thing from sealing itself shut and going stale.

Inside, the sickroom was dim and over-warm and smelled like willowbark and the specific copper tang Wren associated, from her training, with sustained low-grade Bleed — not the room itself, she understood, intellectually, the room couldn't bleed, but the air near a practitioner running an unaddressed Bleed for long enough took on a texture, a kind of metallic patience, the way a kitchen took on the smell of a meal that had been simmering since before you walked in. The shutters had been drawn against the afternoon glare, leaving the room in a thick amber half-light that made everything in it look slightly unreal, slightly underwater. A basin of water sat by the cot, gone cloudy, a cloth draped over its rim. Someone had brought flowers — a small, wilting handful of river-marigold, the kind that grew wild along the silted channel and that Wren's mother used, sometimes, in the dye vats — and set them in a cracked cup on the windowsill, where they'd clearly been forgotten the moment the room's attention turned to its patient.

Mari was sitting up. That was the first thing, and it was meant, Wren understood immediately, to be reassuring, and it worked on her for exactly four seconds before she got close enough to see her sister's face.

Mari's color was wrong. Not dramatically — nothing a stranger would have caught, glancing in from the doorway — but wrong in the specific way Wren had spent twenty-three years learning to read on this one particular face: too pale at the temples, a faint sheen along her hairline that wasn't quite sweat and wasn't quite anything else Wren had a name for, her lips a shade paler than the rest of her, as if some careful internal accountant had started rationing blood to the places that mattered least first. Her hair had come half out of its working braid and hung in damp pieces around her face, and someone — Petra, probably — had gotten a blanket around her shoulders despite the day's heat, the specific over-correction of people caring for someone whose actual problem they didn't yet have a name for.

"You didn't need to run the whole way," Mari said, and even her voice was doing the thing — even now, even like this, doing the warm, steady, faintly amused thing it always did, the thing that had made an entire ward fall in love with her inside a year. "I'm fine. I'm annoyingly fine. Ward healer's being dramatic."

"The ward healer sent a boy who looked like he'd run from here to the river twice," Wren said, crossing the room in three strides and crouching by the cot, close enough now to see the small tremor in Mari's jaw she was clearly working to keep out of her voice. Up close, she could see, too, the faint crusting of dried blood at one nostril, recent, the kind of small undignified detail nobody had thought to clean up yet because everyone in the room had been too busy with the larger problem. "You collapsed in front of people."

"I sat down abruptly. There's a difference."

"Mari."

"I'm *fine*," Mari said again, and reached for Wren's hand with the automatic, easy warmth she'd always had, the warmth Wren had spent her whole life being slightly, privately dazzled by and slightly, privately exhausted by in the same breath — and her hand, when Wren took it, was too warm, dry in a way that felt wrong against Wren's own clammy, sweat-damp palm, and trembling in a way Mari's hands never trembled, not once in twenty-six years, not during their father's funeral, not during the flood the year Wren turned twelve, not ever. Wren held on anyway, both hands now, the way you'd hold something you were afraid of dropping rather than something you were afraid would break, and made herself notice the exact rhythm of the tremor — not constant, but coming in small waves, building and easing and building again, the way water comes up over a lip that's almost, not quite, holding it back.

"You're shaking."

"I've been on my feet eleven hours. Anyone would shake."

"You don't shake."

Mari didn't answer that, which from Mari, who answered everything, who had an answer for every frightened patient and every furious family member and every Court inquiry that had ever come through this ward, was its own kind of answer. She looked instead toward the window, toward the wilting marigolds in their cracked cup, with an expression Wren couldn't quite read and didn't like the unreadability of one bit.

Wren wanted, badly, to do something with her hands — to check a pulse properly, to mix something, to measure anything at all, because she had no idea how to simply sit in a room and be useless at the one craft that actually mattered here, and the not-knowing felt like its own separate, smaller emergency stacked on top of the larger one. She settled, in the end, for smoothing the blanket where it had bunched at Mari's shoulder, a gesture that fixed nothing and cost her nothing and gave her hands somewhere to be other than wrapped uselessly around a feeling she didn't have a tool for.

For one unguarded second, holding her sister's too-warm hand, Wren was twelve years old again, standing in the doorway of this same kind of sickroom — a different ward, the year of the flood, a stranger's baby with a fever that wouldn't break — watching nine-year-old Mari climb up uninvited onto a cot beside a screaming, inconsolable toddler and simply *open*, no technique, no training, nothing but instinct and an apparently bottomless willingness to take someone else's fear into herself so the someone else didn't have to carry all of it alone. She could still see it with a clarity that frightened her a little, the specific physical details that had stuck: the toddler's small fists, the rain still coming down outside loud enough to hear through the shutters, the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke, her own poultice cooling in her hands because nobody had told her where to set it down and she hadn't thought, at eleven, to simply put it down herself. The toddler had gone quiet inside a minute, and the toddler's mother had wept and called Mari a blessing, an actual blessing, sent, and nobody — not their mother, not the ward healer, not a single soul in that crowded room — had so much as glanced at eleven-year-old Wren standing two feet away with a properly mixed poultice in her hands that nobody had needed and nobody had thanked her for, because a poultice, however correctly mixed, was not a miracle, and nine-year-old Mari, without trying, without even understanding yet what she'd done, had been one.

Wren had loved her sister enormously in that doorway. She had also, in some small sealed room of herself she would not have admitted existed under torture, hated being eleven and competent and entirely unnoticed standing two feet from a miracle, hated it with a hot, ugly, immediate intensity that had frightened her more than the toddler's screaming had, because it arrived alongside the love rather than instead of it, the two feelings occupying the exact same half-second of her chest with no apparent friction between them at all, as if her body had simply decided this was a perfectly normal way to feel and had never once consulted her about it. She had spent the fourteen years since teaching herself, with real and total success, not to remember that second feeling at all.

She did not remember it now, exactly. She simply noticed, distantly, that some old and well-worn groove in her chest had been touched, the way you'd notice a draft from a window you'd long since stopped consciously registering as a window, and then she set the noticing down where she set down most things she didn't have room for, because her sister's hand was shaking and that was the only fact in the room that mattered.

The ward healer — a thin, exhausted woman named Petra Vance whom Wren vaguely recognized from a guild dinner two winters back, back when Petra had still had the energy to attend guild dinners — came in a moment later with the specific careful posture of someone about to deliver news she'd already decided to soften, and then, visibly, deciding against softening it. She had the look of someone who had been awake far longer than was good for anyone: hair scraped back without much ceremony, a dark smear of something — tincture, or simply exhaustion — under each eye, and hands that moved with the brisk, slightly too-fast economy of a person rationing her own remaining energy against a day that wasn't done with her yet. She carried a small wooden tray with her, set it down on the room's single table with more care than its contents — a cup, a folded cloth, a stoppered bottle — seemed to require, the kind of careful placement of ordinary objects that Wren recognized as its own form of stalling.

"Bleed," she said, before Wren could ask. "A real one, not a scare. She's run hot for — by my count, about nine days now, though I'd guess longer. Pulse is up, residue's pooling faster than she's discharging it, which you'll have felt the second you walked in, the whole ward's running thick this summer." She said this last part the way you'd apologize for the weather, which was, Wren thought, probably the only way left to say it after the fortieth time that month. She pressed two fingers briefly to the inside of Mari's wrist as she spoke, an automatic, practiced gesture she likely no longer consciously registered herself doing, counting something under her breath before she let go again. "It's serious. It is not, as of this hour, catastrophic. Rest, properly enforced this time, and she comes back down. I've seen worse Bleeds resolve clean inside two weeks."

Wren found herself doing, without meaning to, the thing she always did with new information: trying to fit it into a column, trying to find the place on some imagined chart where *nine days* and *nine days, though longer* and *pooling faster than discharging* belonged in relation to each other, as though enough careful arrangement of the facts might by itself produce a cure. It didn't. It just gave her hands something to do while the rest of her sat very still and afraid.

"Two weeks," their mother said, from the doorway, in the voice of a woman doing very careful arithmetic against everything she actually knew about her elder daughter. She had not yet crossed the threshold properly, as if some part of her wanted to hold the doorway a moment longer before committing herself to whatever the room was about to tell her.

Hesper Wickern had arrived sometime in the last ten minutes without either of her daughters noticing, which was, Wren thought with the distant clarity of someone cataloguing facts to keep from feeling them directly, exactly the kind of thing their mother had always been quietly, devastatingly good at — arriving at the worst possible moment with no fuss whatsoever, already rolling up her sleeves, already assessing the room the way thirty years in the dye trade had taught her to assess a vat that had gone wrong: not panicking, just looking for the actual problem instead of the symptom everyone else was busy reacting to. She still had dye under two of her fingernails, blue-black, the color of a batch she'd clearly walked out on mid-process to get here, and she had not stopped to wash it off, and there was a faint blue-black smudge, too, along the inside of one wrist, where she must have wiped her hands on her apron rather than waiting for water. Her grey hair had come loose from its pins on one side, and she hadn't fixed it, which told Wren almost as much as the dye did about how fast she'd actually moved to get here, because Hesper Wickern did not, as a general rule, leave the house with her hair half down, not for fire, not for flood, not for anything Wren could remember in twenty-three years — except, apparently, for this.

"Two weeks," Petra agreed, "if she rests. Properly." She looked at Mari, not unkindly, with the flat tiredness of someone who had delivered this exact warning to this exact patient more than once already and had stopped expecting it to land. "She hasn't been resting. Not this week. Not the week before that, either. I've told her myself. The Court's liaison told her, twice, that I know of. She tells us both she's fine and walks back into the fever rows by midday."

"There weren't enough hands," Mari said, not arguing, exactly, just stating the thing she'd apparently decided was simply true and not up for discussion, her tone the same warm, reasonable register she'd have used to explain a delayed supper. "There still aren't. You know there aren't, Petra, you were there same as me."

"I was there for eleven hours a day, same as you, and I went home at the end of them," Petra said, and something in her own composure slipped, just slightly, around the edges — not anger, exactly, but the specific exhausted candor of someone too tired to keep performing patience. "You didn't. Not once in three weeks that I know of. I'd find you still here past midnight doing inventory nobody asked you to do, and I'd tell myself, she's just thorough, and then I'd find you here again at dawn before I'd even finished my own tea, and I stopped being able to tell myself that particular story somewhere around day nine."

Mari didn't answer that either. She picked at a loose thread on the blanket someone had tucked around her shoulders instead, a small, restless gesture entirely unlike her usual composed stillness, and Wren watched her sister's fingers worry the thread the way Wren's own fingers might worry a ledger's spine, and thought, with a small cold lurch, that she had never once seen Mari fidget before. Not like this. Not ever.

Wren stood there with her sister's too-warm hand in hers and did the long, silent arithmetic she'd spent her whole life being bad at out loud and competent at in private, the kind of arithmetic that had nothing to do with theory or resonance signatures and everything to do with the specific, infuriating shape of her sister's particular brand of goodness — the goodness that had made Mari beloved across an entire ward and had also, apparently, made her the kind of person who would run a Bleed for three weeks without telling anyone how bad it had actually gotten, because telling people would have meant stopping, and stopping, to Mari, had never once in her life looked like rest. It had only ever looked like failure.

She thought of all the small evidence she'd collected over the years without meaning to build a case out of it: Mari at fifteen, giving up her own seat on the last cart home so an exhausted stranger could sit, walking the final two miles in the dark rather than make a fuss about it. Mari at twenty, working through a fever of her own because the ward had "needed her that week," and being praised for it afterward by exactly the people who should have sent her home. Mari, always, doing the thing that made people's faces go soft with gratitude, and never once, in Wren's memory, doing the much smaller, much harder thing of simply saying *I can't, not today*. Wren had envied that gift for years before she'd understood, slowly, reluctantly, that it might not be a gift at all so much as a debt Mari had never learned how to stop paying.

Wren's hand had begun, without her quite noticing, to ache faintly where she gripped her sister's — not from any resonance, nothing dramatic, just the ordinary mechanical strain of holding on too hard for too long, the kind of small bodily fact she'd have logged without comment in any other context and now simply let happen, because letting go felt, at this exact moment, like its own kind of abandonment.

Hesper crossed to the cot at last, the dye still under her nails, and set her hand briefly against Mari's forehead the way she'd done since both her daughters were small, an old gesture that had nothing to do with any training and everything to do with thirty years of motherhood, and Wren watched her mother's face do, for exactly one unguarded second, something that looked far more like fear than the brisk competence she'd walked in wearing.

"She'll rest now," Hesper said, in the tone Wren remembered from childhood scraped knees and one particularly bad fever of her own, a tone that did not invite argument from anyone in the room, daughter or healer or, implicitly, the Court itself. "I'll see to that personally. Petra, what does she need that she isn't getting?"

"Quiet," Petra said. "Real quiet, not just no patients — the channel out front's silted half to nothing, the whole ward's running thick, and she's not going to discharge properly sitting in a room that's already saturated. If you can get her somewhere with clean running water and actual distance from anyone else's grief for a week, that alone would do more than anything I can put in a cup."

"We have the house upriver," Hesper said. "Father's old place. Clean water, no neighbors fool enough to bring her their troubles."

"Good." Petra picked up the cup from her tray, considered it, set it back down without using it, the small restless gesture of someone working out how to say the next part. "Good," she said again, as if repeating it might make the next sentence easier to deliver. "One more thing. A Bleed this deep, held this long without discharge — I've seen it go past recovery in healers exactly this good at convincing everyone around them they're fine. Including themselves. I'd rather scare you now and be wrong than be polite about it and be right too late."

Nobody in the room said anything to that for a moment. Mari looked, for the first time since Wren had walked in, genuinely caught — not performing reassurance, not deflecting, just looking, for one unguarded second, exactly as frightened as Wren had been since the boy showed up soaked through at the workshop door.

"I'll rest," Mari said, finally, quietly, to the room rather than to anyone in it. "I will. I promise."

Wren believed her. That was the terrible part — she believed her completely, the same way she'd have believed her at eight years old promising to share the last sweet bun, because Mari had never once in her life made a promise she didn't fully intend to keep. The trouble had never been Mari's intentions. The trouble was that Mari had also never once, in twenty-six years, correctly identified the moment at which intending to be fine stopped being the same thing as actually being fine, and Wren did not have the faintest idea how you taught someone that, especially not someone who had spent her whole career being loved, specifically and constantly, for never needing to learn it.

Petra was already moving toward the door, calling for a junior attendant to fetch a stretcher-chair, and Hesper had gone to settle the bill and the arrangements with the kind of brisk, unhurried efficiency that meant nobody watching her would have guessed, unless they knew to look, that her hands were not quite steady either. Wren stayed where she was a moment longer, still holding her sister's hand, and made herself notice, on purpose, the specific shape of the fear she was actually feeling underneath the useful, organizing fear about Bleeds and discharge rates and how many days a body had before serious became something with a worse name. It was an old fear. It had nothing to do with theory. It had to do with the fact that she had spent her entire life arranging herself around the fixed, reliable star of her sister's goodness, and some part of her, the same sealed-off part that had stood in a doorway at eleven with an unwanted poultice, did not actually know who she was supposed to be if that star ever went out.

She thought, with the small, useless clarity of someone reaching for a fact because the feeling underneath it had nowhere safe to go, that this was probably exactly the kind of problem that didn't have a measurement. You couldn't chart it. You couldn't run nine assays and arrive at a number that told you the precise hour a person crossed from holding steady to simply being held up by momentum alone. There was no gauge for it, no column in any ledger she owned, and Wren, who had built her entire professional life around the conviction that almost anything could be measured if you were patient and careful enough about it, found herself furious, in a small, contained way she didn't examine too closely, that this particular thing apparently couldn't be.

She set that fury down where she set down most things she didn't know what to do with. There would be time for it later, if later ever came, and right now there was a cart to find.

---

They got her upriver before dark, in a borrowed cart, Mari arguing the entire way that she could perfectly well walk, which nobody dignified with a response. The cart itself was an old produce cart borrowed from a neighbor, still smelling faintly of the cabbages it usually hauled, its bed lined with two stacked blankets that did nothing to soften the jolt of Confluence's cobbles, and every rut in the road sent a small shudder up through the boards that Wren felt in her own teeth. Hesper rode up front with the driver, giving directions in the clipped, practical voice of a woman who had made this exact trip a hundred times in better circumstances and was not going to let the circumstances change how she gave directions now — *left at the cooper's, mind the low branch past the second bridge, he always forgets the low branch* — her voice carrying back to them over the cart's noise with the same brisk authority she used on a vat of dye that had started to set wrong.

Wren sat in the back with her sister, watching Lowmarket give way to the wider, cleaner streets of the upper wards, watching the pressure in the air thin out by degrees the farther they got from the silted channel, until somewhere past the third bridge it lifted entirely and the ordinary summer evening reasserted itself — birdsong, the smell of someone's supper, a child laughing at something two streets over, the particular gold-orange light that came right before dusk, slanting low across rooftops that had no idea what kind of afternoon Lowmarket had just had — as if none of the last three hours had happened at all, which was its own small, specific cruelty, that a city could be in crisis on one side of a bridge and perfectly pleasant on the other and both of those things could be equally true at the same time. Wren's own stomach reminded her, with an undignified growl she pressed a hand against out of pure reflex, that she had not eaten anything since a heel of bread that morning, and she found the fact of her own hunger almost insulting, that her body would dare want something so ordinary in the middle of a day like this one, and ate anyway, eventually, half an apple Hesper passed back without comment somewhere around the second bridge, because there was no use being noble about food on an empty stomach.

Mari's head eventually, despite everything, came to rest against Wren's shoulder, heavier than Wren expected, the particular dead weight of someone whose body has finally stopped performing wellness and simply collapsed into whatever was nearest. Wren found that she did not have a single useful thing to say, not one — no theory, no ledger, no careful eleven-step process for handling something old and dangerous and only technically still alive, because her sister was none of those things, her sister was just exhausted and frightened and pretending not to be, and the only tool Wren had ever built for situations like this was the same tool she used on Bartholomew: go quiet, go careful, want nothing, give the thing in front of her nothing to lean on.

It worked, in its way. Mari's breathing evened out, slow and a little ragged at first and then, gradually, less ragged, until Wren could feel the rise and fall of it against her own arm, almost steady, almost ordinary. She slept, or came close to it, for most of the ride, and somewhere around the fifth mile Wren caught herself doing the thing she always did when she ran out of useful problems to solve, which was counting — the number of bridges (four, including the narrow footbridge that didn't technically count but which she counted anyway, out of some private completionist instinct she'd never bothered to examine), the number of lamps lit along the road, the number of stars she could pick out past the cart's canvas awning before clouds took the rest — not because the numbers meant anything, but because numbers, unlike feelings, stayed exactly where you put them. Her shoulder had gone stiff and a little numb under Mari's weight, and she did not shift to relieve it, because relieving it would have meant moving, and moving felt, irrationally, like it might wake something worse than her sister.

The driver said nothing the entire ride, a kindness Wren only registered properly afterward, the specific tact of a man who had clearly been told enough, by Hesper's face if nothing else, to know that conversation was not a service anyone in this cart currently wanted. He did, once, when a particularly bad rut threatened to throw them all sideways, reach back without a word and steady the stretcher-chair Mari was propped in with one hand, an entirely unremarkable act of competence that Wren found, in the moment, almost unbearably kind. Somewhere behind them, faint and getting fainter, Wren thought she could still feel the faintest edge of Lowmarket's pressure, like a held note finally fading out of hearing range, and then even that was gone, and there was only the river, running loud and clean and entirely indifferent off to the left, doing the one thing running water always did, which was keep moving regardless of who needed it to stop and pay attention for a moment.

It was not, Wren understood, lying awake herself in the dark of the cart with her sister's weight against her shoulder and the river running loud and clean somewhere off to the left, actually the right tool for this job. It was just the only one she had ever bothered to build, and she held onto it anyway, the whole rest of the way home, because letting go of even an inadequate tool felt, tonight, like one risk more than she could afford to take.
