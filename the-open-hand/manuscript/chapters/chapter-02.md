# Chapter Two — The Lowmarket Fever

Lowmarket smelled like it always did in summer, which was river silt and lamp oil and, underneath both, the particular sweetish staleness of a ward that had been running too many sickrooms for too many weeks without enough hands to keep the channels properly flushed. Confluence had been built, three centuries back, by people who understood the Permeance well enough to know that a city full of feeling things needed running water the way a body needed a pulse — every ward had its channel, every channel fed the river, every flush of current was supposed to carry off the accumulated residue of a hundred small griefs before it could pool and sour. Lowmarket's channel had been silted half-shut since spring. There hadn't been coin to dredge it, or there had been coin and no House willing to spend its name dredging a poor ward's gutters, and either way the residue here had nowhere to go, and Wren felt it the second she crossed the ward line the way you'd feel a held breath in a room — nothing dramatic, nothing dangerous to a Material-opener with her hands in her pockets, just a low, constant, unpleasant pressure, like standing too close to a fire that wasn't yours.

She ran the last three blocks, which she would not, later, describe as running, because running implied a kind of useful urgency, and what she had actually done was move very fast in a straight line while doing the long, practiced work of not thinking about anything at all — not the boy's face, not the word *collapsed*, not the specific arithmetic of how long it had been since Mari had looked anything other than fine. She passed a man selling river-reed baskets who called out her family name without quite meaning to, the way you'd call out at a passing cart you recognized, and didn't stop, and passed two boys diverting a thin trickle of greywater away from a doorstep with the bored, competent ease of children who had grown up doing exactly this kind of small unglamorous maintenance work their whole lives, and didn't stop for them either, and the closer she got to the healing house the thicker the unpleasant pressure in the air became, until it sat against her like weather.

She found the healing house by the crowd outside it, which was its own kind of answer before she'd asked a single question. Lowmarket loved Mari with the specific fierce gratitude of a ward that had had very little reason to expect anyone competent to stay, and the dozen or so people gathered at a careful, respectful distance from the door were not there to gawk. They were there the way you stood outside a house where someone you loved was sick, which was to say: uselessly, and because leaving felt worse. An old woman near the front had a child asleep against her shoulder and a look on her face Wren recognized from her own mirror that morning, four years back, the morning their father didn't wake up — not grief yet, just the waiting room before grief, where you still believed, with your whole exhausted body, that the right news might still arrive.

"She's family," someone said, low, as Wren pushed through, and the crowd parted the way crowds did for that word, and didn't ask her anything else. Someone else — she didn't see who — pressed a hand briefly to her shoulder as she passed, not stopping her, not slowing her down, just a single flat human gesture of *go, we're with you, go*, and Wren filed it away the way she filed everything when she didn't have time to feel it properly yet, in the same patient mental drawer where she kept the things she'd look at later, if later ever came.

Inside, the sickroom was dim and over-warm and smelled like willowbark and the specific copper tang Wren associated, from her training, with sustained low-grade Bleed — not the room itself, she understood, intellectually, the room couldn't bleed, but the air near a practitioner running an unaddressed Bleed for long enough took on a texture, a kind of metallic patience, the way a kitchen took on the smell of a meal that had been simmering since before you walked in.

Mari was sitting up. That was the first thing, and it was meant, Wren understood immediately, to be reassuring, and it worked on her for exactly four seconds before she got close enough to see her sister's face.

"You didn't need to run the whole way," Mari said, and even her voice was doing the thing — even now, even like this, doing the warm, steady, faintly amused thing it always did, the thing that had made an entire ward fall in love with her inside a year. "I'm fine. I'm annoyingly fine. Ward healer's being dramatic."

"The ward healer sent a boy who looked like he'd run from here to the river twice," Wren said. "You collapsed in front of people."

"I sat down abruptly. There's a difference."

"Mari."

"I'm *fine*," Mari said again, and reached for Wren's hand with the automatic, easy warmth she'd always had, the warmth Wren had spent her whole life being slightly, privately dazzled by and slightly, privately exhausted by in the same breath — and her hand, when Wren took it, was too warm, and trembling in a way Mari's hands never trembled, not once in twenty-six years, not during their father's funeral, not during the flood the year Wren turned twelve, not ever.

"You're shaking."

"I've been on my feet eleven hours. Anyone would shake."

"You don't shake."

Mari didn't answer that, which from Mari, who answered everything, who had an answer for every frightened patient and every furious family member and every Court inquiry that had ever come through this ward, was its own kind of answer.

For one unguarded second, holding her sister's too-warm hand, Wren was twelve years old again, standing in the doorway of this same kind of sickroom — a different ward, the year of the flood, a stranger's baby with a fever that wouldn't break — watching nine-year-old Mari climb up uninvited onto a cot beside a screaming, inconsolable toddler and simply *open*, no technique, no training, nothing but instinct and an apparently bottomless willingness to take someone else's fear into herself so the someone else didn't have to carry all of it alone, and the toddler had gone quiet inside a minute, and the toddler's mother had wept and called Mari a blessing, an actual blessing, sent, and nobody — not their mother, not the ward healer, not a single soul in that crowded room — had so much as glanced at eleven-year-old Wren standing two feet away with a properly mixed poultice in her hands that nobody had needed and nobody had thanked her for, because a poultice, however correctly mixed, was not a miracle, and nine-year-old Mari, without trying, without even understanding yet what she'd done, had been one. Wren had loved her sister enormously in that doorway. She had also, in some small sealed room of herself she would not have admitted existed under torture, hated being eleven and competent and entirely unnoticed standing two feet from a miracle, and she had spent the fourteen years since teaching herself, with real and total success, not to remember that second feeling at all.

She did not remember it now, exactly. She simply noticed, distantly, that some old and well-worn groove in her chest had been touched, the way you'd notice a draft from a window you'd long since stopped consciously registering as a window, and then she put it away, because her sister's hand was shaking and that was the only fact in the room that mattered.

The ward healer — a thin, exhausted woman named Petra Vance whom Wren vaguely recognized from a guild dinner two winters back, back when Petra had still had the energy to attend guild dinners — came in a moment later with the specific careful posture of someone about to deliver news she'd already decided to soften, and then, visibly, deciding against softening it.

"Bleed," she said, before Wren could ask. "A real one, not a scare. She's run hot for — by my count, about nine days now, though I'd guess longer. Pulse is up, residue's pooling faster than she's discharging it, which you'll have felt the second you walked in, the whole ward's running thick this summer." She said this last part the way you'd apologize for the weather, which was, Wren thought, probably the only way left to say it after the fortieth time that month. "It's serious. It is not, as of this hour, catastrophic. Rest, properly enforced this time, and she comes back down. I've seen worse Bleeds resolve clean inside two weeks."

"Two weeks," their mother said, from the doorway, in the voice of a woman doing very careful arithmetic against everything she actually knew about her elder daughter.

Hesper Wickern had arrived sometime in the last ten minutes without either of her daughters noticing, which was, Wren thought with the distant clarity of someone cataloguing facts to keep from feeling them directly, exactly the kind of thing their mother had always been quietly, devastatingly good at — arriving at the worst possible moment with no fuss whatsoever, already rolling up her sleeves, already assessing the room the way thirty years in the dye trade had taught her to assess a vat that had gone wrong: not panicking, just looking for the actual problem instead of the symptom everyone else was busy reacting to. She still had dye under two of her fingernails, blue-black, the color of a batch she'd clearly walked out on mid-process to get here, and she had not stopped to wash it off.

"Two weeks," Petra agreed, "if she rests. Properly." She looked at Mari, not unkindly, with the flat tiredness of someone who had delivered this exact warning to this exact patient more than once already and had stopped expecting it to land. "She hasn't been resting. Not this week. Not the week before that, either. I've told her myself. The Court's liaison told her, twice, that I know of. She tells us both she's fine and walks back into the fever rows by midday."

"There weren't enough hands," Mari said, not arguing, exactly, just stating the thing she'd apparently decided was simply true and not up for discussion. "There still aren't. You know there aren't, Petra, you were there same as me."

"I was there for eleven hours a day, same as you, and I went home at the end of them," Petra said. "You didn't. Not once in three weeks that I know of."

Mari didn't answer that either.

Wren stood there with her sister's too-warm hand in hers and did the long, silent arithmetic she'd spent her whole life being bad at out loud and competent at in private, the kind of arithmetic that had nothing to do with theory or resonance signatures and everything to do with the specific, infuriating shape of her sister's particular brand of goodness — the goodness that had made Mari beloved across an entire ward and had also, apparently, made her the kind of person who would run a Bleed for three weeks without telling anyone how bad it had actually gotten, because telling people would have meant stopping, and stopping, to Mari, had never once in her life looked like rest. It had only ever looked like failure.

"She'll rest now," Hesper said, in the tone Wren remembered from childhood scraped knees and one particularly bad fever of her own, a tone that did not invite argument from anyone in the room, daughter or healer or, implicitly, the Court itself. "I'll see to that personally. Petra, what does she need that she isn't getting?"

"Quiet," Petra said. "Real quiet, not just no patients — the channel out front's silted half to nothing, the whole ward's running thick, and she's not going to discharge properly sitting in a room that's already saturated. If you can get her somewhere with clean running water and actual distance from anyone else's grief for a week, that alone would do more than anything I can put in a cup."

"We have the house upriver," Hesper said. "Father's old place. Clean water, no neighbors fool enough to bring her their troubles."

"Good," Petra said, and then, flatly, without any windup at all: "One more thing. A Bleed this deep, held this long without discharge — I've seen it go past recovery in healers exactly this good at convincing everyone around them they're fine. Including themselves. I'd rather scare you now and be wrong than be polite about it and be right too late."

Nobody in the room said anything to that for a moment. Mari looked, for the first time since Wren had walked in, genuinely caught — not performing reassurance, not deflecting, just looking, for one unguarded second, exactly as frightened as Wren had been since the boy showed up soaked through at the workshop door.

"I'll rest," Mari said, finally, quietly, to the room rather than to anyone in it. "I will. I promise."

Wren believed her. That was the terrible part — she believed her completely, the same way she'd have believed her at eight years old promising to share the last sweet bun, because Mari had never once in her life made a promise she didn't fully intend to keep. The trouble had never been Mari's intentions. The trouble was that Mari had also never once, in twenty-six years, correctly identified the moment at which intending to be fine stopped being the same thing as actually being fine, and Wren did not have the faintest idea how you taught someone that, especially not someone who had spent her whole career being loved, specifically and constantly, for never needing to learn it.

Petra was already moving toward the door, calling for a junior attendant to fetch a stretcher-chair, and Hesper had gone to settle the bill and the arrangements with the kind of brisk, unhurried efficiency that meant nobody watching her would have guessed, unless they knew to look, that her hands were not quite steady either. Wren stayed where she was a moment longer, still holding her sister's hand, and made herself notice, on purpose, the specific shape of the fear she was actually feeling underneath the useful, organizing fear about Bleeds and discharge rates and how many days a body had before serious became something with a worse name. It was an old fear. It had nothing to do with theory. It had to do with the fact that she had spent her entire life arranging herself around the fixed, reliable star of her sister's goodness, and some part of her, the same sealed-off part that had stood in a doorway at eleven with an unwanted poultice, did not actually know who she was supposed to be if that star ever went out.

She put that away too. There would be time for it later, if later ever came, and right now there was a cart to find.

---

They got her upriver before dark, in a borrowed cart, Mari arguing the entire way that she could perfectly well walk, which nobody dignified with a response. Hesper rode up front with the driver, giving directions in the clipped, practical voice of a woman who had made this exact trip a hundred times in better circumstances and was not going to let the circumstances change how she gave directions now. Wren sat in the back with her sister, watching Lowmarket give way to the wider, cleaner streets of the upper wards, watching the pressure in the air thin out by degrees the farther they got from the silted channel, until somewhere past the third bridge it lifted entirely and the ordinary summer evening reasserted itself — birdsong, the smell of someone's supper, a child laughing at something two streets over — as if none of the last three hours had happened at all, which was its own small, specific cruelty, that a city could be in crisis on one side of a bridge and perfectly pleasant on the other and both of those things could be equally true at the same time.

Mari's head eventually, despite everything, came to rest against Wren's shoulder, and Wren found that she did not have a single useful thing to say, not one — no theory, no ledger, no careful eleven-step process for handling something old and dangerous and only technically still alive, because her sister was none of those things, her sister was just exhausted and frightened and pretending not to be, and the only tool Wren had ever built for situations like this was the same tool she used on Bartholomew: go quiet, go careful, want nothing, give the thing in front of her nothing to lean on.

It worked, in its way. Mari's breathing evened out. She slept, or came close to it, for most of the ride, and somewhere around the fifth mile Wren caught herself doing the thing she always did when she ran out of useful problems to solve, which was counting — the number of bridges, the number of lamps lit along the road, the number of stars she could pick out past the cart's canvas awning before clouds took the rest — not because the numbers meant anything, but because numbers, unlike feelings, stayed exactly where you put them.

It was not, Wren understood, lying awake herself in the dark of the cart with her sister's weight against her shoulder and the river running loud and clean somewhere off to the left, actually the right tool for this job. It was just the only one she had ever bothered to build.
