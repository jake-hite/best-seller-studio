# Chapter Eighteen — The Dependency

She spent the next day not deciding, exactly, but doing the only thing she had ever known how to do with a question too large to hold all at once: she took it apart into smaller questions and tested each one on her own bench, the way she'd tested the buffer theory before she'd ever trusted it on a person.

The question that wouldn't sit still, that kept surfacing no matter how many times she set it back down, was the one from the kitchen table two nights ago, the one she hadn't let herself finish out loud. *If Severance only quiets the obligation and leaves the feeling itself fully intact, the way Vesper swears it does — then why, in that room yesterday, with my sister going under for real, did Vesper need anything from me at all?*

She went back, methodically, to every word she could remember of what had actually happened in that hour — not the fear of it, which she set aside on purpose, but the technical shape of it, the way she'd have logged a resonance reading rather than a memory. Vesper had not reached for Mari with her own feeling alone. She had asked, specifically, for Wren's seeded scale, for a channel opened *toward herself*, before she could reach for anything past the flood. And what had come through that channel, when Wren held it, had not been thin or quiet or carefully managed the way twenty-six years of practiced control should have left it. It had been enormous. Old. Heavy with exactly the kind of charge Wren's own theory said a feeling needed to function as fuel for opening toward someone else — not just felt, but *obligated*, weighted, the specific density a feeling only carried when some part of the person feeling it still believed it owed something to someone.

She pulled out her grandmother's full journal, the one Hesper had given her, and read, for the first time with this exact question in mind, the old woman's careful notes on saturation and discharge — and found, underlined twice in faded ink, a line she'd skimmed past a dozen times without understanding why it mattered: *A feeling with nowhere left to go does not vanish. It goes quiet, and quiet is not the same as spent.* Her grandmother had been writing about wyrms, about why an old saturated creature's accumulated residue could be drawn on as a buffer precisely because none of it had ever been allowed to simply discharge and disappear — it sat, dense and available, exactly because nothing had been permitted to resolve it.

Severance, Wren understood, sitting very still at her own bench with the pieces finally assembling themselves into a shape she didn't want to see, was not actually a discharge at all. It quieted the obligation, the way Vesper said. But a feeling stripped of its obligation didn't simply remain available the way a wyrm's old residue did — it lost the specific charge that made it *usable* as fuel for reaching toward another person, the same way dye without a mordant could be fully present in a vat and still refuse, stubbornly, to bind to anything. Vesper could feel everything, completely, exactly as she'd claimed. She could no longer turn any of it into the thing a rescue like yesterday's actually required, because the part of her that would have minted that feeling into something spendable had been the very thing she'd traded away.

Which meant every real rescue Vesper had performed in twenty-six years — not the gentle ongoing guidance, not the conversations, but the actual, full-stakes pulling-back-from-the-edge work, the kind she'd done for Mari — had required borrowing exactly the kind of charge she could no longer generate herself.

Wren sat with that for a long time before she let herself follow it the rest of the way to its uncomfortable end. *Four young practitioners,* Iris had said. *Came to her exactly the way you did. Ended up Severed by her own hand.* If Severance left Vesper permanently unable to fuel the highest-stakes work alone, then every one of those four had not simply been mercy extended freely. Each of them, before whatever came after, had first been the open, obligated, still-feeling conduit some earlier crisis required — the borrowed charge that let Vesper do the actual saving — and only afterward, grateful and devoted and certain they owed her everything, had been offered the same "mercy" Wren was being offered now. A mercy that would, if accepted, end exactly where it had ended for at least one of the four: a person who could no longer ask whether the arrangement had ever been fair to her, because asking required the very obligation-link Severance had already taken away.

She went to the lock-house that evening without sending word first, and found Vesper in the garden behind it, tending the same careful lavender that scented the whole house, and said the thing plainly, the way she'd promised herself she would say everything from now on.

"You needed me yesterday," Wren said. "Not my hands. Not my theory. You needed my feeling — obligated, charged, exactly the kind your own Severance doesn't let you make anymore. That's not a guess. I worked the mechanism out on my own bench, the same way I worked out the buffer, and I don't think I'm wrong, and I think some part of you has known I'd eventually get here, because you're too precise a thinker not to have already worked it out about yourself a long time ago."

Vesper didn't deny it. She set down the trowel in her hand, slowly, and for a long moment said nothing at all, which Wren had learned by now to read as its own kind of answer.

"You're not wrong," she said finally. "I want to be exact with you, the way I've been exact about everything else, even now, even with this. Severance quiets the obligation. It does not leave the feeling spendable the way it was before. I have known that since roughly the second year, when I first tried to do for someone else what the hermit who saved me had done for me, and found I simply couldn't — not because I didn't feel enough, but because what I felt no longer had anywhere to go. I have spent twenty-four years since finding people willing to lend me what I'd traded away, and calling what I did for them afterward gratitude, when I think, if I am finally as honest with you as you've just been with me, it was also need."

"The four," Wren said. "Iris told me about them. You offered them exactly what you're offering me. After."

"After they'd already given me what I needed, yes." Something in Vesper's composure cracked, finally, fully, for the first time since Wren had met her — not into anger, but into something rawer and far more frightening, the sound of a person finally hearing her own pattern said aloud by someone she hadn't managed to talk out of seeing it. "I have told myself, every single time, that the offer afterward was its own separate kindness — unconnected to what came before it, freely given, no debt implied. I am not certain, standing here right now with you looking at me the way you're looking at me, that I have ever fully believed my own story about that. I think I have been, for over two decades, exactly the kind of person I built my whole second life to never again be ruled by — except instead of the Court ruling me through obligation, I have been ruling myself through a need I severed away the words for ever questioning."

"You're asking me to be the fifth," Wren said. "Whether you've let yourself call it that or not."

"I am asking you," Vesper said, "to let me sever you, sincerely believing it will protect you from a fear I know intimately and would not wish on anyone I cared for. And I am also, underneath that belief, asking it because some part of me that I have spent a very long time refusing to examine wants a partner who will never again have the standing to ask whether what I'm doing to her is fair. I cannot promise you those two things were ever fully separate in my own chest. I am not certain, after this conversation, that they ever were."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere past the lock-house wall, the controlled water sat in its dammed pool, patient, going exactly as fast as it was permitted to go, and Wren understood, looking at it, that she had finally found the actual shape of the thing she'd been circling since the first afternoon she'd stood on this towpath.

"I'm not going to let you sever me," she said. "Not tonight, not after Mari's safe, not ever, and I don't think that's a door I need to leave open the way I told Mari I'd leave hers open, because I don't think this is a choice with an honest version of staying open about it the way that was. You're not offering me a door. You're offering me a version of safety that requires me to stop being able to ask if it's actually safe."

"I understand," Vesper said, quietly, and there was no manipulation left in her voice at all, only something tired and old and, for the first time, entirely without armor.

"But I'm not leaving either," Wren said, "and I'm not leaving you to do this alone again, the way you've apparently been doing it alone in every way that actually matters for twenty-six years. My sister needs more than the buffer's bought her. You need an open partner to do the kind of saving that buffer can't do by itself. I can be that — once, fully, on purpose, with my eyes open about every part of what it might cost me, because I choose it, not because you talked me into needing to choose it. Not severed. Not grateful into silence afterward. A partner, on my own terms, for exactly as long as I decide it's still my own terms to set." She held Vesper's gaze, steady, the way Vesper had held hers in the kitchen two nights ago. "That's the offer I'm making you instead. I don't know if it's one you've ever actually been given before. I suspect, from everything you've just told me, that it isn't."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face — old grief, the same enormous, heavy, charged thing Wren had felt pass through the seeded scale the day before — finally, visibly, came undefended to the surface.

"No," she said. "It is not. I don't know what to do with being offered that honestly, by someone who has every reason and every right to refuse me entirely instead." She drew a slow breath, and when she spoke again, her voice had steadied, though something in it had changed permanently, Wren thought, in a way that would not go quietly back into its old composed shape. "Then let's go save your sister properly. On your terms. I think, after tonight, I owe you the honesty of admitting I no longer entirely know what mine are."
