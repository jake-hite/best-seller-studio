# Chapter Eighteen — The Dependency

She spent the next day not deciding, exactly, but doing the only thing she had ever known how to do with a question too large to hold all at once: she took it apart into smaller questions and tested each one on her own bench, the way she'd tested the buffer theory before she'd ever trusted it on a person.

She set up the bench the way she always set it up when a problem needed to feel smaller than it was — ledger open to a clean page, the day's date entered in the corner in her grandmother's careful upright hand before she'd let herself write a single line of actual thought, the seeded scale cooling in its cradle within reach but not yet touched, because touching it before she'd finished thinking would have meant letting the scale do the thinking for her, and this particular question, she suspected, would punish her badly for that kind of shortcut. The dye-shed at that hour smelled of cold mordant and last week's indigo, the specific bitter-green smell she'd long since stopped noticing except on days like this one, when her body apparently needed something ordinary to hold onto while the rest of her did something it had never done before.

The question that wouldn't sit still, that kept surfacing no matter how many times she set it back down, was the one from the kitchen table two nights ago, the one she hadn't let herself finish out loud. *If Severance only quiets the obligation and leaves the feeling itself fully intact, the way Vesper swears it does — then why, in that room yesterday, with my sister going under for real, did Vesper need anything from me at all?*

She wrote the question down, finally, in the ledger, in the same hand she used for resonance readings, because writing a thing down in that hand had always been her way of promising herself she would not flinch away from whatever answer it produced.

It was, she thought somewhere in the second hour, a deeply ridiculous way to find out whether someone she was starting to love had spent two weeks very gently grooming her for exactly this offer — cataloguing it like a resonance reading, breaking a relationship down into discrete measurable claims the way she'd once broken down a mordant ratio — and also the only method she actually trusted herself to use without simply believing whatever felt best to believe by lamplight. She thought, briefly, of how this would sound said aloud to anyone outside this shed: *I am attempting to determine whether I am being manipulated by running it through the same procedure I would use to verify a dye lot.* It sounded absurd. It was also, she decided, turning the thought over once more before discarding the absurdity as irrelevant, the most honest tool she owned, and she would rather use an absurd honest tool than a dignified dishonest one. So she kept going.

She made herself start with the part she least wanted to look at directly: her own wanting. Not Vesper's offer, not the mechanism, but the plain, undignified fact that some part of her had spent the better part of last night hoping the bench would clear Vesper entirely, hand her back a clean answer with no further question attached, so that she could simply say yes to the easiest thing she had ever been offered and stop being frightened. She wrote that down too, in smaller letters, off to the side, where it wouldn't interfere with the actual working — *I want this to come back clean* — because she had learned, the hard way, with humidity readings and with her own grief both, that an unacknowledged want was the single likeliest thing to quietly bend a measurement toward whatever answer it preferred.

She went back, methodically, to every word she could remember of what had actually happened in that hour — not the fear of it, which she set aside on purpose, but the technical shape of it, the way she'd have logged a resonance reading rather than a memory. She made herself walk through it twice, the second time slower than the first, the way she'd re-check a measurement she didn't trust the first time she'd taken it. Vesper had not reached for Mari with her own feeling alone. She had asked, specifically, for Wren's seeded scale, for a channel opened *toward herself*, before she could reach for anything past the flood. That detail had seemed, at the time, like a technical necessity, one more unfamiliar instruction in a room full of them, and Wren had not questioned it because there had been no room in that hour to question anything. She questioned it now, turning it the way she'd turn a vessel to check a seam from every angle the light allowed.

And what had come through that channel, when Wren held it, had not been thin or quiet or carefully managed the way twenty-six years of practiced control should have left it. It had been enormous. Old. Heavy with exactly the kind of charge Wren's own theory said a feeling needed to function as fuel for opening toward someone else — not just felt, but *obligated*, weighted, the specific density a feeling only carried when some part of the person feeling it still believed it owed something to someone. She tried, for the better part of twenty minutes, to find an alternative explanation that didn't require that conclusion — some other reason a Severed woman's grief might still register that dense on contact, some property of the scale itself she'd misjudged, some quirk of the channel she'd opened too quickly to read accurately. She came up with nothing that held weight against the plain memory of how it had actually felt in her own hands.

She pulled out her grandmother's full journal, the one Hesper had given her, and turned the pages slowly, the spine soft with handling long before it had ever reached Wren's shelf, and read, for the first time with this exact question in mind, the old woman's careful notes on saturation and discharge. Most of it she had read before, more than once, in the idle, hungry way she read anything with her grandmother's hand in it. This time she read it like evidence rather than company, and found, underlined twice in faded ink, a line she'd skimmed past a dozen times without understanding why it mattered: *A feeling with nowhere left to go does not vanish. It goes quiet, and quiet is not the same as spent.* She read it three times before she let herself move on, the third time aloud, in the empty dye-shed, just to hear whether it sounded as certain spoken as it did on the page. It did. Her grandmother had been writing about wyrms, about why an old saturated creature's accumulated residue could be drawn on as a buffer precisely because none of it had ever been allowed to simply discharge and disappear — it sat, dense and available, exactly because nothing had been permitted to resolve it.

Severance, Wren understood, sitting very still at her own bench with the pieces finally assembling themselves into a shape she didn't want to see, was not actually a discharge at all. It quieted the obligation, the way Vesper said. But a feeling stripped of its obligation didn't simply remain available the way a wyrm's old residue did — it lost the specific charge that made it *usable* as fuel for reaching toward another person, the same way dye without a mordant could be fully present in a vat and still refuse, stubbornly, to bind to anything. She sat with that comparison longer than she meant to, turning it the way she'd have turned an actual swatch in actual light, because it was the cleanest analogy she'd ever built on the spot and she distrusted clean analogies almost as much as she distrusted clean confessions, on the grounds that real mechanisms were rarely so considerate as to resemble something she already understood. She tested it twice more against the journal's other entries on residue and found it held both times, which did not make her feel better. Vesper could feel everything, completely, exactly as she'd claimed. She could no longer turn any of it into the thing a rescue like yesterday's actually required, because the part of her that would have minted that feeling into something spendable had been the very thing she'd traded away.

Which meant every real rescue Vesper had performed in twenty-six years — not the gentle ongoing guidance, not the conversations, but the actual, full-stakes pulling-back-from-the-edge work, the kind she'd done for Mari — had required borrowing exactly the kind of charge she could no longer generate herself.

Wren sat with that for a long time before she let herself follow it the rest of the way to its uncomfortable end. *Four young practitioners,* Iris had said. *Came to her exactly the way you did. Ended up Severed by her own hand.* If Severance left Vesper permanently unable to fuel the highest-stakes work alone, then every one of those four had not simply been mercy extended freely. Each of them, before whatever came after, had first been the open, obligated, still-feeling conduit some earlier crisis required — the borrowed charge that let Vesper do the actual saving — and only afterward, grateful and devoted and certain they owed her everything, had been offered the same "mercy" Wren was being offered now. A mercy that would, if accepted, end exactly where it had ended for at least one of the four: a person who could no longer ask whether the arrangement had ever been fair to her, because asking required the very obligation-link Severance had already taken away.

She thought of the one who wouldn't speak to anyone from the reform faction at all — *which we've chosen to read as her right rather than a verdict*, Iris had said, careful even then, careful in the specific way people were careful around a wound they suspected went deeper than anyone was willing to measure out loud. She thought of the one who had asked Iris, very quietly, whether there was any way to undo it, and of Iris having to say there wasn't, and of Iris saying she thought about that conversation more than she'd like to admit. Wren had heard all of that two days ago and filed it, at the time, as context — useful, troubling, but abstract, a thing that had happened to other people in another part of the story she wasn't yet inside of. It did not feel abstract anymore. It felt like standing in a room where the floor had just turned out to be a held breath the whole time, waiting to see whether she'd notice before she put her full weight down.

She closed the ledger, finally, somewhere past the dye-shed's usual closing hour, the day gone from gold to grey outside the high window without her once noticing the light change, and found she was no longer hungry, no longer cold, no longer aware of much beyond the single, clean, terrible clarity of having an answer she would have given a great deal not to have.

She went to the lock-house that evening without sending word first, and found Vesper in the garden behind it, tending the same careful lavender that scented the whole house, and said the thing plainly, the way she'd promised herself she would say everything from now on.

The garden, at that hour, was doing the thing it always did in late light — the lavender gone purple-grey rather than purple, bees slowing into the lazy, end-of-day circuits that meant the hive was nearly ready to close up for the night, the whole small plot smelling of crushed stem where Vesper's knees had been pressed into the bed for however long she'd been kneeling there. She didn't look up immediately when Wren came through the gate, which was, Wren registered distantly, itself unusual — Vesper always looked up, always met whoever had arrived with the same composed, immediate attention, as though arriving were a kind of announcement that deserved an answer.

"You needed me yesterday," Wren said. "Not my hands. Not my theory. You needed my feeling — obligated, charged, exactly the kind your own Severance doesn't let you make anymore. That's not a guess. I worked the mechanism out on my own bench, the same way I worked out the buffer, and I don't think I'm wrong, and I think some part of you has known I'd eventually get here, because you're too precise a thinker not to have already worked it out about yourself a long time ago."

The silence that followed lasted long enough that Wren heard, with unwelcome clarity, the specific small sounds of a garden deciding to ignore a human crisis happening inside it — a bee's low complaint somewhere near the lavender, the lock-house wall's old stone still giving back the day's banked heat, a bird two gardens over working through the same four notes it had apparently decided were worth repeating indefinitely.

Vesper didn't deny it. She set down the trowel in her hand, and Wren saw it before she had a name for what she was seeing — a small, involuntary tremor in the fingers that had set it down, there and gone in under a second, the first time in four weeks of watching those exact hands that they had done anything but hold perfectly, professionally still. It was such a small thing. It was also, Wren understood with a cold, vertiginous lurch, the missing proof she'd quietly clocked the absence of on a towpath a month ago and never once managed to stop thinking about since — the thing she'd have written down, if she'd been logging Vesper the way she logged a wyrm scale, as the single anomalous reading in an otherwise unbroken column of perfect numbers. Vesper said nothing at all for a long moment, which Wren had learned by now to read as its own kind of answer, and in the silence Wren made herself stand still and let it stretch, rather than filling it the way four years of discomfort with other people's quiet would once have made her do.

"You're not wrong," she said finally. "I want to be exact with you, the way I've been exact about everything else, even now, even with this." She straightened, slowly, brushing the dirt from her knees with the same precision she brought to everything, a small ordinary gesture that looked, in the moment, like the last scrap of composure she had left to spend on anything. "Severance quiets the obligation. It does not leave the feeling spendable the way it was before. I have known that since roughly the second year, when I first tried to do for someone else what the hermit who saved me had done for me, and found I simply couldn't — not because I didn't feel enough, but because what I felt no longer had anywhere to go."

She was quiet for a moment, and when she continued, her voice had dropped into something lower, more careful, the voice of a woman picking her way across a floor she no longer fully trusted. "I remember exactly where I was standing when I understood it. A bridge, not unlike the one we walked together a fortnight ago, except I was alone on it, and the person I'd failed to properly help that week was someone I had genuinely loved, and I stood there for the better part of an hour trying to locate the grief I knew, intellectually, that I ought to be drowning in, and finding instead a kind of clean, contained sorrow that behaved itself, that let me go home and sleep, that did not cost me anything at all. I told myself, that night, that this was simply what healing looked like. I have spent twenty-four years since finding people willing to lend me what I'd traded away, and calling what I did for them afterward gratitude, when I think, if I am finally as honest with you as you've just been with me, it was also need."

"The four," Wren said. "Iris told me about them. You offered them exactly what you're offering me. After."

"After," Vesper repeated, the word landing strangely in her own mouth, as though she were hearing it spoken back for the first time and did not entirely recognize its shape.

"After they'd already given me what I needed, yes." Something in Vesper's composure cracked, finally, fully, for the first time since Wren had met her — not into anger, but into something rawer and far more frightening, the sound of a person finally hearing her own pattern said aloud by someone she hadn't managed to talk out of seeing it. "I have told myself, every single time, that the offer afterward was its own separate kindness — unconnected to what came before it, freely given, no debt implied. I am not certain, standing here right now with you looking at me the way you're looking at me, that I have ever fully believed my own story about that. I think I have been, for over two decades, exactly the kind of person I built my whole second life to never again be ruled by — except instead of the Court ruling me through obligation, I have been ruling myself through a need I severed away the words for ever questioning."

"You're asking me to be the fifth," Wren said. "Whether you've let yourself call it that or not."

Vesper flinched at that — an actual flinch, small and immediate, the kind of involuntary recoil Wren had spent four years training out of her own body and had never once, until this exact second, seen on Vesper's.

"I am asking you," Vesper said, "to let me sever you, sincerely believing it will protect you from a fear I know intimately and would not wish on anyone I cared for. And I am also, underneath that belief, asking it because some part of me that I have spent a very long time refusing to examine wants a partner who will never again have the standing to ask whether what I'm doing to her is fair. I cannot promise you those two things were ever fully separate in my own chest. I am not certain, after this conversation, that they ever were."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere past the lock-house wall, the controlled water sat in its dammed pool, patient, going exactly as fast as it was permitted to go, and Wren understood, looking at it, that she had finally found the actual shape of the thing she'd been circling since the first afternoon she'd stood on this towpath — not a current at all, not really, whatever the towpath's water made it look like from outside. A held thing. A managed thing. Exactly as patient and exactly as controlled as the woman standing in front of her, and exactly as capable, given the right pressure in the right place, of breaking entirely instead of merely flowing differently.

She thought, with the strange, sideways clarity that sometimes arrived at the worst possible moments, of her own ledger — of the small private satisfaction of a column that balanced, of a reading that landed exactly where she'd predicted. There was no version of this column that balanced. There was only the choice of which imbalance she could live inside.

"I'm not going to let you sever me," she said. "Not tonight, not after Mari's safe, not ever, and I don't think that's a door I need to leave open the way I told Mari I'd leave hers open, because I don't think this is a choice with an honest version of staying open about it the way that was. You're not offering me a door. You're offering me a version of safety that requires me to stop being able to ask if it's actually safe."

"I understand," Vesper said, quietly, and there was no manipulation left in her voice at all, only something tired and old and, for the first time, entirely without armor.

A long beat passed in which neither of them moved. Somewhere behind Vesper, a late bee finally gave up its circuit of the lavender and went home, and the small ordinary fact of it — a bee, going home, indifferent to either of them — steadied Wren more than she would have expected anything that small to manage.

"But I'm not leaving either," Wren said, "and I'm not leaving you to do this alone again, the way you've apparently been doing it alone in every way that actually matters for twenty-six years. My sister needs more than the buffer's bought her. You need an open partner to do the kind of saving that buffer can't do by itself. I can be that — once, fully, on purpose, with my eyes open about every part of what it might cost me, because I choose it, not because you talked me into needing to choose it. Not severed. Not grateful into silence afterward. A partner, on my own terms, for exactly as long as I decide it's still my own terms to set." She held Vesper's gaze, steady, the way Vesper had held hers in the kitchen two nights ago. "That's the offer I'm making you instead. I don't know if it's one you've ever actually been given before. I suspect, from everything you've just told me, that it isn't."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face — old grief, the same enormous, heavy, charged thing Wren had felt pass through the seeded scale the day before — finally, visibly, came undefended to the surface. Her hands, Wren noticed, had not stopped their small tremor since the trowel; if anything it had worsened, just slightly, a fine unsteadiness she no longer seemed to be trying to hide.

"No," she said. "It is not. I don't know what to do with being offered that honestly, by someone who has every reason and every right to refuse me entirely instead." She drew a slow breath, and when she spoke again, her voice had steadied, though something in it had changed permanently, Wren thought, in a way that would not go quietly back into its old composed shape. "Then let's go save your sister properly. On your terms. I think, after tonight, I owe you the honesty of admitting I no longer entirely know what mine are."

They stood another moment in the failing light, the lavender's smell thick around them, the garden gone the particular blue-grey of evening fully arrived, and Wren found she had no further words ready, which was, she thought, perhaps the most honest place either of them could have landed — not a clean ending to the conversation, just the place it happened to stop, the way a measurement stopped when the instrument had nothing further to tell you that night.

"Tomorrow," Vesper said finally. "I'd like a night to be afraid of this properly, the way I suspect you've already been afraid of it since the kitchen. I don't think either of us does Mari any good walking into that room tonight, on legs neither of us currently trusts."

"Tomorrow," Wren agreed, and found, walking back along the darkening towpath toward the upriver house, that she was not afraid in quite the shape she'd expected to be — not of Vesper, not anymore, not exactly. Afraid, instead, of the size of the thing she'd just agreed to become, and of how little practice either of them actually had at the specific, narrow, unprecedented shape of partnership she'd just invented out of nothing more than refusal and stubbornness and four weeks of careful watching. It was not a comfortable kind of afraid. It was, she thought, turning the day over one final time before she let herself stop, at least an honest one.
