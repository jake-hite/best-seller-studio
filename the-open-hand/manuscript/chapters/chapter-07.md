# Chapter Seven — A Theory With No Guild Behind It

She set up the work in the old dye-shed behind the upriver house, mostly because it had a stone floor, good ventilation, and enough distance from the main house that nobody would hear her swearing at increasingly small pieces of equipment over the course of what turned into four straight days. Her mother hadn't worked the shed in years — Hesper had sold the last of the standing vats to a cousin in Brindle the winter after their father died, keeping only the building itself, half out of sentiment and half, Wren suspected, because nobody had gotten around to deciding what else it was for — but it still smelled the way it had her whole childhood, madder-root and lye and something mineral underneath both, a smell she associated so completely with the idea of careful, patient work that she'd half expected, walking in, to feel something settle in her simply from the air.

It did, a little, and then it didn't, because the first thing she actually had to do once she'd carried in her own bench-tools and her grandmother's journal and the satchel Corvin had given her was clean four years of accumulated dust off a workbench nobody had used since before she could legally hold an apprentice's license, and cleaning, it turned out, was not the same as settling. She found a dead wren's nest tucked into the rafters, abandoned probably a season ago, and left it where it was out of a kind of superstitious courtesy she didn't examine too closely. She found, in the bottom drawer of the old cutting table, a child's chalk drawing on a scrap of paper gone brown and brittle at the edges, unmistakably her own hand at perhaps six years old, a lopsided shape that might have been a wyrm or might have been a very ambitious dog, and she sat looking at it for longer than she'd planned to before she set it carefully on the windowsill, out of the way, and made herself get to work before the morning got away from her entirely.

Her hands ached by the second hour, a dull, specific ache along both forearms that she recognized after a moment as the particular cost of the six-mile walk catching up with her a full day late, the way a body sometimes saved its complaints for whenever the mind finally stopped being useful enough to ignore them. She ate standing, when she remembered to eat at all — bread her mother kept sending out with whichever child happened to be passing the dye-shed on an errand, a hard apple she ate down to a core so thin it was barely worth calling a core, water from the rain barrel by the door that tasted faintly of the tin cup she drank it from — and slept, the first night, for perhaps three hours on a folded blanket on the stone floor rather than walk back to the house and lose the half hour it would have cost her, a decision she suspected her mother would have had very specific opinions about, if her mother had known, which Wren took some care to ensure she did not, at least not until it was too late to be talked out of.

What she found, instead, in the first hour, were the dye-beetles.

Nobody had explained to her, as a child, why the dye-shed always had a few of them — small, glossy, faintly iridescent things, no bigger than a thumbnail, that fed on whatever pigment residue collected in the cracks of an old vat-house and went, over a season, the exact rust-and-indigo color of whatever batch had been run there last. She'd asked her grandmother once, she remembered now, watching one work its way along a baseboard at perhaps eight years old, and her grandmother had said something about them eating what nobody else wanted and turning it into something worth looking at, which Wren had taken at the time for a nice thing grown-ups said to satisfy a child's question and had only much later, reading the journal properly, recognized as her grandmother's actual considered theory of how residue accumulation worked on a living creature small enough to study without specialized equipment. There was one working its slow, single-minded way along the edge of her workbench within the first ten minutes, the same blue-black as her mother's stained fingers, and Wren watched it for a full minute before she got back to work, charmed against her own better judgment. It had six legs and the specific unhurried gait of something that had never once in its life needed to be anywhere by a particular hour, and she found herself, absurdly, a little envious of it.

"You're not helping," she told it. "But you're also not actively making this worse, which by the standards of my last two weeks puts you well ahead of most things I've dealt with."

The beetle, displaying the same reliable conversational range as Bartholomew, said nothing back, and went on with its slow blue-black progress along the bench while Wren turned, finally, to the actual problem. By the second day there were three of them, by her count, though she suspected this undercounted the actual population by some margin, since they were small enough to vanish into a crack in the stone the instant she looked at them directly, the way Bartholomew never quite managed to do despite considerably more practice at being inanimate. She named none of them, on the grounds that naming a wyrm scale that outranked her in age by three centuries was eccentric in a forgivable, professional way, and naming a household pest was simply unhinged, a distinction she maintained with some effort over the following four days and ultimately, she would admit much later to nobody but herself, failed to maintain at all. By the third day the largest of the three had acquired, entirely without her permission, the working designation Magistrate in her own head, on the grounds that it conducted its slow progress along the bench with the specific unhurried self-importance of someone who believed the room owed it patience, and she caught herself, twice, addressing it by that name out loud before catching herself and pretending, to no one, that she hadn't.

The anonymous ledger entry, once she'd sat with it properly, turned out to be less a recipe than a single stubborn idea dressed up as one: that a saturated material — something old enough and dense enough with its own accumulated residue — could be made to function the way a riverbed functioned for a flooding current. Not a wall, which broke. Not an open channel, which simply let the flood through to wherever it was already heading. A bed. Something with enough of its own old weight that a flood passing over it lost some of its force to the friction, the drag, the sheer patient mass of everything already settled there, without the bed itself being swept away in the process.

The trouble, as far as Wren could tell from three different angles by the end of the first day, was that nobody who'd ever written about overextension had considered using a *Material*-opener as the hand on the bed. Every text she had, official and otherwise, assumed the buffer would be operated by another Person-opener — someone who could meet a flooding mind on its own terms and absorb some of the structure of what was happening. A Material-opener interposing a saturated object between herself and a drowning person was, as far as the historical record went, not untested because it was forbidden. It was untested because nobody had apparently ever thought to try it, the same way nobody had ever thought to ask whether a smith could do a healer's job with the right tool, because the entire shape of Aldermere's gifted economy had decided, three centuries back, which jobs belonged to which kind of opening, and nobody much questioned the borders after that. She said as much out loud to the empty shed at one point, mostly to hear how the argument sounded carried by an actual voice instead of just sitting flat on a page — "a Material-opener can hold three hundred years of accumulated residue in a single scale without flinching, and everyone agrees that's perfectly safe, but the idea of using that same hand to stand between a person and a flood is apparently where the whole profession draws its very serious, very considered line" — and found the argument sounded, if anything, more obviously correct out loud than it had in her own head, which was either a good sign or the first symptom of having spent too many consecutive hours alone with only insects for company.

She spent the better part of the first afternoon simply listing, in the back of her own ledger, every text she could remember that touched the subject at all, cross-referenced against what she actually had in front of her — three guild primers, her grandmother's six volumes, Corvin's overnight transcription, and a battered student copy of *Foundations of Resonant Transfer* she'd owned since her second year and had never once expected to need for anything beyond passing an examination. None of them, reading them again with the actual problem in front of her rather than an abstract one, so much as gestured at the possibility she was chasing. They discussed buffers exclusively as a Person-opener's tool, the way a recipe book might discuss knife technique without once considering that someone reading it might only have one hand. She found this, on the whole, more useful as evidence than as comfort — if nobody had written the failure modes down, that meant nobody had actually tried it and failed, which left the door, infuriatingly, all the way open and entirely unmapped.

Wren found, working alone in the dye-shed with her grandmother's journal propped open against a jar of dried madder root, that she was furious about this in a way that surprised her — not at any one person, not even really at the guild, but at the sheer unexamined laziness of a system that had simply never bothered to check whether its own categories were as solid as everyone assumed. The fury had a physical quality to it she hadn't expected, a tightness across her shoulders that no amount of stretching seemed to loosen, and she worked through two full afternoons with her jaw clenched tight enough that it ached afterward, a dull throb along the hinge of it she didn't connect to anything until she caught her own reflection, dimly, in the dark glass of the shed's single window, and recognized the expression staring back as one she'd never once seen on her own face before — something closer to her mother's face arguing with a Council notice than to anything she usually wore over a workbench. She wrote the anger down, too, in the margin of her own notes, because it seemed like the kind of observation that deserved a permanent home even if nobody but her ever read it: *Nobody checked the border because nobody who mattered had to live on the wrong side of it.*

"Nobody's tried this," she told the scale, on the second evening, because four days of solitary work had apparently eroded whatever remained of her dignity around talking to equipment. "Not because it doesn't work. Because nobody thought a person like me would be standing where I'm currently standing." The dye-beetle, three feet away on the bench, had not moved in several hours, which she chose to interpret as profound agreement rather than the much likelier explanation that it had simply died there sometime that afternoon. She didn't check. She wasn't ready, that particular night, to be wrong about one more thing.

The scale, predictably, said nothing back, which she had always found to be one of its more reliable qualities. She'd brought it down from its cradle and set it on a folded cloth in the lamplight, turning it once in her palm the way she always did before a session, cataloguing, out of long habit, the small irregularities along its edge she'd memorized years ago — a hairline chip near the base, a faint discoloration along one ridge that her grandmother's notes attributed to a poorly cured tanning batch sometime in the scale's first century of guild ownership, the particular cool, dense weight of it that never quite matched what her arm expected to be holding, no matter how many times she picked it up. It was, by any reasonable measure, the single object she knew most intimately in the world, and she was about to ask it to do something nobody had asked of a scale like it in at least a hundred years, possibly ever, and she found she wanted, however absurdly, to apologize to it first, the way you'd apologize to a horse before asking it to jump something it had never seen before.

She didn't apologize out loud. She'd already used up her quota of dignity for the evening on the conversation with the beetle.

The actual theory, once she stopped being angry long enough to build it properly, came together faster than she expected, which frightened her slightly, because the things that came together fast were rarely the things you'd checked enough. She would hold the scale — fully alive to its old, patient weight, the way she'd trained herself never to flinch from — and instead of keeping herself blank toward it, the way she did at the guild-house, she would do the opposite: open toward it on purpose, invite its accumulated saturation to rise and meet her own attention, and then, in the same motion, hold that opened channel steady while reaching, with the same hand in a sense, toward something else entirely. A second point of contact. A controlled flood, manufactured on purpose, routed through the scale instead of through herself. She drew it out first as a diagram before she ever attempted it as a technique, three separate versions over the course of an hour, crossing out the first two for reasons she could not, afterward, entirely reconstruct beyond a vague sense that the arrows hadn't been honest about where the danger actually lived.

She tested it first on a cracked clay vessel she'd deliberately overcharged with residue from the dye-shed's own waste runoff — a small, contained, entirely inanimate flood, exactly the kind of thing a Material-opener was supposed to be able to handle without anyone clutching their pearls about it. She prepared for it the way she'd been trained to prepare for anything involving the scale: eleven measurements first, humidity and ambient temperature and the scale's own resting resonance logged in her own hand before she let herself touch anything, a ritual that felt, that particular evening, almost unbearably slow, and that she made herself complete in full regardless, because the one lesson four years at Lantern Street had actually managed to drill all the way into her bones was that the measurements were never the part you were allowed to skip.

The first attempt failed instructively and immediately: the scale simply absorbed the contact and gave nothing back, like trying to make two separate fires share the same flame without either catching, and she sat afterward feeling faintly ridiculous, as though she'd knocked on a door that wasn't actually attached to a house. Her hands had gone cold despite the evening's warmth, a small, specific physical tell she logged without comment and filed under *interesting, revisit later* rather than *alarming.* The second attempt collapsed the instant she tried to hold both ends of the channel at once, a failure so total and so fast that she didn't even have time to be frightened by it before it was already over, just a sharp, unpleasant lurch like missing a step in the dark, the kind of full-body jolt that left her catching the edge of the workbench for balance and her own pulse hammering somewhere up near her throat for a full minute afterward. The third attempt was the one that actually scared her: she lost concentration at the wrong moment, for less than a second, and felt the dye-runoff's manufactured flood lean toward her own open hand instead of the scale's — a small, hungry tug, gone almost before she registered it, but unmistakably the exact failure mode every text she owned warned killed people, scaled up to an actual person instead of a jar of waste pigment.

She sat on the dye-shed floor afterward for a long time, shaking slightly, recalibrating, her own breath loud in her ears in the particular over-quiet that settled into a room once something dangerous had passed through it and decided, this time, to leave. Her shirt had gone damp at the back despite the cool stone floor under her, and she noticed, distantly, that her hands smelled faintly of the dye-runoff now, a sharp mineral tang she'd be finding under her fingernails for days, and that she was, despite everything, ravenously and inconveniently hungry, the specific bottomless hunger that always arrived in the wake of real fear, as though her body had decided that whatever had nearly happened required immediate refueling regardless of the hour. She ate the last of the bread standing right there on the floor, not bothering to get up first, and felt marginally more human for it. The dye-beetle resumed its glacial progress along the bench above her head as though nothing of any importance had happened at all, which from its perspective, she supposed, nothing had.

The fourth attempt worked.

It worked in the specific, electric way that the genuinely correct answer to a hard problem always felt different from a merely plausible one — not louder, not more dramatic, just suddenly, obviously *right*, the way a key turned in a lock you'd been jiggling for an hour. The manufactured flood from the cracked vessel hit the scale's old saturated weight and lost most of its force to it, the way a wave loses its force crossing a long shallow reef, and what reached Wren's own open hand on the other side was a fraction of what she'd sent out, easily, comfortably within what she could hold without flinching. She actually laughed, once, a short startled bark of a sound that embarrassed her even alone in an empty shed at that hour, because nothing in four days of careful technical language had prepared her for how much the sensation itself would feel like relief rather than triumph — like setting down a weight she hadn't fully registered carrying until her arms came up light.

She did it nine more times before she trusted it, and made herself log every one of the nine with the same flat, unglamorous thoroughness she'd have used logging a guild commission, because a result you only wrote down when it excited you was a result you'd eventually convince yourself of regardless of what it actually showed. She varied the intensity, varied the duration, twice deliberately trying to break it to find the edges of where it stopped working — overcharging the vessel further than any sensible test required, on the grounds that she would rather find the failure point herself, on a piece of pottery, at two in the morning, than have someone else's body find it for her later. Once she left careful notes for a full hour in between attempts because her hand had started shaking too badly to write legibly, sat on the cold floor chewing the end of her own pencil until the shaking settled, and refused, on principle, to record a single number she couldn't read back clearly later, no matter how badly she wanted to simply keep going. By the fourth day she had something that was no longer just an old, undated note folded into the back of a ledger. It was a working theory, written out now in her own hand in the front of her grandmother's journal, with real numbers attached, real ranges, a real account of exactly how much buffer the scale's particular density and age could provide before it would, by her own calculations, start to saturate past its own safe threshold and become a danger in its own right rather than a shield. She drew a small, careful diagram in the margin — the scale as a riverbed, the flood as an arrow losing height as it crossed — redrew it twice more when the first two versions struck her as too tidy to be trusted, and looked at the third, finally, for a long moment with the specific pride of someone who had just turned four sleepless days and three failures into a single picture simple enough that even Corvin, who distrusted diagrams on principle, would understand it in one glance.

It worked. That was the triumphant part, the part that had her grinning alone in a dye-shed at two in the morning over a piece of cracked pottery like she'd just been handed the single best gift of her professional life, because in a sense she had. She held the cracked vessel up to the lamplight, turned it once, and addressed the dye-beetle with the full, unembarrassed solemnity of someone who had not spoken to another human being in approximately fourteen hours.

"I would like it on the record," she told it, "that I have just done something nobody in three hundred years of guild history thought a person like me was allowed to try, using a dead king's spine and a broken pot, in a building my mother stopped using for actual dye work before I was old enough to remember it being used for dye work. I think that's worth noting. I don't think anyone is ever going to write it down except me, so I'm telling you, because you've been here the whole time and you've earned it."

It was also, she made herself sit with fully before she let herself sleep that night, the most dangerous thing she had ever built, because everything she had just proven held true for a clay vessel's manufactured, contained, entirely impersonal flood. She had no idea — none, not a single data point in four days of careful, joyful, increasingly reckless testing — what it would do interposed between herself and an actual person. A person who loved her back. A person whose flood wouldn't arrive as a clean, predictable surge but as everything Mari had ever felt and never said, all at once, with twenty-six years of practiced selflessness behind it making the flood smarter, more persistent, better at finding the soft places in anyone foolish enough to stand in its way.

She closed the journal, finally, somewhere near dawn, and sat for a long while in the grey early light with the scale cooling in her lap, feeling something she hadn't expected to feel at the end of the best four days of work she'd ever done: afraid, specifically and completely, of her own competence, for what was very possibly the first time in her life. Her body, now that there was nothing left urgent enough to override it, had begun submitting its accumulated complaints all at once — the blisters from the walk down, gone tight and shiny under four days of standing on them anyway, a headache she'd been too occupied to notice arriving and now couldn't ignore, the particular hollow, scraped-out feeling of having slept perhaps nine hours total across four nights and eaten roughly half of what her mother had sent out. She noted all of it the way she noted everything, clinically, for later, and did nothing whatsoever to address any of it yet, because there was one more thing she wanted to sit with first while the light was still grey and the shed was still quiet enough to hear herself think in.

The dye-beetle had gone, at some point in the night, somewhere she didn't see it leave to, and she found, looking at the empty patch of bench where it had spent four days keeping her unacknowledged company, that she missed it slightly, which struck her as a faintly ridiculous thing to feel on a morning that had otherwise rearranged the entire shape of what she understood herself to be capable of. She left the window propped open an inch on her way out, in case it or one of its unnamed, undercounted relatives wanted back in, and could not, afterward, have explained that decision to anyone who asked, least of all herself.
