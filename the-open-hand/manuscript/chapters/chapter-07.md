# Chapter Seven — A Theory With No Guild Behind It

She set up the work in the old dye-shed behind the upriver house, mostly because it had a stone floor, good ventilation, and enough distance from the main house that nobody would hear her swearing at increasingly small pieces of equipment over the course of what turned into four straight days. Her mother hadn't worked the shed in years — Hesper had sold the last of the standing vats to a cousin in Brindle the winter after their father died, keeping only the building itself, half out of sentiment and half, Wren suspected, because nobody had gotten around to deciding what else it was for — but it still smelled the way it had her whole childhood, madder-root and lye and something mineral underneath both, a smell she associated so completely with the idea of careful, patient work that she'd half expected, walking in, to feel something settle in her simply from the air.

What she found, instead, in the first hour, were the dye-beetles.

Nobody had explained to her, as a child, why the dye-shed always had a few of them — small, glossy, faintly iridescent things, no bigger than a thumbnail, that fed on whatever pigment residue collected in the cracks of an old vat-house and went, over a season, the exact rust-and-indigo color of whatever batch had been run there last. There was one working its slow, single-minded way along the edge of her workbench within the first ten minutes, the same blue-black as her mother's stained fingers, and Wren watched it for a full minute before she got back to work, charmed against her own better judgment.

"You're not helping," she told it. "But you're also not actively making this worse, which by the standards of my last two weeks puts you well ahead of most things I've dealt with."

The beetle, displaying the same reliable conversational range as Bartholomew, said nothing back, and went on with its slow blue-black progress along the bench while Wren turned, finally, to the actual problem.

The anonymous ledger entry, once she'd sat with it properly, turned out to be less a recipe than a single stubborn idea dressed up as one: that a saturated material — something old enough and dense enough with its own accumulated residue — could be made to function the way a riverbed functioned for a flooding current. Not a wall, which broke. Not an open channel, which simply let the flood through to wherever it was already heading. A bed. Something with enough of its own old weight that a flood passing over it lost some of its force to the friction, the drag, the sheer patient mass of everything already settled there, without the bed itself being swept away in the process.

The trouble, as far as Wren could tell from three different angles by the end of the first day, was that nobody who'd ever written about overextension had considered using a *Material*-opener as the hand on the bed. Every text she had, official and otherwise, assumed the buffer would be operated by another Person-opener — someone who could meet a flooding mind on its own terms and absorb some of the structure of what was happening. A Material-opener interposing a saturated object between herself and a drowning person was, as far as the historical record went, not untested because it was forbidden. It was untested because nobody had apparently ever thought to try it, the same way nobody had ever thought to ask whether a smith could do a healer's job with the right tool, because the entire shape of Aldermere's gifted economy had decided, three centuries back, which jobs belonged to which kind of opening, and nobody much questioned the borders after that.

Wren found, working alone in the dye-shed with her grandmother's journal propped open against a jar of dried madder root, that she was furious about this in a way that surprised her — not at any one person, not even really at the guild, but at the sheer unexamined laziness of a system that had simply never bothered to check whether its own categories were as solid as everyone assumed. She wrote that down, too, in the margin of her own notes, because it seemed like the kind of observation that deserved a permanent home even if nobody but her ever read it: *Nobody checked the border because nobody who mattered had to live on the wrong side of it.*

"Nobody's tried this," she told the scale, on the second evening, because four days of solitary work had apparently eroded whatever remained of her dignity around talking to equipment. "Not because it doesn't work. Because nobody thought a person like me would be standing where I'm currently standing." The dye-beetle, three feet away on the bench, had not moved in several hours, which she chose to interpret as profound agreement rather than the much likelier explanation that it had simply died there sometime that afternoon. She didn't check. She wasn't ready, that particular night, to be wrong about one more thing.

The scale, predictably, said nothing back, which she had always found to be one of its more reliable qualities.

The actual theory, once she stopped being angry long enough to build it properly, came together faster than she expected, which frightened her slightly, because the things that came together fast were rarely the things you'd checked enough. She would hold the scale — fully alive to its old, patient weight, the way she'd trained herself never to flinch from — and instead of keeping herself blank toward it, the way she did at the guild-house, she would do the opposite: open toward it on purpose, invite its accumulated saturation to rise and meet her own attention, and then, in the same motion, hold that opened channel steady while reaching, with the same hand in a sense, toward something else entirely. A second point of contact. A controlled flood, manufactured on purpose, routed through the scale instead of through herself.

She tested it first on a cracked clay vessel she'd deliberately overcharged with residue from the dye-shed's own waste runoff — a small, contained, entirely inanimate flood, exactly the kind of thing a Material-opener was supposed to be able to handle without anyone clutching their pearls about it. The first attempt failed instructively and immediately: the scale simply absorbed the contact and gave nothing back, like trying to make two separate fires share the same flame without either catching, and she sat afterward feeling faintly ridiculous, as though she'd knocked on a door that wasn't actually attached to a house. The second attempt collapsed the instant she tried to hold both ends of the channel at once, a failure so total and so fast that she didn't even have time to be frightened by it before it was already over, just a sharp, unpleasant lurch like missing a step in the dark. The third attempt was the one that actually scared her: she lost concentration at the wrong moment, for less than a second, and felt the dye-runoff's manufactured flood lean toward her own open hand instead of the scale's — a small, hungry tug, gone almost before she registered it, but unmistakably the exact failure mode every text she owned warned killed people, scaled up to an actual person instead of a jar of waste pigment.

She sat on the dye-shed floor afterward for a long time, shaking slightly, recalibrating, the dye-beetle resuming its glacial progress along the bench above her head as though nothing of any importance had happened at all, which from its perspective, she supposed, nothing had.

The fourth attempt worked.

It worked in the specific, electric way that the genuinely correct answer to a hard problem always felt different from a merely plausible one — not louder, not more dramatic, just suddenly, obviously *right*, the way a key turned in a lock you'd been jiggling for an hour. The manufactured flood from the cracked vessel hit the scale's old saturated weight and lost most of its force to it, the way a wave loses its force crossing a long shallow reef, and what reached Wren's own open hand on the other side was a fraction of what she'd sent out, easily, comfortably within what she could hold without flinching.

She did it nine more times before she trusted it, varying the intensity, varying the duration, twice deliberately trying to break it to find the edges of where it stopped working, once leaving careful notes for an hour in between attempts because her hand had started shaking too badly to write legibly and she refused, on principle, to record a single number she couldn't read back clearly later. By the fourth day she had something that was no longer just an old, undated note folded into the back of a ledger. It was a working theory, written out now in her own hand in the front of her grandmother's journal, with real numbers attached, real ranges, a real account of exactly how much buffer the scale's particular density and age could provide before it would, by her own calculations, start to saturate past its own safe threshold and become a danger in its own right rather than a shield. She drew a small, careful diagram in the margin — the scale as a riverbed, the flood as an arrow losing height as it crossed — and looked at it for a long moment with the specific pride of someone who had just turned four sleepless days and three failures into a single picture simple enough that even Corvin, who distrusted diagrams on principle, would understand it in one glance.

It worked. That was the triumphant part, the part that had her grinning alone in a dye-shed at two in the morning over a piece of cracked pottery like she'd just been handed the single best gift of her professional life, because in a sense she had. She held the cracked vessel up to the lamplight, turned it once, and addressed the dye-beetle with the full, unembarrassed solemnity of someone who had not spoken to another human being in approximately fourteen hours.

"I would like it on the record," she told it, "that I have just done something nobody in three hundred years of guild history thought a person like me was allowed to try, using a dead king's spine and a broken pot, in a building my mother stopped using for actual dye work before I was old enough to remember it being used for dye work. I think that's worth noting. I don't think anyone is ever going to write it down except me, so I'm telling you, because you've been here the whole time and you've earned it."

It was also, she made herself sit with fully before she let herself sleep that night, the most dangerous thing she had ever built, because everything she had just proven held true for a clay vessel's manufactured, contained, entirely impersonal flood. She had no idea — none, not a single data point in four days of careful, joyful, increasingly reckless testing — what it would do interposed between herself and an actual person. A person who loved her back. A person whose flood wouldn't arrive as a clean, predictable surge but as everything Mari had ever felt and never said, all at once, with twenty-six years of practiced selflessness behind it making the flood smarter, more persistent, better at finding the soft places in anyone foolish enough to stand in its way.

She closed the journal, finally, somewhere near dawn, and sat for a long while in the grey early light with the scale cooling in her lap, feeling something she hadn't expected to feel at the end of the best four days of work she'd ever done: afraid, specifically and completely, of her own competence, for what was very possibly the first time in her life. The dye-beetle had gone, at some point in the night, somewhere she didn't see it leave to, and she found, looking at the empty patch of bench where it had spent four days keeping her unacknowledged company, that she missed it slightly, which struck her as a faintly ridiculous thing to feel on a morning that had otherwise rearranged the entire shape of what she understood herself to be capable of.
