# Chapter Sixteen — Going Under

Vesper arrived faster than Wren had thought possible, still in the plain clothes she must have been wearing at home, her composure intact but her breathing not quite even, the first time Wren had ever seen her arrive anywhere looking like the journey had cost her something.

"Tell me the number," she said to the house healer, before she'd even fully crossed the threshold, and listened to his answer with a stillness that had gone, in the space of a sentence, entirely beyond her usual unhurried warmth into something flatter and more urgent.

"How long has she been like this."

"Forty minutes. Maybe less."

"That's close," Vesper said, mostly to herself, already moving to the bed, already taking Mari's slack hand in both of hers with a gentleness that did nothing to disguise the speed underneath it. "That's closer than I'd like. Wren. I need you exactly where I tell you to stand, and I need you to do precisely what I say, the instant I say it, with no improvising no matter how it looks from outside. Can you do that."

"Yes."

"Then come here, and bring the scale, and understand before I start that I am not going to explain what I'm doing while I'm doing it, because there will not be time, and I need every part of my attention on her rather than on making this comprehensible to you. I will explain afterward. I promise you that much."

Wren came, the great dark quarry scale heavy and cool in her hands, and stood where Vesper positioned her — close, at the head of the bed, close enough that she could feel, even without opening toward anything, the specific wrongness radiating off her sister like heat off a fevered stone.

What Vesper did next was nothing like Wren's own work, and nothing, either, like the clean clinical Person-opening Mari practiced on her best, most ordinary days. She opened toward Mari fully, without a flinch, without the careful incremental testing Wren had needed nine cracked vessels to earn the right to skip — and then, instead of absorbing what came back, she did something Wren had no proper word for yet and would spend a long time afterward trying to name precisely: she reached past the flood itself, past the formless structureless static that had swallowed every boundary Mari's mind used to hold itself together, toward whatever older, steadier thing in Mari was still, underneath all of it, recognizably Mari, and held onto that instead.

"She's not gone," Vesper said, low, urgent, more to herself or to Mari than to anyone in the room. "Going. Not gone. There's a difference and I have personally measured it from the wrong side once already in my life, I am not about to mismeasure it from this side." Her voice, for the first time since Wren had met her, had lost every trace of its careful unhurried warmth, replaced by something rawer, older, frightened in a way Wren would not have believed her capable of being twenty minutes ago. "Marigold. Mari. I need you to hear one true thing through whatever's drowning you right now, and I need you to hold onto it the way you'd hold onto a confirmed measurement, because that's the only kind of rope that's ever worked on someone like you or me — you are allowed to stop. Not forever. Not as a verdict on what you're worth. Just for today, just for this hour, you are allowed to stop holding everyone else up, because there are people in this room who are holding you instead, and they are not asking you to be useful to deserve it."

It should have sounded, Wren thought even through her own fear, like exactly the kind of thing a clever and dangerous woman would say to make a desperate family grateful. It also happened to be, as far as Wren could tell from inside the room, simply and entirely true, and she had stopped, somewhere in the last week, being able to hold both facts about Vesper Reed at a comfortable distance from each other.

"Now," Vesper said, sharp, to Wren. "The scale. Open toward it, seeded the way I taught you, and hold the channel — not toward her, toward me. I need somewhere old and patient to set down what I'm currently carrying so I don't have to carry all of it and reach for her at the same time. You are not the buffer between her and the flood right now. You are the buffer between me and the cost of doing this properly, and I would not ask if there were any other way, and there is not."

Wren did it without question, the seeded channel opening clean and ready the way it had two days ago, and felt, in the same instant, something arrive through it that startled her more than Mari's flood ever had: not chaos, not structureless static, but a controlled, deliberate, terribly heavy stream of feeling that had clearly been carried, alone, for a very long time, by someone who had told herself for twenty-six years that she had stopped needing anywhere to set it down. It was old grief, she understood distantly, turning it over even as she held the channel steady. Old grief and a very specific, very particular fear, the fear of a woman who had watched someone she loved go under once already and had never, not once in over two and a half decades, found a way to stop bracing for it happening again.

The scale drank it the way it had drunk every flood before this one, patient and old and unbothered, and Wren held the channel exactly as long as she was told to, watching Vesper's face the entire time — composed again, slowly, as the weight left her, but composed now in a way that looked, for the first time since Wren had met her, like genuine relief rather than practiced control.

It took the better part of an hour. Wren would not, afterward, be able to give a clean account of most of it — only fragments, the house healer's hands steady on Mari's pulse, Sela standing rigid by the door with her arms no longer folded at all, Vesper's low, unbroken voice finding its way, again and again, back to the same handful of true things and repeating them until they had somewhere to land. Slowly, in stages, the way a wound closed rather than a door slammed, Mari's breathing evened, her unseeing stare narrowed back into focus, and somewhere near the end of the hour her hand, slack in Vesper's for so long, closed weakly around it on its own.

"There," Vesper said, the old warmth back in her voice, exhausted now but real. "There you are."

Mari's eyes found Wren's face first, the way they always had, since they were children, the order never once reversing no matter how bad things got.

"I heard you," Mari said, hoarse, barely audible. "Both of you. I don't — I don't think I can explain what I heard, but I heard it."

"Don't explain it now," Vesper said, gently, releasing her hand at last. "Sleep. Properly, this time, the kind your sister's buffer never quite managed to give you, because it was never built to do what I just did and it did everything it could anyway. You're not fixed. I want to be honest about that the moment I can manage a full sentence, the same way your sister was honest with you the first time she tried this. You're back from where you were an hour ago. That's not nothing, and it's also not enough, not on its own, not for very much longer."

Mari was asleep again, this time genuinely, within minutes, and the house healer's hands finally came away from her pulse with something like real relief in his face for the first time since the runner had been sent.

Vesper sat heavily in the room's one chair, the kind of sitting that came from a body that had spent everything it had to spend, and Wren, watching her, felt the question she'd been holding all hour finally find its way out.

"What did I just do," Wren said. "For you. You said the scale was a buffer between you and the cost. I felt what came through. That wasn't — Vesper, that wasn't a small contained feeling like Bram and I practiced. That was years of something."

"It was," Vesper said, simply, not denying it, too tired in the moment, Wren suspected, to perform the careful composure she usually wore even this far past exhausted. "I'll explain it properly. Not tonight. Tonight I would like to sit in this chair for a while longer and be grateful that the number we just measured wasn't the final one, and I'd ask you to let me have that, just this once, before you ask me to account for the rest of it."

Wren let her have it, watching the still water of the channel beneath the floor grate run on, patient and constant, while her sister slept and the room's fear slowly, finally, began to recede — and filed the question carefully alongside Iris Hale's quiet, unconfronted warning, in the same patient corner of her mind, understanding without yet being able to say why that the two facts had just moved, for the first time, close enough together to nearly touch.
