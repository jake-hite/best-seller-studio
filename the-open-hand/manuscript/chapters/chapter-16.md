# Chapter Sixteen — Going Under

The wait, after Sela ran for the door, lasted either eleven minutes or several years, depending on which part of Wren was keeping the count. The small bright room, which had felt almost cheerful on better visits — clean linens, a window that actually let in light, the particular optimistic tidiness of a place built to make frightened people feel like patients rather than casualties — had taken on, in the space of twenty minutes, the precise hushed quality of a room everyone has silently agreed not to call by its true name yet. The house healer worked a low, steady patter beside the bed — pulse, pupil, the visible heat under the skin, the same three measurements taken again and again, less because he expected them to change in the next sixty seconds than because his hands needed something to do that wasn't simply standing still and watching a woman not wake up. Sela did not sit down once. She stood by the door with her arms crossed tight over her chest, eyes fixed on the corridor beyond it as though sheer attention could make the runner's return arrive faster, and twice Wren heard her mutter something under her breath that might have been a count of her own, or might have been a prayer, or might simply have been the particular wordless noise a person makes when waiting has stopped being bearable in silence. Wren did not sit either, the cooling scale still in her lap, watching her sister's open, unseeing eyes and promising herself, with the specific stubborn fierceness of someone who had nothing else left to offer, that she would not look away before help arrived — even though whether help arrived fast enough was, by every number the healer had already quoted her, no longer entirely a thing she got to control. She counted the healer's pulse-checks instead, because counting was at least something her mind knew how to do without her permission, and reached eleven before she lost the thread of it entirely and simply watched her sister breathe, in, out, in, out, the only arithmetic that mattered anymore reduced to that single repeating fact.

Vesper arrived faster than Wren had thought possible, still in the plain clothes she must have been wearing at home — no fine collar, no carefully arranged hair, nothing of the composed woman who received visitors at the lock-house — hair coming loose at one temple in a way Wren had never once seen, a thin sheen of exertion on a brow she'd only ever known composed, her breathing not quite even, one sleeve pushed up past the elbow as though she'd rolled it back mid-stride without breaking pace. It was the first time Wren had ever seen her arrive anywhere looking like the journey itself had cost her something, and some small, distant part of Wren's mind — the part that never quite stopped cataloguing, even now — noted that she had never once seen Vesper run, and that the woman apparently could, fast and without grace, like someone who had not expected to need the skill again after a great many years of not needing it.

"Tell me the number," she said to the house healer, before she'd even fully crossed the threshold, and listened to his answer with a stillness that had gone, in the space of a sentence, entirely beyond her usual unhurried warmth into something flatter and more urgent.

"How long has she been like this."

"Forty minutes. Maybe less."

"That's close," Vesper said, mostly to herself, already moving to the bed, already taking Mari's slack hand in both of hers with a gentleness that did nothing to disguise the speed underneath it. Her thumb pressed once, briefly, against the inside of Mari's wrist, not checking a pulse exactly — the house healer had that well in hand — but feeling for something else entirely, something Wren couldn't name and didn't ask about. "Closer than I'd like. Wren. I need you exactly where I tell you to stand, and I need you to do precisely what I say, the instant I say it, with no improvising no matter how it looks from outside. Can you do that."

"Yes."

"Then come here, and bring the scale, and understand before I start that I am not going to explain what I'm doing while I'm doing it, because there will not be time, and I need every part of my attention on her rather than on making this comprehensible to you. I will explain afterward. I promise you that much." She said it fast, none of her usual careful unhurried cadence in it at all, and Wren understood, distantly, that she was hearing something rarer than she'd realized: Vesper Reed with no time left over to be eloquent.

Wren came, the great dark quarry scale heavy and cool in her hands, and stood where Vesper positioned her — close, at the head of the bed, close enough that she could feel, even without opening toward anything, the specific wrongness radiating off her sister like heat off a fevered stone. Her own hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady either, and she pressed them flat against the scale's cool surface for a moment before doing anything else, less to ready the scale than to ready herself. She thought, with the particular dislocated clarity that arrives in the worst moments, of every measurement she'd ever taken of this exact scale — its weight, its density, the careful notes she'd kept in a ledger three rooms and half a city away — and understood that none of those numbers had ever once mattered less than they did right now, with the actual object simply a cool, heavy, waiting thing in her hands, asked to hold something far larger than any number she'd assigned it.

What Vesper did next was nothing like Wren's own work, and nothing, either, like the clean clinical Person-opening Mari practiced on her best, most ordinary days. She opened toward Mari fully, without a flinch, without the careful incremental testing Wren had needed nine cracked vessels to earn the right to skip — and then, instead of absorbing what came back, she did something Wren had no proper word for yet and would spend a long time afterward trying to name precisely: she reached past the flood itself, past the formless, structureless static that had swallowed every boundary Mari's mind used to hold itself together, toward whatever older, steadier thing in Mari was still, underneath all of it, recognizably Mari, and held onto that instead. For one terrible moment partway through, her grip on Mari's hand went white-knuckled and her whole body went rigid, as though whatever she'd reached for had nearly slipped past her in the dark before she found it again — a half-second only, gone almost before Wren registered it as wrong, but long enough that the house healer's head came up sharply and Sela took one fast step forward from the door before Vesper's shoulders eased again and the moment passed. It was, Wren would think much later, the closest she ever came to watching someone use the exact maneuver that had once been used to pull her back from the same place, twenty-six years of practice having sanded off whatever had once been desperate or improvised about it, until it looked, from the outside, almost peaceful — almost, but not quite, and Wren suspected she was one of perhaps three people alive who would have caught the difference.

"She's not gone," Vesper said, low, urgent, more to herself or to Mari than to anyone in the room. "Going. Not gone. There's a difference, and I have personally measured it from the wrong side once already in my life — I am not about to mismeasure it from this side." Her voice, for the first time since Wren had met her, had lost every trace of its careful unhurried warmth, replaced by something rawer, older, frightened in a way Wren would not have believed her capable of being twenty minutes ago. "Marigold. Mari. I need you to hear one true thing through whatever's drowning you right now, and I need you to hold onto it the way you'd hold onto a confirmed measurement, because that's the only kind of rope that's ever worked on someone like you or me — you are allowed to stop. Not forever. Not as a verdict on what you're worth. Just for today, just for this hour, you are allowed to stop holding everyone else up, because there are people in this room who are holding you instead, and they are not asking you to be useful to deserve it."

It should have sounded, Wren thought even through her own fear, like exactly the kind of thing a clever and dangerous woman would say to make a desperate family grateful. It also happened to be, as far as Wren could tell from inside the room, simply and entirely true, and she had stopped, somewhere in the last week, being able to hold both facts about Vesper Reed at a comfortable distance from each other. There wasn't time to do anything with the thought, and it wasn't the moment, but some part of her noted it anyway, the way she'd note an anomalous reading she meant to come back to later, that the easiest lies and the truest comforts apparently sounded identical from the inside of a crisis, and that she had no idea yet which one she'd just heard.

"Now," Vesper said, sharp, to Wren. "The scale. Open toward it, seeded the way I taught you, and hold the channel — not toward her, toward me. I need somewhere old and patient to set down what I'm currently carrying so I don't have to carry all of it and reach for her at the same time. You are not the buffer between her and the flood right now. You are the buffer between me and the cost of doing this properly, and I would not ask if there were any other way, and there is not."

Wren did it without question, the seeded channel opening clean and ready the way it had two days ago — though her hands shook faintly around the scale's edges as she did it, a tremor she hadn't had the last time and didn't have the spare attention to address now — and felt, in the same instant, something arrive through it that startled her more than Mari's flood ever had: not chaos, not structureless static, but a controlled, deliberate, terribly heavy stream of feeling that had clearly been carried, alone, for a very long time, by someone who had told herself for twenty-six years that she had stopped needing anywhere to set it down. It was old grief, she understood distantly, even as she held the channel steady and let it keep pouring through rather than flinching from it — old grief, and underneath it a very specific, very particular fear: the fear of a woman who had watched someone she loved go under once already, in a hall full of people who adored her and not one of them with the first idea what to do, and had never, not once in over two and a half decades, found a way to stop bracing for it happening again.

Partway through, the channel bucked — not failed, exactly, but lurched, the seeded shape Wren had built straining for one bad half-second against a current heavier than anything she'd practiced against, and she felt the same hot, almost-overflow sensation she'd felt with Mari's flood that very afternoon, the unmistakable warning that whatever she was holding might be about to outgrow the vessel holding it. She did not pull back. She thought, fast and wordless, of the buffer's actual shape rather than its theory — *wider, not thinner, let it spread rather than narrow* — and adjusted the channel on instinct alone, something she had never once tried mid-flood and would not, if asked afterward, be able to fully explain how she'd known to do. It held. The current settled, found the wider shape, and kept pouring through without tearing anything further.

The scale drank it the way it had drunk every flood before this one, patient and old and unbothered, and Wren held the channel exactly as long as she was told to, watching Vesper's face the entire time — composed again, slowly, as the weight left her, but composed now in a way that looked, for the first time since Wren had met her, like genuine relief rather than practiced control.

It took the better part of an hour. Wren would not, afterward, be able to give a clean account of most of it — only fragments, the house healer's hands steady on Mari's pulse, Sela standing rigid by the door with her arms no longer folded at all, Vesper's low, unbroken voice finding its way, again and again, back to the same handful of true things and repeating them until they had somewhere to land. *You are allowed to stop. There are people in this room holding you instead.* She said variations of it perhaps a dozen times over the course of the hour, never quite the same words twice, the way a person repeats a true thing they're afraid of getting tired in the saying, testing each new phrasing for whether it had worn thin yet, and discarding none of them, because apparently none of them had.

The light in the room shifted while it happened, the grey afternoon outside the single window sliding slowly toward evening without anyone in the room noticing it do so, and somewhere far off a bell rang the hour and then, later, the half, the only outside evidence that ordinary time was still passing at all while the room itself seemed to hold perfectly still around the one task it had left. Wren's own arms had gone stiff and aching from holding the scale steady without shifting, and she did not move them, because moving them had stopped being a thing she was willing to spend any attention on. At some point — she could not afterward say when, exactly, only that it happened somewhere in the long featureless middle of the hour — she stopped being frightened in the sharp, immediate way she'd been frightened at the start, and settled instead into something steadier and stranger: a kind of patient, focused waiting that felt, distantly, like the same competence she brought to a difficult resonance reading, except that the reading in question was her own sister's continued existence, and she found she did not know whether to be grateful for the steadiness or unsettled by how easily it had arrived.

Slowly, in stages, the way a wound closed rather than a door slammed, Mari's breathing evened, her unseeing stare narrowed back into focus, and somewhere near the end of the hour her hand, slack in Vesper's for so long, closed weakly around it on its own. Wren felt the change before she consciously registered its cause — something in the texture of the room itself loosening, the specific held-breath quality of the last hour finally beginning, by degrees, to exhale.

"There," Vesper said, the old warmth back in her voice, exhausted now but real. "There you are."

Mari's eyes found Wren's face first, the way they always had, since they were children, the order never once reversing no matter how bad things got — first Wren, then whoever else happened to be in the room, as though some old, unspoken arrangement between them required it.

"I heard you," Mari said, hoarse, barely audible. "Both of you. I don't — I don't think I can explain what I heard, but I heard it." A long pause, her eyes drifting half-shut again, her hand still loosely curled in Vesper's, and then, with the particular stubborn deflection Wren had been losing arguments to her entire life: "If anyone asks. I want it on record. I was very dignified about all of it." Wren made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh, mostly relief with nowhere else to go, the kind of sound a body makes when it has been braced too long against something that has finally, against all evidence to the contrary, let go. Sela, by the door, pressed the back of one hand briefly against her own mouth and said nothing at all, which from Sela was its own kind of speech. Vesper, watching the two sisters, looked for one unguarded moment like she envied them both more than she had words available to say so — not the crisis itself, plainly, but whatever lay underneath it, the particular ease of two people who had spent a lifetime finding each other first in any room, the order never reversing — and then the look was gone, tucked back behind the same composed warmth she wore for every patient, before anyone but Wren had time to notice it had been there at all.

"Don't explain it now," Vesper said, gently, releasing Mari's hand at last. "Sleep. Properly, this time — the kind your sister's buffer never quite managed to give you, because it was never built to do what I just did, and it did everything it could anyway. You're not fixed. I want to be honest about that the moment I can manage a full sentence, the same way your sister was honest with you the first time she tried this. You're back from where you were an hour ago. That's not nothing, and it's also not enough, not on its own, not for very much longer."

"How much longer," Mari murmured, already half gone again, the question arriving more out of old professional habit than any real capacity to absorb the answer.

"Not tonight's question," Vesper said. "Tomorrow's, maybe, or the day after's. Tonight's only job is sleeping, and you're already doing it well enough that I'm fairly sure you stopped listening to me three words ago, which I'll take as a good sign rather than an insult." A small, tired warmth had crept back into her voice as she said it, the first hint, since she'd burst through the door, of the woman Wren had come to know in a dye-shed two evenings running.

Mari was asleep again, this time genuinely, within minutes, her breathing settling into the slow, even rhythm Wren had learned, over three weeks of watching it, to recognize as the real thing rather than its counterfeit, and the house healer's hands finally came away from her pulse with something like real relief in his face for the first time since the runner had been sent. He busied himself for a while with small unnecessary tasks of his own — refilling a water glass no one had asked for, checking a lamp wick that didn't need checking — the same restless aftermath Wren was beginning to recognize as universal among people who had just watched something nearly go wrong and needed their hands occupied before their minds would agree to stand down.

Vesper sat heavily in the room's one chair, the kind of sitting that came from a body that had spent everything it had to spend, her hands loose in her lap and her hair still escaping its pins, and for a long moment she simply closed her eyes, not asleep, just resting in the particular way of someone giving herself permission, briefly, to stop performing competence for an audience. Wren had never seen her sit like that before — not the careful, considered stillness she wore in the lock-house's good chair, ankles crossed, always faintly composed even at rest, but something looser and more honest, a body simply occupying a chair because the chair happened to be there and the body could no longer be anywhere else. It occurred to her, distantly, that she was looking at something almost nobody else had ever been allowed to see, and that the privilege of it sat strangely alongside everything else she'd learned about this woman in the last two days. Wren, watching her, felt the specific, faintly absurd urge — the kind that always arrived several beats behind whatever crisis had actually just happened — to tell Bartholomew about the whole impossible hour, except that Bartholomew was thirty miles away in his cradle, and the scale that had actually done the work tonight was sitting right there in her own lap, which seemed like exactly the sort of detail he would have found personally insulting if she ever said it out loud. She let the urge pass, unactioned, as further proof that nearly losing her sister was apparently not enough on its own to cure her of being, at bottom, a little bit ridiculous, and set the scale down at last on the small table beside the bed, her arms aching properly now that she'd finally let them, and let the question she'd been holding all hour finally find its way out.

"What did I just do," Wren said. "For you. You said the scale was a buffer between you and the cost. I felt what came through. That wasn't — Vesper, that wasn't a small contained feeling like Bram and I practiced. That was years of something."

"It was," Vesper said, simply, not denying it, too tired in the moment, Wren suspected, to perform the careful composure she usually wore even this far past exhausted. "You felt old grief, and you felt it accurately, which is more than most people manage even when they're trying. I'm not going to pretend it was nothing, because I think you'd see through the pretending in about four seconds, and I no longer have the energy tonight to be unconvincing. I'll explain the rest of it properly. Not tonight. Tonight I would like to sit in this chair a while longer and be grateful the number we just measured wasn't the final one, and I'd ask you to let me have that, just this once, before you ask me to account for the rest of it."

"Whose grief was it."

Something crossed Vesper's face at that — not refusal, exactly, more like a door swinging partway open and then, deliberately, being eased back to where it had been. "Someone I measured the wrong number for, a long time ago. That's all I have left in me tonight, and it happens to be true, which is the only kind of answer I'm willing to hand anyone while I'm this tired. Ask me again when I've slept. I'll still owe you the rest of it then, and I won't have gotten any better at avoiding the question, but I might manage more than one sentence at a time."

"That's not very reassuring, as deferrals go."

"No," Vesper agreed, with the ghost of her usual dry warmth finally surfacing through the exhaustion, "it isn't. I find most true things aren't, when you get right down to it. The reassuring ones are usually the ones somebody constructed on purpose, for the occasion." She closed her eyes again briefly, and when she opened them, something in her had settled, like a measurement finally come to rest. "Thank you. For tonight. I mean that with considerably more weight than I have the energy to put behind it properly, but I'd rather say it plainly and badly than not say it at all."

Wren let her have the silence after that, watching the still water of the channel beneath the floor grate run on, patient and constant, while her sister slept and the room's fear slowly, finally, began to recede. The house healer had gone quiet in his corner, his small unnecessary tasks finally exhausted, and Sela had at last taken a seat on the room's low stool, her arms loose now, watching Mari's even breathing with the particular fierce attention of someone recording it for later, in case she needed proof to herself that it had actually happened this way. Outside the window the light had gone properly toward evening, the grey rooftops beyond it turning the deeper blue-grey of approaching dusk, and somewhere below in the Quiet House's own courtyard a door opened and closed, ordinary domestic sounds resuming as though the whole building hadn't just held its breath for the better part of an hour.

Wren would be afraid, she suspected, of what she'd just learned about the shape of an exchange between two people who measured each other for a living — that it could run two directions at once, and that she had just been trusted to carry the older, heavier half of it without ever quite being asked whether she wanted the job. She thought of Iris's careful, deliberate warning, *one came to my office four years ago and asked me whether there was any way to undo it,* and set it down again beside the memory of grief she'd just held steady in a scale at her own two hands, and found, as she had every time she'd tried this particular reckoning over the last two days, that neither fact cancelled the other out.

She thought, too, of the scale itself, cooling now on the small table beside the bed, and of how strange it was that an object three hundred years dead had spent its evening holding both her sister's flood and Vesper Reed's old grief inside the same patient, ancient quiet, asking nothing of either of them in return, and gave brief, useless thanks that at least one presence in the room tonight had wanted nothing back for what it carried.

Tonight she let herself have only the relief, because the relief was real and so was her sister's pulse, and Iris Hale's warning could go on waiting, wherever Wren had been keeping it, until it had something solid enough to finally attach itself to. It did not feel, tonight, like that place was very far away anymore. She gathered the scale, finally, wrapping it back into its cloth with hands that had nearly steadied, and let Sela walk her to the door, leaving Vesper still seated, eyes closed, in the one chair the small bright room had to offer, looking, for once, exactly as tired as everything she had just done should have made her look.
