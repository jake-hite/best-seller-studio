# Chapter Twenty — What Stays Open

Six weeks later, Mari took her first full day off in three years, and the household treated it with the specific reverence usually reserved for a holy day nobody had previously believed existed.

The morning of it, Wren woke before either of them, the way she had most mornings since the Quiet House, her own sleep still settling into whatever new shape it was going to keep now that her edges no longer closed quite as completely as they used to. She lay still for a while, cataloguing the change the way she catalogued everything: a faint, ambient awareness of the house around her, of Hesper already awake two rooms over, of Mari's sleep one floor up, neither of them pressing on her the way the open hour at the Quiet House had pressed, just present, the way the channel's voice was present under the Quiet House's floorboards, a fact of the building rather than a demand from it. She had decided, somewhere in the second week, to stop being alarmed by this every single morning and simply note it, the way she noted weather. Today's reading: porous, mild, no immediate cause for concern.

"I don't know what to do with myself," Mari announced, at breakfast, sounding genuinely alarmed by the prospect, turning her teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer in a gesture Wren recognized, with some private amusement, as one she'd apparently passed down without either of them noticing. Hesper, without looking up from her own tea, said, "You're going to sit in that chair and be useless for an entire morning if I have to sit on you myself," in the tone of a woman who had clearly been rehearsing the sentence for some time and was not going to be talked out of delivering it.

"I have patients," Mari tried.

"Your patients have a ward full of other healers today, three of whom I personally vetted myself twenty years ago and would trust with my own life, and you are going to sit in that chair." Hesper set down her cup with the precise, final click of a woman closing an argument rather than continuing one. "I raised you to be capable. I did not raise you to be incapable of sitting in a chair, and if that's what's happened to you, I'd like to know exactly which year it happened so I can apologize for my specific part in it properly instead of generally."

Mari sat in the chair. She lasted, by Wren's count, forty minutes — Wren actually counted, glancing at the workshop clock she'd brought down to the kitchen for exactly this purpose, because some habits of measurement had simply become how she loved people now, and she'd stopped apologizing for it — before she found a basket of mending and declared, with the air of someone negotiating a treaty, that mending wasn't *work*, exactly, it was *restful occupation of the hands*, and Wren let her have the distinction rather than argue the point, because watching her sister slowly relearn how to be a person who occasionally did nothing useful was, itself, a kind of restful occupation.

She was better. Not fixed — the house's healer had been careful about that word from the first week, and Wren had learned to be careful with it too — but genuinely, measurably better, the metallic patience under her skin gone, the composure that used to arrive fastest exactly when she was most afraid replaced, slowly, by something that looked, to Wren's relief, almost ordinary. Her appetite had come back first, oddly specific in its preferences in a way it had never been before — she had developed, in the space of a fortnight, a genuine and faintly comic devotion to the bakery on the third bridge's seeded loaf, to the point that Hesper had started buying two on principle rather than argue about who got the second half. Her sleep had taken longer, fitful for the first two weeks and then, gradually, ordinary, the kind that simply happened rather than the kind that had to be fought for.

She had gone back to the Lowmarket ward three weeks after the Quiet House, but on a schedule with actual edges to it now, a schedule that included, for the first time in her professional life, days that were simply hers, and twice now Wren had watched her say the word *no* to a patient's family out loud, plainly, without apologizing for it afterward, which struck Wren as a more remarkable piece of magic than anything either of them had built in a dye-shed.

"I keep waiting to feel guilty about it," Mari admitted, one evening, watching the mending pile shrink under her hands, the lamp throwing its small warm circle over the basket and the half-darned heel of one of Hesper's stockings. She held the needle still for a moment, turning the thought over the way Wren turned over a reading she didn't quite trust yet. "The no. I keep bracing for the part where it catches up to me. It hasn't yet. I don't know what to do with that either."

"What does the bracing actually feel like?" Wren asked, mostly because she genuinely wanted the data, and partly because she'd learned, this past month, that asking Mari a specific question got further than offering her a general comfort. "I'm asking professionally. I've spent four years cataloguing what things feel like before I decide what they are."

Mari considered that with more seriousness than the question probably deserved, which Wren appreciated. "Like waiting for a knock at the door that never comes. Like the version of me from a year ago is still in the room somewhere, just out of sight, waiting to point out that I've finally proven what everyone always suspected — that I was only ever good for as long as I never stopped."

"Maybe it's not going to come," Wren said. "Maybe that was never actually a law of the world. Maybe it was just a thing you got taught so early nobody, including you, ever thought to ask whether it was true."

"That's an alarmingly direct thing to say to me over mending."

"I've been told I'm direct now. It's a whole new development. I'm finding I rather enjoy it." She considered her own teacup for a moment, turning it the same quarter-turn Mari had turned hers at breakfast, and decided to say the rest of it too, since apparently that was simply what she did now. "I used to save the direct things for Bartholomew, mostly, because he couldn't argue back and didn't seem to mind the practice. I'm finding people are a more interesting audience. They occasionally argue back, which turns out to be useful information I wasn't getting from a piece of dead king's spine, however good a listener he is."

"How is Bartholomew," Mari asked, with the specific gravity of someone humoring a sister's most beloved eccentricity, which Wren had long since stopped minding and started, faintly, enjoying.

"Steady at four. Green strip. Entirely unimpressed with the events of the last two months, as far as I can tell, which I find very grounding."

Cae Rookwood's case was settled, in the specific, imperfect way Wren had learned, over the last two months, most things in Aldermere actually got settled: not won outright, not lost outright, but argued down to something survivable. It had not looked, for most of the hearing itself, like it was going to go that way.

The hearing chamber itself was smaller than Wren had expected, given how much of her life it had occupied in the two weeks leading up to it — a plain, high-ceilinged room off the Council annex, its walls lined with the kind of dark, water-stained wood that suggested the building had stood through more than one flood the Riverbed Council had quietly decided not to commemorate, a single tall window letting in grey midmorning light that made the whole proceeding feel, to Wren, oddly like standing trial inside an aquarium. Cae's mother sat rigid in the front row, her hands folded so tightly in her lap that Wren could see the white at her knuckles from across the room. Cae himself sat beside her on a chair clearly built for someone twice his size, feet not reaching the floor, dressed in a collar visibly too stiff for him, swinging his legs in small, controlled arcs that Wren recognized, from two months of teaching him, as the precise physical shape of a person doing his level best to seem calmer than he was.

Iris had warned her, the week before, exactly what to expect, in the brisk, unsentimental cadence she used for delivering bad news she'd rather get through quickly than soften. *Aldric doesn't lose arguments. He loses cases, occasionally, but he does it by making the other side look reckless rather than by losing the argument itself, and you should plan for him to come at your credentials before he ever comes at your theory, because your theory's actually sound and he knows it, and he's not going to fight you on the ground where you're strongest.* Wren had nodded along at the time, certain she understood what that meant. She had not, it turned out, fully understood it until she was sitting in the chamber's narrow witness chair watching it happen.

The Houses' advocate, a precise, unhurried man named Master Aldric, had spent the better part of an hour doing exactly what Wren had privately feared the moment she saw him take his seat — grey-haired, soft-spoken, dressed in the unornamented dark robes the Houses' counsel always wore, the kind of man who never once raised his voice because he had learned, decades ago, that a calm voice was harder to argue against than a loud one. He had asked Wren a series of perfectly reasonable, perfectly technical questions about the buffer theory itself, nodding along amiably at her answers, building, Wren realized too late, an entire foundation of her own careful credibility before he ever moved to dismantle it. Rather than attack her theory directly, he attacked its source, reminding the magistrate, gently and at length, that the alchemist offering this technical assessment had spent the better part of a season apprenticed to Vesper Reed, a practitioner whose own methods had, by the reform faction's own records, left at least four prior subjects Severed beyond recall — and asked, in the specific reasonable tone of a man who knew exactly how reasonable he sounded, whether the chamber was truly prepared to gamble one boy's entire future on a technique one generation removed from a woman the Court itself no longer trusted.

Wren felt the question land the way she imagined a held breath felt right before it finally gave out. She had rehearsed, in the days leading up to this, at least four different answers to exactly this line of attack, and found, in the actual moment, that every single one of them dissolved into something thin and inadequate the second Aldric's careful, unhurried voice actually finished asking it. Iris, beside her, had gone very still, the particular stillness of a woman doing the math on how much of the morning's careful work was about to come apart. For one long, terrible minute, watching the magistrate's face go carefully unreadable in the particular way Wren had learned, these past two months, meant a decision sliding the wrong direction — her pen no longer moving across her own notes, her eyes resting somewhere past the chamber's far window rather than on either advocate — Wren understood that she was about to lose, and that losing would cost Cae a great deal more than it cost her. She thought, with a sudden, vertiginous lurch, of Iris's warning in the cluttered office two months ago — *one came to my office four years ago and asked me whether there was any way to undo it* — and understood, in that exact moment, precisely how thoroughly her own history was now somebody else's weapon, and how little she could do about it from the witness chair.

It was Cae who fixed it, not Wren, not Iris, not any careful rebuttal either of them had prepared. He stood up, without being asked, in the middle of Master Aldric's closing sentence, his chair scraping loud against the old floorboards in the sudden silence that followed, his mother's hand reaching for his sleeve a half-second too late to stop him. He did not wait to be given leave to speak. He said, in the same plain, unhedged voice he'd used in Iris's cluttered office two weeks before, that he didn't much care whose teacher had taught whose theory to whom, he only cared whether anyone in the room actually meant to ask him what he wanted before deciding it for him a second time, because nobody had asked the first time, and he'd had two years of being decided about instead of asked, and he would like the chamber to notice that particular difference even if it found everything else about his case very complicated.

The magistrate was quiet long enough that Wren stopped breathing properly — long enough that Wren heard, with absurd clarity, the single tall window rattling faintly in its frame, the small shuffle of someone in the back of the gallery shifting their weight, Cae's own breathing, fast and a little ragged now that the nerve that had carried him to his feet had nothing left to spend itself on but waiting — and then said, in a voice considerably less composed than the one she'd used for the rest of the hearing, that she did not feel she could in good conscience decide a child's future today without first answering the exact question he had just put to her directly, and granted the pilot-program exception on the spot — less, she said plainly, out of full confidence in an unproven buffer theory than out of the conviction that a boy who could say that to a room full of magistrates had earned the right to be heard rather than filed.

Cae's mother made a sound that was not quite a sob and not quite a laugh, and put both hands over her own face for a moment before she remembered where she was and lowered them again, and Cae himself sat back down rather abruptly, as though his legs had only just remembered they were holding him up, and looked, for one unguarded second, considerably more like an eleven-year-old who had just done something enormous than like the composed small adult he'd managed to sound like a moment before.

The exception itself, once granted, took the shape Iris had been quietly building toward for weeks: a newly created pilot program — her doing, mostly, pushed through the reform faction on the strength of exactly this case — pairing young Naturals with trained Material-openers for resonance education at home rather than confinement at an Armoring House. It was not, Iris was careful to say, a victory over the Houses themselves, which remained exactly as entrenched as they had been two months earlier. It was one boy, kept out of one institution, by one narrow, hard-won exception that the reform faction intended to use as precedent for the next case, and the one after that, in the slow, unglamorous, decades-long way real change actually seemed to happen, as far as Wren could now tell.

Wren taught the first three sessions herself, in the same dye-shed where she'd built the buffer, on the same bench, with the same humidity log open beside her out of sheer habit even though nothing in the lesson actually required it. She prepared for the first session the way she prepared for everything else that mattered — a written plan, three contingencies, a fourth contingency in case the first three failed in some combination she hadn't anticipated — and then watched the entire careful structure dissolve within the first ten minutes, because Cae, it turned out, did not want a lesson plan. He wanted to know things, specifically and in whatever order they occurred to him, and Wren found, to her genuine surprise, that she was good at answering them — not in the way she was good at logging humidity readings, where the satisfaction came from precision, but in the way Mari had always seemed to be good with patients, an ease built out of taking the question exactly as seriously as the person asking it, regardless of how it sounded out loud.

"Do wyrms ever get embarrassed?" Cae asked, on the second session, apropos of nothing, holding the small resonance stone Wren had given him to practice on with the particular careful grip of a boy who had already been told once that week he could break something expensive. "About how big their feelings are. Bartholomew, I mean. You said he reacts to things."

Wren considered the question with more seriousness than it probably required, which was, she'd discovered, generally the correct amount of seriousness to give Cae's questions. "I don't know that 'embarrassed' is exactly the right word for what a piece of dead king's spine experiences, but I've wondered something similar. He goes a deeper green when something's actually wrong, and a paler one when it's minor, and I've never once seen him go the deep green over something that turned out to be nothing. So either he's never embarrassed by anything, which seems unlikely given how much time he spends around me, or he's simply decided embarrassment isn't worth a whole color change, which I find I rather admire." She turned the comparison over once more before she let it go. "I think the actual answer is that he doesn't have an audience to be embarrassed in front of, and feelings being big isn't really the problem people make it out to be. It's having somewhere to put them that's the trick. He just sits with his, the same color the whole time, until they pass or don't. I've started thinking that's not actually a bad model." She caught herself a full ten minutes later, with Cae nodding along seriously and the resonance stone entirely forgotten on the bench between them, and laughed at herself for the first time that week. "We have wandered very far from the lesson plan."

"I liked this better than the lesson plan," Cae said, with the flat, unhedged honesty that made him, in Wren's private opinion, considerably easier to teach than most adults she'd encountered.

Corvin came by the dye-shed three times that month, ostensibly to inspect her continued use of unsanctioned guild theory, and stayed considerably longer than inspection required every single time.

The first visit, he mostly just watched from the doorway, arms folded, saying nothing for the better part of twenty minutes while Cae worked through the second exercise, and Wren let him watch without comment, recognizing, by now, the particular quality of his silence — not the disapproving kind, which had its own distinct posture, but the kind he used when something was costing him more than he wanted to spend words on yet. He left that first evening with nothing more than a nod and an instruction to keep the humidity log current, which she understood, by then, to be Corvin for *I don't have the words for this tonight and I'd rather not force them*.

"Yvet would have liked this," he said, the second visit, watching Cae carefully practice the small, deliberate opening exercises Wren had adapted from her own recovery. "Teaching it properly, I mean. Not hiding it in a locked cabinet because the one time anybody tried anything new it went badly enough to frighten a whole guild out of trying again for thirty years." He didn't look at Wren when he said it, which she understood, by now, was simply how Corvin said the things that cost him the most.

"You could un-lock the cabinet," Wren said. "Officially, I mean. Write the buffer theory into the actual register. Put my name on it if you want, or don't, I don't actually care which, but it shouldn't sit in your private correspondence for another thirty years because the guild's too proud to admit a journeywoman found something it missed."

"I've been thinking about exactly that," Corvin said, "more than I've told you. Give me until spring. I'd like to do it properly, with real cross-referencing, the kind Yvet would have insisted on, rather than rushing it because you've made me sentimental in my old age." He paused at the door, the way he always did when he meant to say something and was working up to it sideways. "Your bench is still yours, for whatever that's worth, given you haven't sat at it in two months."

"I know. I think I might actually come back to it. Properly, I mean — not as an excuse to avoid the rest of my life, the way I think I used it before, but because I genuinely missed the ledger." She considered that, surprised to find it true. "I missed the boring parts most of all, if I'm honest. I didn't expect that."

"Nobody ever does," Corvin said, "until the boring parts turn out to be the ones that were actually holding everything else up."

He came on a Tuesday, holding a glass jar with the specific careful grip of a man who had already been bitten once that evening and was determined not to let it show.

"I need a second opinion," he announced, by way of greeting, before Wren had finished opening the door, holding the jar up to the lamplight where something small and iridescent and visibly furious paced the inside of it in tight, outraged circles. "I think it's a dye-beetle. I think I've seen one of yours do something similar to a swatch of indigo last spring. I would like someone who actually knows what she's looking at to confirm that before I write it into the guild register as a new subspecies and humiliate myself in front of people who already think I spend too much time talking to insects."

"You walked the whole way upriver. After dark. For a bug."

"I walked the whole way upriver for your opinion on a bug, which is an entirely different errand, and one I would like credit for, if it's not too much trouble." He held the jar out, and something in the gesture — earnest, slightly defensive, utterly without performance — undid her a little, the way it always did lately and never quite stopped surprising her.

She took the jar to the window, turned it once in the lamplight, and felt the small, specific satisfaction of being asked a real question by someone who'd clearly thought about it on the entire walk over rather than invented it as an excuse. "It's a dye-beetle. A young one — they don't get the full shimmer until their second season, which is probably why you didn't recognize it."

"I assumed it was sick," Bram said. "The pacing. I thought maybe it was dying and I'd walked an hour to bring you a dying bug, which would have been a genuinely terrible evening for both of us."

"It's not dying. It's furious. There's a difference, and you'll learn to tell them apart eventually — the dying ones go still in the corners, the furious ones pace the whole jar like they're personally offended by the glass." She caught the look on his face, the small flicker of someone realizing he'd gotten something wrong in front of the one person whose opinion on bugs he'd walked an hour to get, and found, unexpectedly, that she wanted to fix it rather than let the moment sit there being awkward between them. "It's not an unreasonable thing to assume. Most people can't tell the difference. I only can because I've spent four years staring at vat-house cracks waiting for them to show up."

"I wasn't trying to seem like I knew more than I did," he said, a little stiffly, and she realized, too late, that her correction had landed somewhere she hadn't intended it to. "I know you're the one who actually knows the insects. I just wanted an excuse to—" He stopped, visibly annoyed with himself now, and started over in a different direction entirely. "It doesn't matter. You're right. It's furious, not dying. I'll remember the difference."

"I didn't mean it as a correction," Wren said, and heard, with some discomfort, how much that sounded like exactly the kind of thing a person said right after delivering a correction. She tried again, more carefully this time, the way she'd have approached a reading that hadn't come out the way she expected. "I mean — you weren't wrong to be careful with it. I'd rather you bring me a bug you weren't sure about than not bring it at all because you were worried about getting it wrong in front of me. That's actually the whole point of asking someone."

He considered that for a moment, the stiffness easing out of his shoulders by degrees. "All right. Noted. For the record, I'd still like credit for the walk, the wrongness of my diagnosis notwithstanding."

"Fully noted. Entered into the ledger." She handed the jar back, careful not to let it loose in the kitchen a second time, an incident neither of them had fully recovered from. "You can let it go in the vat-house. There's a whole colony in the cracks by the south wall. It'll be insufferably pleased with itself within the hour."

"I was hoping you'd say that." He didn't move to leave. He stood instead in her doorway with the jar held loosely now, working up to something sideways the way he always did, and for a moment Wren thought he might finally say whatever it was he'd been visibly not-saying for three weeks running — and then watched him decide, plainly, visibly, not to, and ask instead whether she'd had any luck with the humidity readings, which was its own kind of answer, and one she found she didn't mind receiving.

It became, without either of them quite deciding it should, a pattern after that — not the beetle specifically, though there would be others before the season turned, but the visit itself: some small, specific, entirely unglamorous offering every third evening or so, a pigment that finally behaved, a properly translated fragment of an old Brindle text on resonance density, an hour at her own bench that had nothing to do with anyone's crisis at all. Neither of them said anything out loud about what it was, exactly, that had quietly shifted between the recovery bed and now, and Wren found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she didn't feel any urgency to name it before it was ready to be named.

"You could just say it," Hesper observed, one evening, watching Bram leave with the specific satisfaction of a mother who had given up pretending she wasn't paying close attention. She was darning again, the same stocking heel Mari had abandoned weeks ago and never quite finished, her needle moving in small, unhurried stitches that didn't match the directness of what she was saying at all. "Whatever it is. He's clearly waiting for permission, and you're clearly not in any hurry to give it, and I find I'm too tired after thirty years of this family's particular brand of feelings management to watch you do the slow version if you don't actually need to."

"Say what, precisely. I'd like the question asked plainly, since you're apparently in a plain mood tonight."

"That you're courting. Or being courted. Or whatever the current word is for two grown people who bring each other beetles and humidity readings instead of flowers, which, for the record, I find considerably more romantic than flowers, but I am an old dye-woman and my opinion on romance was formed entirely by what holds up in a vat."

Wren laughed, surprised by it, and found she didn't actually mind the question the way she might have, six months ago, when any version of this conversation would have sent her straight to the workshop to alphabetize something that didn't need alphabetizing. "I'm not avoiding it," she said instead. "I want to be careful about that, actually, now that I'm apparently being careful about everything I say out loud. I like the slow version. I like that nobody's required to perform anything yet, that there isn't a date by which it has to have become a particular kind of thing with a particular kind of name." She considered it further, turning her teacup the same quarter-turn she'd noticed Mari do, the same one she now suspected had come from Hesper originally, handed down through three women without any of them noticing the gesture traveling. "I think I get to decide the pace is the point, rather than the obstacle. That's new too. I spent a long time assuming that taking my time with a feeling meant something was wrong with the feeling. I'm finding out it mostly just means I'm being thorough."

"Thorough," Hesper repeated, with the particular fondness she reserved for watching her daughters describe their own hearts using vocabulary better suited to a ledger.

"I'd like to enjoy being thorough about it for a while before anyone, including me, rushes it along."

"That," Hesper said, setting the darning down at last, "is possibly the wisest thing either of my daughters has said to me in your entire lives, and I include myself fully in the category of people who needed to hear it. Your father would have liked him, for what it's worth. He brought me a jar of something once, in the early days. I don't even remember what was in it anymore. I remember that he walked a long way to bring it."

She saw Vesper once more before autumn turned fully, at the lock-house, at Vesper's own quiet invitation rather than Wren's seeking. The note had arrived by ordinary courier, three lines in a careful hand, asking nothing and assuming nothing, which Wren noted, walking the towpath out to meet it, was itself a kind of change. The garden lavender had gone to seed, the heavy purple spikes gone brittle and pale, and the lock-house's water sat exactly as still and contained as it always had, the controlled water of someone whose whole trade had once been keeping things from moving. Vesper looked, Wren thought, older than she had in any of their previous meetings — not diminished, exactly, but unguarded in a way that had nothing left to perform, sitting on the same bench by the water with her hands loose and open in her lap rather than folded.

"I've stepped back from the work," Vesper said, without preamble. "The kind I used to do. I want you to hear that from me directly rather than through Iris's careful files, which I understand are considerably less kind to me than I might once have insisted they be, and which I have decided not to argue with." She paused, watching a moorhen pick its way along the lock's far edge, before she went on. "I'm not equipped, yet, to do the work the honest way — your way — and I'd rather stop than keep doing it the only way I know how, now that I can't unknow what that way actually cost the people who trusted me with it."

"What will you do instead?"

"I don't entirely know yet." She said it plainly, without the old hedge that would once have followed, no antithesis arriving to balance it back into something safer. "I find I've never once, in twenty-six years, had to answer that question for myself, separate from being needed by someone. It is, I'll admit, a strange thing to be my age and as new at something as Cae is at his lessons." She looked, for a moment, almost amused at her own admission. "Iris has suggested I might be useful to the reform faction's records — the four practitioners, properly documented this time, with their own accounts attached rather than mine, their own words instead of my summary of them. I find the idea terrifies me considerably more than near-Dissolution ever did, which I take as a reasonably reliable sign that it's the right next thing to attempt."

"I'm glad," Wren said, and meant it, without quite being able to say whether she meant glad that Vesper was trying, or glad that she herself no longer felt obligated to fix whatever the trying didn't manage to finish. Both, perhaps. She had stopped, somewhere in the last six weeks, needing the feeling to resolve into only one clean thing, and found she could simply let it sit there, mixed, the way the lock-house's water sat mixed with whatever the river brought it before it ever reached the gate.

They sat a while longer without saying much else, which struck Wren as its own kind of ending — not the tearful, complete kind a story might have wanted, but the ordinary kind, two people who had cost each other something real sitting together by still water because the alternative, not sitting there, seemed like a worse use of the evening. Vesper did not offer to walk her back, and Wren did not ask her to, and when she finally stood to go, Vesper said only, "Thank you for coming when I asked instead of when you needed something. I don't believe anyone's done that for me in longer than I can comfortably say," and Wren found she had no tidy answer for that at all, and let the silence stand in for one.

She walked home along the towpath in the gold late light, past the lock-house's still, controlled water, and did not look back, and did not feel the specific need to that she might have, once.

The workshop on Lantern Street was exactly as she'd left it two months ago, dust settled thin over the ledger, Bartholomew waiting, faintly and technically alive, in his brass cradle by the window. She lit the lamp, opened the ledger to a clean page, and worked for the better part of an hour on nothing more urgent than the season's first humidity readings, the long, satisfying, unglamorous arithmetic of doing a thing carefully enough that it stopped, for all practical purposes, being anything other than exactly what it was.

Somewhere past the second measurement, she heard footsteps on the lane outside — Bram, probably, or Corvin, or no one in particular, she found she didn't especially need to know which before she decided what to do about it — and she did not get up to see who it was, and she did not close the door against the evening air the way four careful years of habit would once have insisted she do.

She left it open, on purpose, mid-page, for no urgent reason at all, and went back to her ledger, and let the ordinary sounds of Lantern Street drift in across the threshold exactly as far as they wanted to come.
