# Chapter One — A Perfectly Reasonable Amount of Feeling

The trick to handling a wyrm scale that was still faintly, technically alive was to want it less than it wanted to be left alone, and Wren had spent four years getting genuinely, professionally bad at wanting things.

It was, by the workshop clock — a squat brass thing on the back shelf that Corvin wound every morning with the same three turns, no more, no less, the way some men touched a doorframe for luck — eleven minutes past seven, which meant she had been sitting in this exact position, spine straight, hands open and empty above the cradle, for the better part of forty minutes, long enough that her lower back had filed its own quiet protest and been overruled. The workshop smelled the way it always smelled at this hour: cold ash from last night's banked stove, the mineral tang of the quieting cloths drying on their line above the sink, and underneath both, faint and specific, the smell Wren had never managed to describe to anyone outside the trade — old paper left in a damp room, if the paper had once had opinions. Outside, Lantern Street was just beginning to wake up around her: a cart somewhere two streets over, the particular creak of the bakery's shutters going up, a gull arguing with another gull over something neither of them would actually eat. None of it touched the room. The room had its own weather, and right now its weather was Wren, holding very still, and Bartholomew, deciding whether to notice her.

"You're doing the breathing again," said Corvin, from the doorway, in the tone of a man who had walked in on this exact scene roughly four hundred times and had decided, around the hundredth, that commenting on it was cheaper than being surprised by it.

"I'm not doing anything," Wren said, not moving, because the scale was balanced on the brass cradle in front of her with the particular stillness of something that knew it was being watched and resented it, and the last apprentice who'd lost focus at this stage had needed three days of bed rest and a humiliating letter to his mother. "I am holding very still and thinking about absolutely nothing, which you have told me, personally, on multiple occasions, is the correct technique."

"I've told you to want it *less*. I have never told you to want *nothing*. Wanting nothing is how Aldric ended up in bed for a week wondering why his own boots felt like someone else's."

"Aldric wanted his lunch. That's different. That's an active want. I am a calm, blank, uninteresting person to this scale right now."

"You are the least blank person I have ever met."

"I'm blank *at it*," Wren said. "It's a very specific, professional blankness. You wouldn't understand, you're a Tuesday-blank person, I'm a — " She lost the thread of the sentence because the scale, at that exact moment, did the thing it did roughly once every dozen sessions, which was to remember, briefly and without malice, that it had once been part of something enormous, and the memory came off it like heat off a stone three hours after the sun went down. Not dangerous. Not even close to dangerous, not at this distance, not with the cradle's iron baffles doing their patient unglamorous job. Just enormous, and old, and entirely uninterested in the fact that a twenty-three-year-old alchemist's journeywoman was, at this exact moment, the only thing standing between it and the open air of Confluence's most respectable apothecary district.

The hairs on Wren's forearms rose anyway, a purely physical fact she'd long since stopped being embarrassed by, the way her body insisted on having an opinion about a thing her training had already decided not to. Her mouth had gone dry. Her pulse, she knew without checking, had ticked up by perhaps four beats, no more, the same small honest betrayal it always managed even when the rest of her held perfectly, professionally still. She let it. The body could be afraid. The body was allowed. It was the *wanting* that had to stay home.

Wren did not flinch. She had trained herself, with real and specific discipline, not to flinch at this part, because flinching was a kind of wanting too — wanting it to stop, wanting it to be smaller, wanting, wanting, wanting, and the whole maddening genius of the Permeance was that wanting was the same instrument as power, so a flinch wasn't a failure of nerve. It was a *signal*, sent straight down whatever invisible line connected her open hand to the thing in the cradle, and the signal said *here, this part of me is soft, lean on this part,* and the scale, being very old and very saturated and not even slightly malicious about it, would lean.

So she didn't flinch. She thought, instead, with the specific deliberate dullness of long practice, about the quarterly inventory report she owed the guild by Thursday, and about whether she had in fact returned Petra's crucible tongs — she hadn't, she was almost certain she hadn't, there was a particular guilty itch behind that thought that felt less like memory and more like an unpaid debt — and about a really quite boring argument she'd had with herself last week regarding optimal storage humidity for unstabilized residue (sixty-two percent, she'd decided, against her own earlier instinct toward sixty-five, on the strength of a single badly-stored sample three years back that had gone brittle at the edges in a way she still thought about more than was strictly healthy), and the heat-off-a-stone feeling subsided, the way it always did when she gave it nothing to hold on to, and the scale went back to being, for all practical purposes, a very pretty, very valuable, extremely dead-looking piece of keratin sitting in a brass cradle in a workshop on Lantern Street.

"There," she said, and let her shoulders down from somewhere up near her ears. "Boring. I was so boring just now you could've sold tickets."

"You undersell yourself," Corvin said, coming the rest of the way into the room, which from him was practically a hug. He was sixty-one years old, built like a man who had spent thirty of those years lifting things that did not want to be lifted, and he had the kind of face that had apparently decided sometime in his forties that smiling was a young man's extravagance it could no longer afford. He looked at the scale the way other men looked at racehorses or, Wren suspected privately, the way some men in better families looked at daughters who'd tested into the Court. "Bartholomew behaving?"

"Bartholomew is always behaving. Bartholomew has never once let me down. I would like that noted formally, in case anyone ever again suggests that naming a piece of evidence is unprofessional."

"I have never suggested that."

"You suggested it in front of the whole guild assembly in Brindle."

"I suggested that naming a scale after a dead king was unprofessional. The naming itself I have no opinion on."

"He looks like a Bartholomew," Wren said, with the absolute conviction of someone who had clearly had this argument enough times to have worn smooth the part of her brain that questioned it, and bent to mark the cradle's pressure gauge before she let herself go back to the ledger, because there was an order to this, and the order mattered more than the argument did. First the gauge — needle steady at four, which it had been steady at for two weeks now, long enough that she'd started to distrust the steadiness a little, the way you distrusted a child being unusually quiet. Then the temperature strip taped along the cradle's inner lip, which had gone the pale, unremarkable green that meant nothing at all was wrong, which was the single most satisfying color in her entire professional life — better than blue, better than the rare, longed-for, faintly embarrassing thrill of gold, because green meant *nothing happened today*, and nothing happening today, around something this old, was the whole and entire point of her job. Then the ledger.

She liked the ledger's smell almost as much as its contents: leather gone soft at the spine from four years of being opened and closed at the same angle, ink that had bled, very slightly, through page eleven from the year she'd still been learning how much pressure a nib actually needed. She turned to the day's column and wrote the numbers down in the small, upright hand her grandmother's journals had taught her to imitate before she'd even known she was imitating anything, and only then did she let her shoulders drop the last finger's width they'd been holding.

"You photograph the gauge readings now," Corvin said, watching her do it, in the tone of someone noting a development he had opinions about but had decided, on balance, not to share.

"I sketch them. There's a difference. A photograph is somebody else's eye. A sketch is mine, which means if I get it wrong, I get it wrong in a way I can retrace later and learn something from. You taught me that, actually, in my second month, you just don't remember because you say true things constantly and at low volume and never seem to notice when one of them lands."

"I notice," Corvin said. "I just don't make a parade of it."

"This was a parade. I made you a parade just now, with my mouth, and you stood there and let it happen, which is the most flattering thing you've done all week."

"It's Tuesday."

"It's an early week."

Corvin made a sound that, on a less disciplined face, might have qualified as a huff of amusement, and crossed to his own bench on the far side of the room, where a row of smaller scales — none of them Bartholomew's age or weight class, ordinary stock, the kind the guild actually sold rather than merely guarded — sat waiting for the day's unglamorous maintenance. He took up a cloth and began, without being asked, the same patient wiping-down he did every morning, a ritual Wren suspected he found more soothing than he'd ever admit, the alchemical equivalent of a man who couldn't sleep until the dishes were done. His hands, she noticed, the way she noticed everything mechanical about a room without quite meaning to, had gone permanently stained at the knuckles from forty years of residue work — not dangerous staining, just the slow cosmetic toll of a trade that left its mark on a person the way dye left its mark on her mother's, the kind of hands that told you what someone did for a living before they ever opened their mouth.

This was the part of the job nobody asked her about at family dinners. Not the scale-handling — people loved the scale-handling, people's eyes went a little glassy and delighted when she mentioned what was in the cradle, the way they'd go glassy and delighted at a parade, or a particularly good thunderstorm, something enormous and far away and safely behind glass. What nobody asked about was the ledger. The humidity logs. The eleven separate measurements she took before she let herself touch anything that had once belonged to something with a heartbeat older than the Riverbed Council itself, the comparison against last month's readings, the long, satisfying, unglamorous arithmetic of doing a dangerous thing carefully enough that it stopped, for all practical purposes, being dangerous.

She loved the ledger best of anything in the building. She had never told anyone that, because saying it out loud made it sound like a joke at her own expense, and she preferred to make those jokes herself, on her own schedule, rather than have them handed to her.

It was not, in fact, only the ledger. There was a whole private inventory of things in this workshop Wren loved in a manner she'd never have admitted to anyone over the age of nine: the particular click the brass cradle's latch made when it seated correctly, distinct from the slightly wronger click it made when it didn't, a difference of perhaps a sixteenth of a second that she could nonetheless hear from across the room with her back turned; the smell of the quieting cloths just after they'd been laundered and before they'd absorbed their first real feeling, clean and a little like rain; the specific, smug satisfaction of a humidity reading that landed exactly where she'd predicted it would the night before, as though the air itself had agreed to be reasonable on her behalf. She had once tried to explain this to Mari, years ago, over a shared plate of bread and soft cheese at their mother's table, and Mari had listened with her whole attentive face, the way Mari listened to everyone, and said, *that's lovely, Wren, truly*, in a tone that made it unmistakably clear she had understood the words and missed the thing underneath them by a country mile, and Wren had let the subject drop rather than try again, because trying again would have meant admitting how much she'd wanted, just once, to be understood instead of indulged.

"Letter came for the guild," Corvin said, in the particular voice he used for things he'd already decided weren't very important, which Wren had learned over four years to listen to closely, because it was also the voice he used for things he'd decided not to be alarmed by on purpose. He'd had it folded into his apron pocket the whole time, she realized, which meant he'd been carrying it around the workshop for at least an hour deciding when to mention it, the way he carried anything he'd already half-decided not to make a fuss over. "Lowmarket ward's asking around for spare hands. Fever's going through the lower blocks again."

"Again? It was just there in spring."

"It's always there in spring. This is summer." He set the letter down on her workbench without quite handing it to her, which was its own kind of sentence. The paper was cheap stock, the ink a little blotted at the edges where whoever had written it had been working fast, and the guild's own intake stamp sat crooked in the corner, as if even the clerk processing it had been in a hurry. "They're not asking us for healers, obviously. Asking around for material-side help — quieting work, mostly, clearing residue out of the sickrooms so the healers don't have three jobs going at once. I told them we'd send someone next week once the Hallorin order's out the door."

"Mari's down there," Wren said, not really a question. Mari was always down there. Mari had been down there, on and off, for three weeks, which Wren knew because their mother mentioned it at every single dinner in the specific tone of a woman trying very hard not to sound worried and succeeding at about sixty percent.

"Mari's down there," Corvin agreed. "Mari's good at her job. You're good at yours. Neither of those is the same job, and you've got a Hallorin order due Thursday same as the inventory."

"I know what I've got due Thursday."

"I know you know. I'm reminding myself, mostly. You get a look."

"I don't get a look."

"You get a look," Corvin said, with the weary fondness of a man who had watched this particular look cross this particular face for four years and had never once successfully talked her out of whatever it preceded, "like you're doing arithmetic on a problem that isn't yours to solve."

"It's not a problem. It's my sister having a busy week. People have busy weeks. You had a busy week in Brindle, nobody accused you of needing rescuing."

"Nobody in Brindle was foolish enough to think I needed rescuing. Different matter entirely." He picked the letter back up, folded it along its original crease with more care than the cheap paper deserved, and set it on the shelf with the other things waiting for next week, which was where, Wren had noticed, things tended to go when Corvin had decided he didn't want to look at them again until he absolutely had to — a shelf that currently held, by her last informal count, two unanswered guild bulletins, an invoice from the tin merchant on Caul Street he'd been disputing since spring, and a letter from his own niece in Brindle that had sat there long enough to gather a faint film of workshop dust. "Go finish your eleven measurements. Bartholomew's not going to log himself, whatever you tell people at parties."

"I keep telling you, I don't go to parties, and even if I did, nobody at a party wants to hear about Bartholomew's pressure readings. I learned that the hard way. There was an incident. A man named Fenwick. We don't discuss it."

"You've never once told me about the incident."

"That's correct," Wren said, with great dignity, "and it's going to stay that way."

"I don't go to parties."

"No," Corvin said, already most of the way out the door, in a voice with something almost gentle buried in the dryness of it, "you don't, do you."

---

The bell over the front door went a half hour later, while Wren was three measurements deep and elbow-deep in the Hallorin order besides — a merchant family wanted the wyrm-bone inlay on an old dining table re-stabilized, which meant six hours of patient, repetitive, deeply satisfying work convincing three centuries of dead king's spine that it did not, in fact, need to remember anything about the river battle it had allegedly witnessed, every time someone's elbow grazed it during supper. The inlay panel currently lay across two trestles in the back room, propped at the angle she'd worked out over the past two days minimized strain on the original glue joins, and she'd just gotten her hands properly settled into the rhythm of it — open, hold, listen for the faint, old shudder of remembered cannon-fire, soothe it back down to something that would behave itself through a dinner party — when the bell cut through the quiet like someone dropping a tray. She went out front wiping her hands on her apron, expecting a courier.

It was Mrs. Tarn instead, which meant the visit would take four times as long and involve no actual business for at least the first ten minutes. Mrs. Tarn had on her good shawl, the green one she wore for errands she'd decided were occasions, and she brought with her, the way she always did, a small private weather system of lavender water and peppermint drops that arrived a full two seconds before she did and lingered a full two minutes after she left.

"Wren, dear, I won't keep you," Mrs. Tarn said, by way of keeping her, settling onto the customer stool with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had outlived two husbands and most of her own urgency. She set a small cloth bundle on the counter and unwrapped it with the ceremony of someone unveiling something far more important than a pocket watch, smoothing the cloth flat first, then folding back each corner in turn, an entire small liturgy performed for an audience of one mildly impatient alchemist. "It's Aldous's. It's started running fast again. Or slow. I can never remember which one means what, but it's wrong, and it's been wrong since the anniversary, and I thought, well, who do I know who fixes things that have gone wrong from being held too much."

"That's a very accurate description of my entire job," Wren said, drawing the watch toward her. It was a heavy, old-fashioned piece, brass gone soft and dark at the edges from forty years of being carried in one coat pocket and worried at with one thumb, the crystal scratched in a small fan pattern just off-center where a man's thumbnail had clearly traveled the same half-inch arc ten thousand times without ever once making it all the way around. She turned it over. The case-back had a hinge gone slightly loose, the kind of looseness that came only from being opened and shut, opened and shut, far more times than checking the hour required. When she opened her hand over it — not touching, not yet, just *opening*, the small, careful, professional version of the thing the wyrm scale required in industrial quantities — she felt immediately what the trouble was. It wasn't broken. It was *loud*. Forty years of one man's grief over one dead brother, handled and re-handled into the metal a thousand evenings running, until the watch had gone the way ordinary objects sometimes did when nobody around them knew, or wanted to know, how to put a thing down: saturated with a single feeling, repeated past usefulness, the way a story told too many times stops being a memory and becomes a groove the mind just falls into out of habit.

"It's not broken," Wren said. "It's just full. Things can get full, the same as people, only slower, and usually about one specific thing instead of everything at once. This is — " she turned it gently in her palm, professional, unhurried, the exact blankness she'd spent the morning rehearsing on something considerably larger and considerably less sentimental — "this is mostly missing him. Forty years of missing him, never quite set down anywhere, all stacked up in one small brass object that didn't ask for the job."

Mrs. Tarn's eyes went bright and a little wet, in the way of someone who had been waiting a long time for somebody to say a true thing about her own grief without being asked to perform feeling it back. Her hand, where it rested on the counter, had gone still in a way it hadn't been a moment ago, and Wren watched her swallow once before she spoke again, the small private effort of a woman deciding not to cry in a shop on a weekday morning. "Can you take it out?"

"I can take *some* of it out. I wouldn't take all of it, even if I could — you'd be left with a watch that told time and nothing else, and I don't think that's actually what you want from it." Wren reached for the quieting cloth, the dull grey one that had absorbed more ordinary household sorrow than she liked to think about, soft with washing and a little thin at one corner where it had finally started to wear through, and opened toward the watch with the small, deliberate trickle she used for exactly this — not a flood, not even a stream, just a thread, the width of a single held breath. She kept her own face carefully arranged into nothing in particular while she worked, the same trained blankness she'd been using on Bartholomew an hour before, because a customer crying over a quieted watch was one thing and a customer crying because her alchemist had visibly *felt something* about it was an entirely different, much longer conversation. The old grief came up off the brass and into the cloth, patient and unhurried, the way water finds the lowest point in a floor without being told to. It took perhaps twenty seconds, though it always felt to outsiders like longer, and Wren had learned not to fill the silence with reassurances she didn't yet know she could keep. The watch, when she set it back down, sat differently in its own weight, the way a room sits differently once someone's finally opened a window in it. "There. Still missing him. Just — missing him at a volume you can hear other things over now."

"You're as good as your sister," Mrs. Tarn said, with the warm, careless generosity of someone paying what she believed was the highest compliment available, "only with watches instead of people. It must run in the family, whatever it is. I told my daughter, I said, that whole family's got the gift, the Council ought to give them a street named after them."

"It's not quite the same gift," Wren said, and her voice came out exactly as even as it had a second ago, which took a small, specific effort she did not let show on her face. "Mari opens toward people. I open toward — " she gestured at the watch, the workbench, the whole dead-quiet, brass-and-glass room behind her — "this. It's a different specialization. Most people don't actually need the difference explained to them, they just need their watch to stop being loud."

"Well, it's all the same kindness underneath, isn't it," Mrs. Tarn said, gathering the watch back into its cloth with the same careful folding she'd used to unwrap it, entirely satisfied with her own generosity and utterly unaware of having said anything that required a response, which was, Wren reflected, the precise and infuriating shape of most compliments she received about her sister. *It's all the same kindness underneath.* It was not, in fact, the same kindness. It was not even the same instrument. Mari's gift got written up in Council bulletins, got her name said at dinner parties Wren was not invited to, by people who had never met either of them, in the specific reverent tone reserved for the genuinely beloved. Wren's gift got handed forty-year-old watches across a counter by women who meant every word kindly and would never, not once, think to ask whether being told *you're as good as your sister, only smaller* might land on the smaller half of that sentence with any particular weight.

She thought, not for the first time, that she could compile an entire second ledger of these moments if she let herself — date, time, exact phrasing, name of offender, degree of intended kindness (usually total) — and that the existence of such a ledger, even hypothetically, even only in her own head, would tell anyone who read it more about her than she had any interest in being known for. So she didn't keep it. She had decided, years ago, that the discipline of *not* keeping that particular ledger was itself a kind of measurement, the same species of careful restraint she applied to the cradle, and that this counted, on the days she needed it to count, as a virtue rather than a wound she'd simply declined to dress.

It was a want. She recognized it the instant it rose — recognized the specific, hot, embarrassing shape of it, the same family of feeling as flinching at the scale, *wanting it to be different, wanting it to be smaller, wanting, wanting, wanting* — and she did to it exactly what four years of professional discipline had taught her to do to any want she hadn't invited: she gave it nothing to hold on to. She thought about the quieting cloth's absorption rate, which dropped by roughly a third once a cloth had taken on its first hundred uses, a fact nobody but her had ever bothered to chart. She thought about whether she'd entered today's date correctly on the Hallorin paperwork, and then, because that thought solved nothing, she thought about the Hallorin paperwork's actual ink, which had been mixed slightly too thin this month and was already starting to feather at the edges of her letters, an annoyance she made a private note to raise with the supplier. The want subsided, the way it always did when she starved it on purpose, and she was aware, distantly, the way you're aware of a held breath only once you let it out, that this was a trick she had gotten so good at that she had stopped being able to tell, most days, whether the feeling underneath it had actually gone away or had just learned, like the watch, like the scale, to go quiet instead.

"That'll be four coppers for the cloth and the time," she said. "Tell Aldous's nephew the inscription on the back's worn thin enough I'd get it re-cut this year or not at all. Another winter of that thumb and you won't be able to read it at all, and then what's the watch even for."

"Oh, he'll want to hear that from you and not me, he never listens to me about Aldous's things, thinks I'll have him melt it all down for the silver given half a chance." Mrs. Tarn gathered her shawl a little closer, though the day outside was warm enough that the gesture seemed more habit than necessity. "You know, you ought to come round for supper sometime. My niece is about your age. Lovely girl. No interest in dead kings' spines whatsoever, which I think might do you some good."

"I'll think about it," Wren said, in the tone she used for invitations she had no intention of accepting and no wish to be rude about, a tone she had practiced enough times that it had stopped costing her anything to produce.

"You're a treasure," Mrs. Tarn said, paying, patting Wren's hand with the particular weightless affection of someone who would have been horrified to learn she'd just been, in the smallest possible way, unkind, and let herself out into the afternoon, bell jangling cheerfully behind her, the lavender-and-peppermint weather system following her out the door a half-second behind, leaving Wren alone with the dull grey cloth and four coppers and a feeling she had already, mostly, successfully put away.

She swept the coppers into the till, recorded the transaction in the day-book in the small precise hand that made every entry look identical regardless of what had actually happened during it, and went back to the Hallorin table. The wyrm-bone inlay, at least, had no opinion whatsoever about which Wickern sister it preferred. It only wanted, as far as Wren could tell after four years of close professional acquaintance, to stop remembering a battle nobody currently living had fought, and that, at least, she knew exactly how to give it.

---

She did finish the eleven measurements, eventually, after the table and the inventory and a second, much shorter, much less talkative customer who wanted a charm-cracked teakettle quieted and didn't stay for conversation, which Wren appreciated more than she let herself say out loud. The teakettle had been an easy job, the alchemical equivalent of clearing your throat — somebody's mother-in-law's irritation at a ruined batch of stew, no deeper than a single bad afternoon, gone in under a minute with barely a thread of effort — and Wren had found herself almost disappointed by how little of her it required, the way a runner might feel cheated by too short a route.

The light by then had shifted the particular two notches toward afternoon-gold that told her, without needing to check the clock, that she had perhaps three good working hours left before the angle through the high window stopped being useful for fine work and she'd have to light the lamps, which she preferred not to do before she had to, both because oil cost money and because lamp-light made the gauge readings harder to trust. She finished the measurements carefully, in order, the way she finished everything — pressure, then temperature, then the faint resonance hum she checked by holding two fingers a careful inch above the scale's surface and counting how long it took her own pulse to stop trying to match it, a homemade test no guild manual had ever sanctioned and that she trusted more than half the things the manuals did sanction — and she let herself, for one indulgent quarter of an hour at the very end of the session, do the thing she actually loved best, which was not the danger and not even really the craft of it but the *theory* — the slow, patient, only-slightly-respectable habit of sitting with Bartholomew's resonance signature and her grandmother's old journal open beside it, trying to work out, scale by scale, year by year, what exactly three centuries of accumulated residue actually *did* to the underlying structure of a material, whether it built up the way silt built up in a slow river bend or whether it was something stranger, something closer to memory than sediment.

Her grandmother had kept the journal for forty years before Wren was born, in a hand that started out cramped and apologetic in the first volume and ended, six volumes later, in the confident, slashing scrawl of a woman who had stopped expecting anyone to read it over her shoulder and disapprove. The volumes themselves had a smell Wren had never encountered anywhere else, a dry, sweetish must that she associated so completely with this exact quarter-hour of her day that she sometimes caught herself, on bad weeks, opening the cover just to breathe it in before she'd read a single line. The pages of the fourth volume in particular had gone soft and a little translucent at the corners, worn thin by a thumb — her grandmother's, then her own, two sets of hands fifty years apart wearing the same groove into the same paper without either of them planning to. *Residue is not dirt,* her grandmother had written, in the fourth volume, in the middle of an otherwise unremarkable entry about cataloguing a dead war-wyrm's claw sheaths for the Court archive. *Dirt accumulates indifferently. Residue accumulates toward something — every saturated object I have ever measured carries more of one feeling than any other, never an even mix, as though the material itself were being slowly, patiently taught what it was for.* Wren had read that line more times than she would ever admit to Master Ashe, who considered the theory work an indulgence at best and a distraction from billable hours at worst, and every time she read it she felt the specific, private thrill of standing very close to a door nobody else in her field had so much as noticed was a door.

She had tried, twice, to explain the thrill of it to Corvin directly, in her second year and again in her third, and both times he had listened with real attention — he always listened with real attention, that was one of the things she trusted about him — and both times had said some version of *that's a fine thing to wonder about on your own clock*, which was not a no, exactly, but was also not the door swinging open the way she'd hoped. She had stopped asking. She had not stopped wondering. There was, she thought, a meaningful difference between giving up on a question and simply declining to keep asking other people's permission to keep asking it.

Nobody had ever paid her to think about this. Nobody at the guild considered it billable hours. Master Ashe tolerated it the way you tolerated a hobby in an employee who otherwise showed up on time and never once burned the workshop down, and her mother had stopped asking about it around the third year, in the specific way people stopped asking about things they'd quietly decided were a phase. Wren did not consider it a phase. Wren considered it the single most interesting unanswered question in her field, possibly in any field, and she thought about it the way other people her age apparently thought about romance, which was to say: constantly, privately, and with an enthusiasm she had learned, young, to keep to herself in mixed company.

She had a system for the comparison work, the way she had a system for everything that mattered to her. Two columns, ruled by hand because she didn't trust the printed ledgers the stationer sold to keep their margins consistent across two hundred pages: resonance density on the left, in her own modified notation, and on the right, in smaller script, anything she could establish about the scale's documented history — age, prior ownership where it existed, the specific feeling-family it ran hottest toward, when she could determine one. Bartholomew's column ran four pages already, more than any other scale she'd had access to, and she still didn't have an answer to the question that mattered most, which was *why this one and not that one*, why two scales of near-identical age and provenance could carry such wildly different ratios of grief to anger to something she still didn't have a word for, a fourth thing that showed up sometimes in the oldest stock and that her grandmother's journal called, unhelpfully, only *the fourth one*, as if she too had run out of better language for it.

She was three pages into a comparison of scale-density notation — Bartholomew's signature against a smaller, younger scale from two years back, the numbers almost but not quite resolving into a pattern she could feel circling somewhere just behind her own understanding, close enough to taste and not nearly close enough to name, the specific maddening sensation of a word sitting just behind your teeth — when someone knocked on the workshop door hard enough to rattle the latch, which nobody on Lantern Street ever did, because everyone on Lantern Street knew what was in the back room and had a healthy, learned respect for not startling the person standing closest to it.

"It's open," she called, already closing the journal, already half irritated at whoever was about to make her lose her place in a line of reasoning she'd been four months building. She marked her page first — an old habit, a torn corner of paper kept for exactly this purpose, because losing a place in the journal felt, to her, like a genuine small grief, the kind that deserved at least a token gesture of respect before she let the rest of the day interrupt it.

The boy who came in was maybe fourteen, soaked through despite the dry afternoon — sweat, she registered, not rain, his shirt stuck flat to his shoulder blades and his hair plastered dark against his forehead — and breathing like he'd run the whole way from the river ward without stopping once, which, judging by the state of him, he had. He had to brace one hand against the doorframe to stay upright, and his chest was working so hard Wren could see it from across the room, a visible, urgent rise and fall that had nothing professional or measured about it at all, the opposite of every controlled breath she'd spent the morning practicing.

"Wickern," he managed. "You're — you're Wren Wickern. Healer Mari's sister."

Something in Wren's chest did a small, specific, unwelcome thing, a kind of pre-emptive closing she recognized and despised in herself and had never once managed to stop in time — the same family of feeling, she would think much later, as Mrs. Tarn's watch and Mrs. Tarn's compliment and four years of being the second name in every sentence that started with her own surname, except that this time the feeling arrived a full half-second before the fear did, and was gone by the time the fear caught up, replaced entirely and completely and without a trace of itself remaining. The pen was still in her hand. She set it down with more deliberate care than the gesture required, the way her hands always reached for something small and orderly to do whenever the rest of her had nothing useful to offer. "I am."

"They sent me up from Lowmarket," the boy said, and here, for just a second, his nerve seemed to fail him, the way nerve does right before the worst part of a sentence. "She collapsed. Healer Mari. About an hour ago, in the ward, in front of — in front of everybody. They got her stable, they said to tell you stable, they said not to alarm anyone, but they also said —"

"Said what," Wren said, very quietly, and put down the pen she hadn't noticed she was still holding.

"They said come now," the boy said. "Not next week. Now."

The workshop, for one suspended second, was exactly as it had been thirty seconds before — Bartholomew settled and dead-still in his cradle, the ledger open to a careful column of humidity readings, her grandmother's journal lying open to a page about silt and memory and three hundred years of accumulated weight, four coppers still sitting unswept on the counter where Mrs. Tarn had left them — and then it was not the same room at all, because Wren was already moving, already past the boy, already out the door into the bright unbothered afternoon, leaving the eleven measurements and the theory and the one perfectly reasonable amount of feeling she had spent four careful years learning how to hold sitting open on the bench behind her, completely, uselessly unattended.
