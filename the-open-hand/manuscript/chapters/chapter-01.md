# Chapter One — A Perfectly Reasonable Amount of Feeling

The trick to handling a wyrm scale that was still faintly, technically alive was to want it less than it wanted to be left alone, and Wren had spent four years getting genuinely, professionally bad at wanting things.

"You're doing the breathing again," said Corvin, from the doorway, in the tone of a man who had walked in on this exact scene roughly four hundred times and had decided, around the hundredth, that commenting on it was cheaper than being surprised by it.

"I'm not doing anything," Wren said, not moving, because the scale was balanced on the brass cradle in front of her with the particular stillness of something that knew it was being watched and resented it, and the last apprentice who'd lost focus at this stage had needed three days of bed rest and a humiliating letter to his mother. "I am holding very still and thinking about absolutely nothing, which you have told me, personally, on multiple occasions, is the correct technique."

"I've told you to want it *less*. I have never told you to want *nothing*. Wanting nothing is how Aldric ended up in bed for a week wondering why his own boots felt like someone else's."

"Aldric wanted his lunch. That's different. That's an active want. I am a calm, blank, uninteresting person to this scale right now."

"You are the least blank person I have ever met."

"I'm blank *at it*," Wren said. "It's a very specific, professional blankness. You wouldn't understand, you're a Tuesday-blank person, I'm a — " She lost the thread of the sentence because the scale, at that exact moment, did the thing it did roughly once every dozen sessions, which was to remember, briefly and without malice, that it had once been part of something enormous, and the memory came off it like heat off a stone three hours after the sun went down. Not dangerous. Not even close to dangerous, not at this distance, not with the cradle's iron baffles doing their patient unglamorous job. Just enormous, and old, and entirely uninterested in the fact that a twenty-three-year-old alchemist's journeywoman was, at this exact moment, the only thing standing between it and the open air of Confluence's most respectable apothecary district.

Wren did not flinch. She had trained herself, with real and specific discipline, not to flinch at this part, because flinching was a kind of wanting too — wanting it to stop, wanting it to be smaller, wanting, wanting, wanting, and the whole maddening genius of the Permeance was that wanting was the same instrument as power, so a flinch wasn't a failure of nerve. It was a *signal*, sent straight down whatever invisible line connected her open hand to the thing in the cradle, and the signal said *here, this part of me is soft, lean on this part,* and the scale, being very old and very saturated and not even slightly malicious about it, would lean.

So she didn't flinch. She thought, instead, with the specific deliberate dullness of long practice, about the quarterly inventory report she owed the guild by Thursday, and about whether she had in fact returned Petra's crucible tongs, and about a really quite boring argument she'd had with herself last week regarding optimal storage humidity for unstabilized residue, and the heat-off-a-stone feeling subsided, the way it always did when she gave it nothing to hold on to, and the scale went back to being, for all practical purposes, a very pretty, very valuable, extremely dead-looking piece of keratin sitting in a brass cradle in a workshop on Lantern Street.

"There," she said, and let her shoulders down from somewhere up near her ears. "Boring. I was so boring just now you could've sold tickets."

"You undersell yourself," Corvin said, coming the rest of the way into the room, which from him was practically a hug. He was sixty-one years old, built like a man who had spent thirty of those years lifting things that did not want to be lifted, and he had the kind of face that had apparently decided sometime in his forties that smiling was a young man's extravagance it could no longer afford. He looked at the scale the way other men looked at racehorses or, Wren suspected privately, the way some men in better families looked at daughters who'd tested into the Court. "Bartholomew behaving?"

"Bartholomew is always behaving. Bartholomew has never once let me down. I would like that noted formally, in case anyone ever again suggests that naming a piece of evidence is unprofessional."

"I have never suggested that."

"You suggested it in front of the whole guild assembly in Brindle."

"I suggested that naming a scale after a dead king was unprofessional. The naming itself I have no opinion on."

"He looks like a Bartholomew," Wren said, with the absolute conviction of someone who had clearly had this argument enough times to have worn smooth the part of her brain that questioned it, and bent to mark the cradle's pressure gauge before she let herself go back to the ledger, because there was an order to this, and the order mattered more than the argument did. First the gauge. Then the temperature strip taped along the cradle's inner lip, which had gone the pale, unremarkable green that meant nothing at all was wrong, which was the single most satisfying color in her entire professional life. Then the ledger.

"You photograph the gauge readings now," Corvin said, watching her do it, in the tone of someone noting a development he had opinions about but had decided, on balance, not to share.

"I sketch them. There's a difference. A photograph is somebody else's eye. A sketch is mine, which means if I get it wrong, I get it wrong in a way I can retrace later and learn something from. You taught me that, actually, in my second month, you just don't remember because you say true things constantly and at low volume and never seem to notice when one of them lands."

"I notice," Corvin said. "I just don't make a parade of it."

"This was a parade. I made you a parade just now, with my mouth, and you stood there and let it happen, which is the most flattering thing you've done all week."

This was the part of the job nobody asked her about at family dinners. Not the scale-handling — people loved the scale-handling, people's eyes went a little glassy and delighted when she mentioned what was in the cradle, the way they'd go glassy and delighted at a parade, or a particularly good thunderstorm, something enormous and far away and safely behind glass. What nobody asked about was the ledger. The humidity logs. The eleven separate measurements she took before she let herself touch anything that had once belonged to something with a heartbeat older than the Riverbed Council itself, the comparison against last month's readings, the long, satisfying, unglamorous arithmetic of doing a dangerous thing carefully enough that it stopped, for all practical purposes, being dangerous.

She loved the ledger best of anything in the building. She had never told anyone that, because saying it out loud made it sound like a joke at her own expense, and she preferred to make those jokes herself, on her own schedule, rather than have them handed to her.

"Letter came for the guild," Corvin said, in the particular voice he used for things he'd already decided weren't very important, which Wren had learned over four years to listen to closely, because it was also the voice he used for things he'd decided not to be alarmed by on purpose. "Lowmarket ward's asking around for spare hands. Fever's going through the lower blocks again."

"Again? It was just there in spring."

"It's always there in spring. This is summer." He set the letter down on her workbench without quite handing it to her, which was its own kind of sentence. "They're not asking us for healers, obviously. Asking around for material-side help — quieting work, mostly, clearing residue out of the sickrooms so the healers don't have three jobs going at once. I told them we'd send someone next week once the Hallorin order's out the door."

"Mari's down there," Wren said, not really a question. Mari was always down there. Mari had been down there, on and off, for three weeks, which Wren knew because their mother mentioned it at every single dinner in the specific tone of a woman trying very hard not to sound worried and succeeding at about sixty percent.

"Mari's down there," Corvin agreed. "Mari's good at her job. You're good at yours. Neither of those is the same job, and you've got a Hallorin order due Thursday same as the inventory."

"I know what I've got due Thursday."

"I know you know. I'm reminding myself, mostly. You get a look."

"I don't get a look."

"You get a look," Corvin said, with the weary fondness of a man who had watched this particular look cross this particular face for four years and had never once successfully talked her out of whatever it preceded, "like you're doing arithmetic on a problem that isn't yours to solve."

"It's not a problem. It's my sister having a busy week. People have busy weeks. You had a busy week in Brindle, nobody accused you of needing rescuing."

"Nobody in Brindle was foolish enough to think I needed rescuing. Different matter entirely." He picked the letter back up, folded it, and set it on the shelf with the other things waiting for next week, which was where, Wren had noticed, things tended to go when Corvin had decided he didn't want to look at them again until he absolutely had to. "Go finish your eleven measurements. Bartholomew's not going to log himself, whatever you tell people at parties."

"I don't go to parties."

"No," Corvin said, already most of the way out the door, in a voice with something almost gentle buried in the dryness of it, "you don't, do you."

---

The bell over the front door went a half hour later, while Wren was three measurements deep and elbow-deep in the Hallorin order besides — a merchant family wanted the wyrm-bone inlay on an old dining table re-stabilized, which meant six hours of patient, repetitive, deeply satisfying work convincing three centuries of dead king's spine that it did not, in fact, need to remember anything about the river battle it had allegedly witnessed, every time someone's elbow grazed it during supper — and she went out front wiping her hands on her apron, expecting a courier.

It was Mrs. Tarn instead, which meant the visit would take four times as long and involve no actual business for at least the first ten minutes.

"Wren, dear, I won't keep you," Mrs. Tarn said, by way of keeping her, settling onto the customer stool with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had outlived two husbands and most of her own urgency. She set a small cloth bundle on the counter and unwrapped it with the ceremony of someone unveiling something far more important than a pocket watch. "It's Aldous's. It's started running fast again. Or slow. I can never remember which one means what, but it's wrong, and it's been wrong since the anniversary, and I thought, well, who do I know who fixes things that have gone wrong from being held too much."

"That's a very accurate description of my entire job," Wren said, drawing the watch toward her. It was a heavy, old-fashioned piece, brass gone soft and dark at the edges from forty years of being carried in one coat pocket and worried at with one thumb, and when she opened her hand over it — not touching, not yet, just *opening*, the small, careful, professional version of the thing the wyrm scale required in industrial quantities — she felt immediately what the trouble was. It wasn't broken. It was *loud*. Forty years of one man's grief over one dead brother, handled and re-handled into the metal a thousand evenings running, until the watch had gone the way ordinary objects sometimes did when nobody around them knew, or wanted to know, how to put a thing down: saturated with a single feeling, repeated past usefulness, the way a story told too many times stops being a memory and becomes a groove the mind just falls into out of habit.

"It's not broken," Wren said. "It's just full. Things can get full, the same as people, only slower, and usually about one specific thing instead of everything at once. This is — " she turned it gently in her palm, professional, unhurried, the exact blankness she'd spent the morning rehearsing on something considerably larger and considerably less sentimental — "this is mostly missing him. Forty years of missing him, never quite set down anywhere, all stacked up in one small brass object that didn't ask for the job."

Mrs. Tarn's eyes went bright and a little wet, in the way of someone who had been waiting a long time for somebody to say a true thing about her own grief without being asked to perform feeling it back. "Can you take it out?"

"I can take *some* of it out. I wouldn't take all of it, even if I could — you'd be left with a watch that told time and nothing else, and I don't think that's actually what you want from it." Wren reached for the quieting cloth, the dull grey one that had absorbed more ordinary household sorrow than she liked to think about, opened toward the watch with the small, deliberate trickle she used for exactly this — not a flood, not even a stream, just a thread, the width of a single held breath — and felt the old grief come up off the brass and into the cloth, patient and unhurried, the way water finds the lowest point in a floor without being told to. The watch, when she set it back down, sat differently in its own weight, the way a room sits differently once someone's finally opened a window in it. "There. Still missing him. Just — missing him at a volume you can hear other things over now."

"You're as good as your sister," Mrs. Tarn said, with the warm, careless generosity of someone paying what she believed was the highest compliment available, "only with watches instead of people. It must run in the family, whatever it is. I told my daughter, I said, that whole family's got the gift, the Council ought to give them a street named after them."

"It's not quite the same gift," Wren said, and her voice came out exactly as even as it had a second ago, which took a small, specific effort she did not let show on her face. "Mari opens toward people. I open toward — " she gestured at the watch, the workbench, the whole dead-quiet, brass-and-glass room behind her — "this. It's a different specialization. Most people don't actually need the difference explained to them, they just need their watch to stop being loud."

"Well, it's all the same kindness underneath, isn't it," Mrs. Tarn said, gathering the watch back into its cloth, entirely satisfied with her own generosity and utterly unaware of having said anything that required a response, which was, Wren reflected, the precise and infuriating shape of most compliments she received about her sister. *It's all the same kindness underneath.* It was not, in fact, the same kindness. It was not even the same instrument. Mari's gift got written up in Council bulletins. Wren's gift got handed forty-year-old watches across a counter by women who meant every word kindly and would never, not once, think to ask whether being told *you're as good as your sister, only smaller* might land on the smaller half of that sentence with any particular weight.

It was a want. She recognized it the instant it rose — recognized the specific, hot, embarrassing shape of it, the same family of feeling as flinching at the scale, *wanting it to be different, wanting it to be smaller, wanting, wanting, wanting* — and she did to it exactly what four years of professional discipline had taught her to do to any want she hadn't invited: she gave it nothing to hold on to. She thought about the quieting cloth's absorption rate. She thought about whether she'd entered today's date correctly on the Hallorin paperwork. The want subsided, the way it always did when she starved it on purpose, and she was aware, distantly, the way you're aware of a held breath only once you let it out, that this was a trick she had gotten so good at that she had stopped being able to tell, most days, whether the feeling underneath it had actually gone away or had just learned, like the watch, like the scale, to go quiet instead.

"That'll be four coppers for the cloth and the time," she said. "Tell Aldous's nephew the inscription on the back's worn thin enough I'd get it re-cut this year or not at all."

"You're a treasure," Mrs. Tarn said, paying, patting Wren's hand with the particular weightless affection of someone who would have been horrified to learn she'd just been, in the smallest possible way, unkind, and let herself out into the afternoon, bell jangling cheerfully behind her, leaving Wren alone with the dull grey cloth and four coppers and a feeling she had already, mostly, successfully put away.

She went back to the Hallorin table. The wyrm-bone inlay, at least, had no opinion whatsoever about which Wickern sister it preferred.

---

She did finish the eleven measurements, eventually, after the table and the inventory and a second, much shorter, much less talkative customer who wanted a charm-cracked teakettle quieted and didn't stay for conversation, which Wren appreciated more than she let herself say out loud. She finished the measurements carefully, in order, the way she finished everything, and she let herself, for one indulgent quarter of an hour at the very end of the session, do the thing she actually loved best, which was not the danger and not even really the craft of it but the *theory* — the slow, patient, only-slightly-respectable habit of sitting with Bartholomew's resonance signature and her grandmother's old journal open beside it, trying to work out, scale by scale, year by year, what exactly three centuries of accumulated residue actually *did* to the underlying structure of a material, whether it built up the way silt built up in a slow river bend or whether it was something stranger, something closer to memory than sediment.

Her grandmother had kept the journal for forty years before Wren was born, in a hand that started out cramped and apologetic in the first volume and ended, six volumes later, in the confident, slashing scrawl of a woman who had stopped expecting anyone to read it over her shoulder and disapprove. *Residue is not dirt,* her grandmother had written, in the fourth volume, in the middle of an otherwise unremarkable entry about cataloguing a dead war-wyrm's claw sheaths for the Court archive. *Dirt accumulates indifferently. Residue accumulates toward something — every saturated object I have ever measured carries more of one feeling than any other, never an even mix, as though the material itself were being slowly, patiently taught what it was for.* Wren had read that line more times than she would ever admit to Master Ashe, who considered the theory work an indulgence at best and a distraction from billable hours at worst, and every time she read it she felt the specific, private thrill of standing very close to a door nobody else in her field had so much as noticed was a door.

Nobody had ever paid her to think about this. Nobody at the guild considered it billable hours. Master Ashe tolerated it the way you tolerated a hobby in an employee who otherwise showed up on time and never once burned the workshop down, and her mother had stopped asking about it around the third year, in the specific way people stopped asking about things they'd quietly decided were a phase. Wren did not consider it a phase. Wren considered it the single most interesting unanswered question in her field, possibly in any field, and she thought about it the way other people her age apparently thought about romance, which was to say: constantly, privately, and with an enthusiasm she had learned, young, to keep to herself in mixed company.

She was three pages into a comparison of scale-density notation — Bartholomew's signature against a smaller, younger scale from two years back, the numbers almost but not quite resolving into a pattern she could feel circling somewhere just behind her own understanding, close enough to taste and not nearly close enough to name — when someone knocked on the workshop door hard enough to rattle the latch, which nobody on Lantern Street ever did, because everyone on Lantern Street knew what was in the back room and had a healthy, learned respect for not startling the person standing closest to it.

"It's open," she called, already closing the journal, already half irritated at whoever was about to make her lose her place in a line of reasoning she'd been four months building.

The boy who came in was maybe fourteen, soaked through despite the dry afternoon, and breathing like he'd run the whole way from the river ward without stopping once, which, judging by the state of him, he had.

"Wickern," he managed. "You're — you're Wren Wickern. Healer Mari's sister."

Something in Wren's chest did a small, specific, unwelcome thing, a kind of pre-emptive closing she recognized and despised in herself and had never once managed to stop in time — the same family of feeling, she would think much later, as Mrs. Tarn's watch and Mrs. Tarn's compliment and four years of being the second name in every sentence that started with her own surname, except that this time the feeling arrived a full half-second before the fear did, and was gone by the time the fear caught up, replaced entirely and completely and without a trace of itself remaining. "I am."

"They sent me up from Lowmarket," the boy said, and here, for just a second, his nerve seemed to fail him, the way nerve does right before the worst part of a sentence. "She collapsed. Healer Mari. About an hour ago, in the ward, in front of — in front of everybody. They got her stable, they said to tell you stable, they said not to alarm anyone, but they also said —"

"Said what," Wren said, very quietly, and put down the pen she hadn't noticed she was still holding.

"They said come now," the boy said. "Not next week. Now."

The workshop, for one suspended second, was exactly as it had been thirty seconds before — Bartholomew settled and dead-still in his cradle, the ledger open to a careful column of humidity readings, her grandmother's journal lying open to a page about silt and memory and three hundred years of accumulated weight, four coppers still sitting unswept on the counter where Mrs. Tarn had left them — and then it was not the same room at all, because Wren was already moving, already past the boy, already out the door into the bright unbothered afternoon, leaving the eleven measurements and the theory and the one perfectly reasonable amount of feeling she had spent four careful years learning how to hold sitting open on the bench behind her, completely, uselessly unattended.
