# Chapter One — A Perfectly Reasonable Amount of Feeling

The trick to handling a wyrm scale that was still faintly, technically alive was to want it less than it wanted to be left alone, and Wren had spent four years getting genuinely, professionally bad at wanting things.

"You're doing the breathing again," said Corvin, from the doorway, in the tone of a man who had walked in on this exact scene roughly four hundred times and had decided, around the hundredth, that commenting on it was cheaper than being surprised by it.

"I'm not doing anything," Wren said, not moving, because the scale was balanced on the brass cradle in front of her with the particular stillness of something that knew it was being watched and resented it, and the last apprentice who'd lost focus at this stage had needed three days of bed rest and a humiliating letter to his mother. "I am holding very still and thinking about absolutely nothing, which you have told me, personally, on multiple occasions, is the correct technique."

"I've told you to want it *less*. I have never told you to want *nothing*. Wanting nothing is how Aldric ended up in bed for a week wondering why his own boots felt like someone else's."

"Aldric wanted his lunch. That's different. That's an active want. I am a calm, blank, uninteresting person to this scale right now."

"You are the least blank person I have ever met."

"I'm blank *at it*," Wren said. "It's a very specific, professional blankness. You wouldn't understand, you're a Tuesday-blank person, I'm a — " She lost the thread of the sentence because the scale, at that exact moment, did the thing it did roughly once every dozen sessions, which was to remember, briefly and without malice, that it had once been part of something enormous, and the memory came off it like heat off a stone three hours after the sun went down. Not dangerous. Not even close to dangerous, not at this distance, not with the cradle's iron baffles doing their patient unglamorous job. Just enormous, and old, and entirely uninterested in the fact that a twenty-three-year-old alchemist's journeywoman was, at this exact moment, the only thing standing between it and the open air of Confluence's most respectable apothecary district.

Wren did not flinch. She had trained herself, with real and specific discipline, not to flinch at this part, because flinching was a kind of wanting too — wanting it to stop, wanting it to be smaller, wanting, wanting, wanting, and the whole maddening genius of the Permeance was that wanting was the same instrument as power, so a flinch wasn't a failure of nerve. It was a *signal*, sent straight down whatever invisible line connected her open hand to the thing in the cradle, and the signal said *here, this part of me is soft, lean on this part,* and the scale, being very old and very saturated and not even slightly malicious about it, would lean.

So she didn't flinch. She thought, instead, with the specific deliberate dullness of long practice, about the quarterly inventory report she owed the guild by Thursday, and about whether she had in fact returned Petra's crucible tongs, and about a really quite boring argument she'd had with herself last week regarding optimal storage humidity for unstabilized residue, and the heat-off-a-stone feeling subsided, the way it always did when she gave it nothing to hold on to, and the scale went back to being, for all practical purposes, a very pretty, very valuable, extremely dead-looking piece of keratin sitting in a brass cradle in a workshop on Lantern Street.

"There," she said, and let her shoulders down from somewhere up near her ears. "Boring. I was so boring just now you could've sold tickets."

"You undersell yourself," Corvin said, coming the rest of the way into the room, which from him was practically a hug. He was sixty-one years old, built like a man who had spent thirty of those years lifting things that did not want to be lifted, and he had the kind of face that had apparently decided sometime in his forties that smiling was a young man's extravagance it could no longer afford. He looked at the scale the way other men looked at racehorses or, Wren suspected privately, the way some men in better families looked at daughters who'd tested into the Court. "Bartholomew behaving?"

"Bartholomew is always behaving. Bartholomew has never once let me down. I would like that noted formally, in case anyone ever again suggests that naming a piece of evidence is unprofessional."

"I have never suggested that."

"You suggested it in front of the whole guild assembly in Brindle."

"I suggested that naming a scale after a dead king was unprofessional. The naming itself I have no opinion on."

"He looks like a Bartholomew," Wren said, with the absolute conviction of someone who had clearly had this argument enough times to have worn smooth the part of her brain that questioned it, and went back to logging the morning's readings in the ledger, because the readings, unlike the argument, actually mattered.

This was the part of the job nobody asked her about at family dinners. Not the scale-handling — people loved the scale-handling, people's eyes went a little glassy and delighted when she mentioned what was in the cradle, the way they'd go glassy and delighted at a parade, or a particularly good thunderstorm, something enormous and far away and safely behind glass. What nobody asked about was the ledger. The humidity logs. The eleven separate measurements she took before she let herself touch anything that had once belonged to something with a heartbeat older than the Riverbed Council itself, the comparison against last month's readings, the long, satisfying, unglamorous arithmetic of doing a dangerous thing carefully enough that it stopped, for all practical purposes, being dangerous.

She loved the ledger best of anything in the building. She had never told anyone that, because saying it out loud made it sound like a joke at her own expense, and she preferred to make those jokes herself, on her own schedule, rather than have them handed to her.

"Letter came for the guild," Corvin said, in the particular voice he used for things he'd already decided weren't very important, which Wren had learned over four years to listen to closely, because it was also the voice he used for things he'd decided not to be alarmed by on purpose. "Lowmarket ward's asking around for spare hands. Fever's going through the lower blocks again."

"Again? It was just there in spring."

"It's always there in spring. This is summer." He set the letter down on her workbench without quite handing it to her, which was its own kind of sentence. "They're not asking us for healers, obviously. Asking around for material-side help — quieting work, mostly, clearing residue out of the sickrooms so the healers don't have three jobs going at once. I told them we'd send someone next week once the Hallorin order's out the door."

"Mari's down there," Wren said, not really a question. Mari was always down there. Mari had been down there, on and off, for three weeks, which Wren knew because their mother mentioned it at every single dinner in the specific tone of a woman trying very hard not to sound worried and succeeding at about sixty percent.

"Mari's down there," Corvin agreed. "Mari's good at her job. You're good at yours. Neither of those is the same job, and you've got a Hallorin order due Thursday same as the inventory."

"I know what I've got due Thursday."

"I know you know. I'm reminding myself, mostly. You get a look."

"I don't get a look."

"You get a look," Corvin said, with the weary fondness of a man who had watched this particular look cross this particular face for four years and had never once successfully talked her out of whatever it preceded, "like you're doing arithmetic on a problem that isn't yours to solve."

"It's not a problem. It's my sister having a busy week. People have busy weeks. You had a busy week in Brindle, nobody accused you of needing rescuing."

"Nobody in Brindle was foolish enough to think I needed rescuing. Different matter entirely." He picked the letter back up, folded it, and set it on the shelf with the other things waiting for next week, which was where, Wren had noticed, things tended to go when Corvin had decided he didn't want to look at them again until he absolutely had to. "Go finish your eleven measurements. Bartholomew's not going to log himself, whatever you tell people at parties."

"I don't go to parties."

"No," Corvin said, already most of the way out the door, in a voice with something almost gentle buried in the dryness of it, "you don't, do you."

---

She did finish the eleven measurements. She finished them carefully, in order, the way she finished everything, and she let herself, for one indulgent quarter of an hour at the very end of the session, do the thing she actually loved best, which was not the danger and not even really the craft of it but the *theory* — the slow, patient, only-slightly-respectable habit of sitting with Bartholomew's resonance signature and her grandmother's old journal open beside it, trying to work out, scale by scale, year by year, what exactly three centuries of accumulated residue actually *did* to the underlying structure of a material, whether it built up the way silt built up in a slow river bend or whether it was something stranger, something closer to memory than sediment.

Nobody had ever paid her to think about this. Nobody at the guild considered it billable hours. Master Ashe tolerated it the way you tolerated a hobby in an employee who otherwise showed up on time and never once burned the workshop down, and her mother had stopped asking about it around the third year, in the specific way people stopped asking about things they'd quietly decided were a phase. Wren did not consider it a phase. Wren considered it the single most interesting unanswered question in her field, possibly in any field, and she thought about it the way other people her age apparently thought about romance, which was to say: constantly, privately, and with an enthusiasm she had learned, young, to keep to herself in mixed company.

She was three pages into a comparison of scale-density notation when someone knocked on the workshop door hard enough to rattle the latch, which nobody on Lantern Street ever did, because everyone on Lantern Street knew what was in the back room and had a healthy, learned respect for not startling the person standing closest to it.

"It's open," she called, already closing the journal, already half irritated at whoever was about to make her lose her place in a line of reasoning she'd been four months building.

The boy who came in was maybe fourteen, soaked through despite the dry afternoon, and breathing like he'd run the whole way from the river ward without stopping once, which, judging by the state of him, he had.

"Wickern," he managed. "You're — you're Wren Wickern. Healer Mari's sister."

Something in Wren's chest did a small, specific, unwelcome thing, a kind of pre-emptive closing she recognized and despised in herself and had never once managed to stop in time. "I am."

"They sent me up from Lowmarket," the boy said, and here, for just a second, his nerve seemed to fail him, the way nerve does right before the worst part of a sentence. "She collapsed. Healer Mari. About an hour ago, in the ward, in front of — in front of everybody. They got her stable, they said to tell you stable, they said not to alarm anyone, but they also said —"

"Said what," Wren said, very quietly, and put down the pen she hadn't noticed she was still holding.

"They said come now," the boy said. "Not next week. Now."

The workshop, for one suspended second, was exactly as it had been thirty seconds before — Bartholomew settled and dead-still in his cradle, the ledger open to a careful column of humidity readings, her grandmother's journal lying open to a page about silt and memory and three hundred years of accumulated weight — and then it was not the same room at all, because Wren was already moving, already past the boy, already out the door into the bright unbothered afternoon, leaving the eleven measurements and the theory and the one perfectly reasonable amount of feeling she had spent four careful years learning how to hold sitting open on the bench behind her, completely, uselessly unattended.
