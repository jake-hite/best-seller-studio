# Chapter Four — What the Guild Won't Sign For

The guild-house on Lantern Street looked exactly as it had three weeks ago, which struck Wren, walking up to it in the grey hour before the shops opened, as a small personal insult. The brass plate by the door still read LANTERN STREET ALCHEMISTS — MATERIAL OPENING, CONFIRMED & BONDED in the same patient, unhurried lettering it had worn since before she was born. Bartholomew, presumably, was still exactly where she'd left him, still sitting in his cradle not wanting anything in particular, while her entire understanding of how the next several months of her life were going to go had quietly rearranged itself around a name nobody said out loud.

Corvin was already at his bench when she came in, which meant he'd either not slept or had simply decided, the way he decided most things, that there was no point pretending otherwise to a room that already knew him.

"You went to the Court rolls," he said, without turning around. "Oswin Thale sent a boy round yesterday evening to ask after you, very politely, mostly to confirm you'd got home safe. Word travels faster than you do."

"I wasn't trying to be quiet about it."

"No," Corvin agreed. "You never have been, which I've always thought was the most surprising thing about you, given how quiet you manage to be about everything else." He set down the tool he'd been holding and turned, finally, and looked at her with the specific weary directness she'd learned, over four years, to brace for. "You're going to ask me for something."

"I need guild leave. Proper leave, on the books, not just borrowed days nobody notices. And I need — " she made herself say it plainly, the way she'd promised her mother she would do things from now on " — I need access to verified wyrm-sign material. Anything in the cabinet with a clean provenance. I have a theory I want to test, and I can't test it on inert stock alone."

"Wyrm material," Corvin said, in a voice gone very flat, "for a theory. To use on your sister."

"To use near my sister. I'm not proposing anything reckless —"

"You are proposing," Corvin said, "to take a substance whose entire defining property is that it has soaked up three hundred years of someone else's unmanaged feeling, and put it near a woman who is, by every account I've heard secondhand because nobody official will return my letters, already drowning in her own. Tell me which part of that isn't reckless and I'll consider signing for it."

"The part where I'm the one handling it. I know the material better than anyone in this guild who isn't you. I've spent four years on exactly this question."

"You've spent four years on a hobby," Corvin said, and then, seeing her face, softened it by perhaps ten percent, which from him was an apology. "I don't say that to be cruel. I say it because there's a difference between the kind of knowing that lets you log eleven measurements safely in a controlled room, and the kind of knowing that survives contact with an actual emergency, and I have watched exactly one person in my life confuse those two kinds of knowing, and I buried her before she turned twenty-six."

The room went very still. Wren had heard, in the vague, secondhand way you heard things about people who'd been gone longer than you'd been alive, that Corvin had lost an apprentice once, early in his own mastery, but he had never once told her himself, not in four years, and she understood, watching his face now, that he was telling her now entirely on purpose, as the most expensive thing he had to offer instead of his signature.

"Her name was Yvet," he said. "My own apprentice-sister, under the same master. Cleverer than me, faster than me, better hands than I've ever had. She had a theory too, about handling unstable residue near an open wound — a good theory, properly worked out, not reckless the way you're imagining I think yours is. She was right about almost all of it. She was wrong about exactly one variable, in exactly one session, and the gap between *almost all* and *all* killed her in about four minutes, in a room I was standing in, close enough to do nothing useful at all." He let that sit. "I did not become careful because I'm naturally a careful man, Wren. I became careful because I watched what it costs to be ninety percent right about a thing that requires being right entirely."

"I'm not asking you to certify the theory," Wren said, around something tight in her own throat now. "I'm asking for leave and material. I'll take the risk on myself. I already understand it's mine to take."

"And if you're wrong, in exactly the gap Yvet was wrong in, who buries you? Your mother, who's already burying her own father's good name on your sister's behalf? Me, again, twenty-eight years later, having apparently learned nothing at all?" Corvin shook his head, and the refusal, when it came, came gently, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted it. "No. I won't sign the leave, and I won't open the cabinet. Not because I don't believe you're capable — I think you might be the most capable person to come through this guild-house in a decade, which is exactly why I'm not willing to spend you on a theory nobody's checked. Go home. Sit with your sister. Let the people whose actual job this is do their actual job."

"Their job has produced four case files in thirty years and three of them ended in Dissolution," Wren said, and heard her own voice crack slightly on the last word, and let it, because pretending otherwise to this particular man, this morning, felt like exactly the wrong kind of careful. "I'm not asking you because I think it'll be easy, Master Ashe. I'm asking because I've already gone to every single qualified, sanctioned, properly licensed person in this city, and they've all told me, kindly, that they don't know how, and I am not going to go home and sit with my sister and wait to see whether kindness turns out to be enough."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face did move, slightly, in a direction that wasn't agreement but also wasn't quite the flat refusal from before.

"Then you'll do it without the guild's name on it," he said, finally. "I mean that exactly. Not as a threat. As a fact. If you walk out that door today to chase down whatever you're chasing, you walk out as Wren Wickern, private citizen, and not as a journeywoman of this house, because I will not have Lantern Street's name attached to whatever happens next, and I think — when you're less frightened than you are this morning — you'll understand that's the only protection I actually have left to offer either one of you."

It took her a moment to find her voice again.

"You're dismissing me."

"I'm declining to certify you," Corvin said. "There's a difference, though I understand if it doesn't feel like one this morning. Your bench is still your bench. Bartholomew is still your responsibility, when you come back to him. The door isn't locked against you. It's locked against the cabinet, and it's going to stay that way, because I have buried one person I loved for being almost entirely right, and I find, at sixty-one, that I have exactly no more patience left for almost."

Wren stood there a long moment in the workshop that had been, for four years, the one room in the world where she'd never once had to perform anything for anyone — where her competence had simply been true, witnessed, unremarkable in the best possible way — and felt it close to her, gently and completely, the way a door closes when someone has decided, out of love, not to let you through it.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll go as a private citizen."

"Wren —"

"I heard you," she said. "I understood every word. I'm not angry at you, which I think might be the worst part, because it would honestly be easier if I were." She picked up, from the corner of her own bench, the small traveling case she'd packed the night before without quite admitting to herself why, and slid into it, before she could think too hard about what she was doing, the one piece of wyrm-sign material that had never once belonged to the guild's cabinet at all — her grandmother's scale, the one from the journal, the one nobody had ever needed to certify because it had been a private inheritance since before she was born. "I won't need the cabinet. I have my own."

Corvin's face, watching her close the case, did something she didn't have time to read properly, because she was already moving, already past Bartholomew's cradle and the ledger and the eleven careful measurements that had been, until three weeks ago, the entire shape of her ambition, and out the door into a Confluence morning that had not paused, even slightly, to notice that everything in her life had just quietly rearranged itself around a choice she hadn't fully known she was making until she'd already made it.

She did not look back to see whether he watched her go. She suspected, from the particular silence behind her, that he did.
