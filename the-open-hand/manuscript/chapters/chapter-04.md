# Chapter Four — What the Guild Won't Sign For

The guild-house on Lantern Street looked exactly as it had three weeks ago, which struck Wren, walking up to it in the grey hour before the shops opened, as a small personal insult. The brass plate by the door still read LANTERN STREET ALCHEMISTS — MATERIAL OPENING, CONFIRMED & BONDED in the same patient, unhurried lettering it had worn since before she was born, the same plate she'd polished, as an apprentice, every third Friday for two full years before anyone trusted her with anything that could actually hurt someone. The street itself had the particular hush of an hour when the city hadn't decided yet whether it was awake — a single lamplighter working his way down the far side of the road, snuffing wicks one by one with the long brass pole he'd carried, she'd heard once, for nineteen years; the baker's ovens already going, their warmth reaching the pavement a good ten feet before the smell of the first loaves did; somewhere behind a shuttered window, a kettle starting to sing and then, abruptly, stopping. Bartholomew, presumably, was still exactly where she'd left him, still sitting in his cradle not wanting anything in particular, while her entire understanding of how the next several months of her life were going to go had quietly rearranged itself around a name nobody said out loud.

Wren had slept badly and eaten worse — a heel of bread standing up in the upriver kitchen before dawn, because sitting down to eat properly had felt like it would cost her more resolve than she currently had to spare — and she was aware, walking up Lantern Street with her traveling case already packed and her grandmother's scale already a careful, deliberate weight in the case's lined inner pocket, that some part of her was hoping, in a way she would not have admitted to anyone including herself, that Corvin would simply say yes, and she would not have to find out what the alternative cost.

She went to Bartholomew first, before she went to Corvin, which told her something about her own priorities she didn't have time to examine properly. The workshop was cold and dim, the stove banked low from the night before, and she crossed to the cradle in the grey half-light without bothering to light a lamp, her feet finding the familiar path between benches by long habit alone. The scale sat exactly as she'd left it, dead-still in the brass cradle, the temperature strip along its inner lip still the same pale, unremarkable green it had been three weeks ago, and she felt, looking at it, a small unexpected ache that had nothing to do with theory and everything to do with the simple fact of having missed a piece of dead king's spine more than she'd missed several actual people during the same three weeks. She stood over it for a moment doing the small, slightly absurd thing she'd never admitted to anyone she did, which was explain herself to it, quietly, as though three hundred years of accumulated patience entitled it to an accounting.

"I have to go away for a while," she told it, resting two fingers lightly on the cradle's cool brass rim, well clear of the scale itself. "Not because of anything you did. You've been exemplary. I want that on the record, in case anyone ever again suggests you're difficult." Bartholomew, being technically alive and entirely without ears, did not respond, which was, as always, the correct and professional response. "Corvin's going to look after you. He pretends he doesn't enjoy it. He enjoys it enormously. Don't tell him I said that. I'd also appreciate it if you didn't do the thing where you remember the river battle while I'm gone, just generally, as a courtesy. I won't be here to log it and that will bother me more than is probably healthy."

"Telling who what," Corvin said, from the doorway, in a voice that made it clear he'd been standing there for at least the back half of that sentence, which meant he'd either not slept or had simply decided, the way he decided most things, that there was no point pretending otherwise to a room that already knew him. He looked, in the grey morning light, older than she remembered him looking three weeks ago, or perhaps she had simply never stood in this room early enough to see him before he'd had time to arrange his face for the day — the lines around his eyes deeper, his shoulders set with the particular stiffness of a man who'd also slept badly, on a chair rather than a bed, judging by how he favored his lower back crossing the threshold. He had Bartholomew's gauge open on the bench behind him and a small file in one hand, the kind of unnecessary, deeply satisfying maintenance work he did with his hands whenever his mind had somewhere worse to be.

"Nothing. I was being professional with a dead king's spine. It's a private ritual. You wouldn't understand it, you're a Tuesday-blank person."

"I understood it perfectly. You told a wyrm scale I enjoy looking after it, which I do, and then told it not to tell me you'd said so, to a thing with no mouth, which is either the most or the least private confession I've ever overheard, and I genuinely cannot decide which." He set the file down. Whatever had been almost-warm in his face a moment ago folded itself away, businesslike, the way it always did right before he said something he'd clearly rehearsed on the walk in. "You went to the Court rolls," he said. "Oswin Thale sent a boy round yesterday evening to ask after you, very politely, mostly to confirm you'd got home safe. Word travels faster than you do."

"I wasn't trying to be quiet about it."

"No," Corvin agreed. "You never have been, which I've always thought was the most surprising thing about you, given how quiet you manage to be about everything else." He set down the file, squared it against the bench edge with the same unnecessary care he gave most small tools when his hands needed something to do, and turned, finally, and looked at her with the specific weary directness she'd learned, over four years, to brace for. The light through the high window caught the grey at his temples, more of it than she remembered, though she supposed grey didn't arrive all at once, it simply accumulated the way everything else in this trade accumulated, quietly, until one morning somebody finally noticed. "You're going to ask me for something."

"I'm going to ask you for several things, actually. I made a list. I find lists steady me."

"Of course you did," Corvin said, not unkindly, and something that might almost have been the start of a smile crossed his face and was gone again before it could properly arrive. "Go on, then. Read me the list."

"I need guild leave. Proper leave, on the books, not just borrowed days nobody notices." She made herself say the rest plainly, the way she'd promised her mother she would do things from now on, and was aware, saying it, of the specific effort it took to keep her voice as level as she wanted it to sound. "And I need access to verified wyrm-sign material. Anything in the cabinet with a clean provenance. I have a theory I want to test, and I can't test it on inert stock alone."

Corvin didn't answer right away. He picked the small file back up, turned it over once in his fingers, set it down again, the closest thing to a fidget she'd ever seen from him in four years, and when he finally spoke his voice had gone very flat, the particular flatness she now understood, looking back, as the sound of a man closing a door before he'd even let himself feel what was on the other side of it.

"Wyrm material," he said, "for a theory. To use on your sister."

"To use *near* my sister. I'm not proposing anything reckless —"

"You are proposing," Corvin said, "to take a substance whose entire defining property is that it's soaked up three hundred years of someone else's unmanaged feeling, and put it near a woman who is, by every account I've heard secondhand because nobody official will return my letters, already drowning in her own. Tell me which part of that isn't reckless and I'll consider signing for it."

"The part where I'm the one handling it. I know the material better than anyone in this guild who isn't you. I've spent four years on exactly this question."

"You've spent four years on a hobby," Corvin said, and then, seeing her face, softened it by perhaps ten percent, which from him was an apology. "There's a kind of knowing that lets you log eleven measurements safely in a controlled room. There's a kind that survives contact with an actual emergency. I've watched exactly one person I loved confuse the two. I buried her before she turned twenty-six."

The room went very still. Even Bartholomew, three centuries dead and entirely without opinions on the conversation, seemed to settle deeper into his cradle, as if the whole workshop had decided, collectively, to hold its breath. Wren had heard, in the vague, secondhand way you heard things about people who'd been gone longer than you'd been alive, that Corvin had lost an apprentice once, early in his own mastery, but he had never once told her himself, not in four years, not even the one time three winters back when a guild dinner's wine had loosened most of the room's tongues and left his alone untouched, and she understood, watching his face now, that he was telling her now entirely on purpose, as the most expensive thing he had to offer instead of his signature.

He didn't sit down. He stood with both hands flat on the bench's edge, weight braced on them, the posture of a man steadying himself against something rather than simply resting.

"Her name was Yvet," he said. "My apprentice-sister, same master. Cleverer than me. Faster hands — I want that understood, I wasn't the talented one in that workshop, I was the careful one, and for most of two years I resented her a little for making careful look like the lesser virtue." He paused, and something crossed his face that Wren had genuinely never seen there before, something unguarded and much younger than sixty-one. "Had a theory too — handling unstable residue near an open wound, properly worked out, not reckless the way you think I think yours is. She'd run the numbers eleven times. I checked them myself, twice, because I wanted to find the flaw and couldn't. She was right about almost all of it. Wrong about one variable, one session, and the gap between *almost all* and *all* took four minutes to kill her, in a room I was standing in, close enough to do nothing useful at all."

He stopped there, and the silence that followed had a different quality than the silence before it — not weighted with what he hadn't said yet, but weighted with the fact that he had now said the worst of it and there was nothing left to brace for.

"I have told exactly two people that story in thirty-four years," he said. "You're the third. I'm not telling you for sympathy, and I'd thank you not to offer me any, because I haven't built the muscle for receiving it gracefully and I'm too old to start now." He let that sit a moment, his hands gone still on the bench for the first time since she'd walked in. "I didn't get careful because carefulness comes naturally to me. I got careful because I watched what almost costs. Four minutes, Wren. I have measured a great many things in my life and that is still the only measurement I've never once been able to make sit right in my chest."

"I'm not asking you to certify the theory," Wren said, around something tight in her own throat now. "I'm asking for leave and material. I'll take the risk on myself. I already understand it's mine to take."

"And if you're wrong, in the gap Yvet was wrong in — who buries you? Your mother, already burying her own father's good name on your sister's behalf? Me, twenty-eight years later, having learned nothing?" Corvin shook his head, and the refusal, when it came, came flatly, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted it. "No. I won't sign the leave. Won't open the cabinet. Not because I doubt you're capable — I think you're the most capable hand to come through this house in a decade, which is exactly why I won't spend you on a theory nobody's checked. Go home. Sit with your sister. Let the people whose actual job this is do their actual job."

"Their job has produced four case files in thirty years and three of them ended in Dissolution," Wren said, and heard her own voice crack slightly on the last word, and let it, because pretending otherwise to this particular man, this morning, felt like exactly the wrong kind of careful. "I'm not asking you because I think it'll be easy, Master Ashe. I'm asking because I've already gone to every single qualified, sanctioned, properly licensed person in this city, and they've all told me, kindly, that they don't know how, and I am not going to go home and sit with my sister and wait to see whether kindness turns out to be enough."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face did move, slightly, in a direction that wasn't agreement but also wasn't quite the flat refusal from before. He picked the file back up, turned it over once in his fingers, didn't use it.

"Then you'll do it without the guild's name on it," he said, finally. "Not a threat. A fact. Walk out that door today, you walk out as Wren Wickern, private citizen. Not a journeywoman of this house. I won't have Lantern Street's name attached to whatever happens next. It's the only protection I've got left to give either one of you, and you'll see that, once you're less frightened than you are this morning."

It took her a moment to find her voice again.

"You're dismissing me."

"I'm declining to certify you. There's a difference." He set the file down for good, squared it against the edge of the bench with more care than the small tool required, a man giving his hands something to do because his mouth had run out of room to be gentle in any other way. "Your bench is still your bench. Bartholomew's still your responsibility, when you come back to him. The door's not locked against you. It's locked against the cabinet. Stays that way. I've buried one person I loved for being almost entirely right, and at sixty-one I've got exactly no patience left for almost."

Wren stood there a long moment in the workshop that had been, for four years, the one room in the world where she'd never once had to perform anything for anyone — where her competence had simply been true, witnessed, unremarkable in the best possible way — and felt it close to her, gently and completely, the way a door closes when someone has decided, out of love, not to let you through it.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll go as a private citizen."

"Wren —"

"I heard you," she said. "I understood every word. I'm not angry at you, which I think might be the worst part, because it would honestly be easier if I were." She picked up, from the corner of her own bench, the small traveling case she'd packed the night before without quite admitting to herself why, and went through it once more out of pure habit while he watched, narrating it under her breath the way she narrated everything that mattered to her: spare cradle-cloth, two small glass vials with cork stoppers, a folding brass measuring rule that had belonged to her grandmother before it belonged to her, her own ledger, three days of dried provisions she'd packed with more optimism than the situation probably warranted, and, slid into the lined inner pocket before she could think too hard about what she was doing, the one piece of wyrm-sign material that had never once belonged to the guild's cabinet at all — her grandmother's scale, the one from the journal, the one nobody had ever needed to certify because it had been a private inheritance since before she was born. "I won't need the cabinet. I have my own."

Corvin's face, watching her close the case, did something she didn't have time to read properly, because she was already moving, already past Bartholomew's cradle and the ledger and the eleven careful measurements that had been, until three weeks ago, the entire shape of her ambition.

"It's not even verified," he said, behind her, in a voice that had given up trying to stop her and settled, instead, for simply being heard. "Your grandmother's scale. Nobody's ever run a proper resonance assay on it."

"I've run nine," Wren said, without turning around. "On my own time. It's clean. It's old, and it's tired, and it's clean, and I would not be taking it within a hundred feet of my sister if it weren't."

"Of course you have," Corvin said, and there was something in it — not quite a laugh, Corvin didn't laugh, but the nearest dry cousin of one — that she chose, deliberately, to take with her out the door instead of the last look on his face.

She did not look back to see whether he watched her go. She suspected, from the particular silence behind her, that he did.

---

She stood for a minute on the step outside, traveling case in hand, in the grey hour before Lantern Street properly woke up around her — a baker two doors down banking his first fire of the day, a cat surveying the street from a windowsill with the proprietary disdain of something that owned the building and merely tolerated the humans inside it — and made herself do, out loud, under her breath, the kind of accounting she trusted more than feelings.

What she had: a clean scale, nine resonance assays deep, no guild signature attached to any of it. A theory four months built and zero days tested on anything that mattered. A name — *Vesper Reed* — and a general direction, south, along a mill channel, and absolutely no certainty the woman would open her door to a stranger, let alone help one.

What she didn't have: leave, certification, her employer's blessing, a map more specific than "follow the water," or any reasonable expectation that any of this would work.

It was not, by any measure she'd have accepted from a guild apprentice presenting a research proposal, a plan. It was a direction and a deadline she didn't know the exact date of, which was, she thought, closing the case and starting down toward the river road before she could talk herself into a more sensible morning, possibly the most honest description of love she'd ever managed to produce.
