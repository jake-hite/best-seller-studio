# Chapter Twelve — Reciprocity

Vesper came to the Quiet House two days later, exactly as she'd said she would, and the first thing she did — before any examination, before a single question to Mari about how she felt — was apologize to Sela, plainly and without performance, for the inconvenience of her own reputation.

"I know what my presence here costs the house's standing with the Court," she said, in the front room, while Sela stood with her arms folded in the same posture of provisional trust she'd worn for Wren a week earlier. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise, or ask you to. If you'd rather I examine her somewhere less visible, I'll go where you tell me to go. The patient's care is the only thing that should decide this, and I'd rather inconvenience myself than have you spend political capital you didn't choose to spend."

It was, Wren thought, watching Sela's arms unfold a fraction, exactly the right thing to say, delivered in exactly the right tone, and she found herself filing the observation away with the same wary precision she'd have used to log an unusually clean resonance reading — *too clean, almost, for something this complicated* — even as the rest of her relaxed at the sheer competence of it.

The examination itself took the better part of an hour, and Wren made herself stay in the room for all of it, watching Vesper's hands rather than her sister's face, because she had decided, somewhere on the walk over, that she trusted hands before she trusted anything a clever person said about her own intentions. Vesper's hands were precise, unhurried, entirely without the flinching caution Wren had half expected from someone working this close to a Saturation case — she handled the resonance readings the way Wren handled a wyrm scale, with the specific confident gentleness of someone who had stopped being afraid of the material a long time ago because she'd finally understood it well enough not to need to be.

"Your buffer bought her more than I expected," Vesper said afterward, in the corridor, with Mari resting and Sela hovering at a polite, listening distance. "I want to say that plainly, because I think you've spent the last week assuming it bought less than it did. The deepening is real — she's measurably worse than she was the day you Bled for her — but the rate is slower than an unbuffered Saturation at this density usually runs. You bought her closer to three weeks than the four days you calculated, I think, though I'd want to watch the curve a few more days before I'd commit to that number in writing."

"That's not what Sela's notes said."

"Sela's notes are tracking the surface symptoms, which is the right thing for a house healer to track, and not the underlying resonance load, which is the thing that actually determines when she goes under. I'm not correcting her. I'm telling you there's more than one true number, and you've been reading the more frightening one because nobody's shown you the other."

It should have felt like comfort, manufactured to keep Wren pliant and grateful, and some small, watchful piece of her — the same piece that catalogued resonance signatures whether or not anyone had asked it to — noted, almost admiringly, that it managed to feel like comfort and be entirely true at the same time, which was a harder trick to pull off than a simple lie would have been, and a great deal harder to argue with afterward.

They walked out together along the footbridge afterward, Confluence's lesser channel running fast and bright beneath them in the late light, and Vesper, unprompted, said the thing that would turn out to matter more than the examination had.

"You're angry," she said. "I noticed it the first afternoon, and I've watched it sit under everything you've said to me since, very carefully managed, the way you manage everything. You're allowed to say it to me plainly. I promise you I am the single least shockable person you are likely to meet this year."

"I'm not angry. I'm worried about my sister."

"You're both, and the second one is making the first one harder to admit, because admitting you're angry at a system that produced your sister feels, to you, disloyal to her specifically, when actually it's the opposite — it's the only form of loyalty that doesn't require you to also let her keep dying for it quietly." Vesper said it without heat, the way she seemed to say everything, and it landed harder for the lack of heat than any accusation could have. "Say it once, to me, where it costs you nothing. What are you actually angry about."

Wren walked another dozen paces before she answered, and when she did, it came out faster than she meant it to, the way water came when a gate finally gave.

"She was nine years old," she said, "the first time anyone praised her for not crying. *Nine.* I watched it happen — I didn't understand what I was watching at the time, I just knew Mama looked at her like she'd done something wonderful and looked at me, the same afternoon, like I'd done something merely fine, because I'd cried about something ordinary and Mari hadn't cried about something that should have flattened her. And it kept happening, every year after that, this whole — this whole *machine* the Court built and the guild reinforced and our own mother, without meaning a single bit of harm, kept feeding her into, until being beloved and being competent fused into the exact same thing in her head, and now she can't stop because stopping reads to her as proof she was never any good at all. And nobody built that machine on purpose. That's the part that makes me angriest, I think. Nobody sat down and decided to grind her down for parts. They just kept rewarding the thing that was killing her because it looked, from outside, exactly like virtue."

"Yes," Vesper said, simply, and the single word carried more weight than the entire careful examination had. "That is precisely the machine, and you have just described it more exactly than the Council's own reform faction has managed in the eleven years I've been listening to them try. The prestige ideal does not need anyone to be cruel. It only needs everyone to keep applauding the people who never say no, and the people who keep saying no — your Bram Coll, your Corvin Ashe, every unglamorous Material-opener doing useful, unwatched work nobody writes ledgers about — get treated, at best, as the necessary background to somebody else's heroism." She stopped on the bridge, turning to face Wren fully. "I am not telling you this to make you angrier than you already are. I am telling you because you are the first person in three weeks who has said it to me in those exact words instead of the Court's softer, more forgivable version, and I find I have very little patience left for the softer version."

They walked the rest of the way to the upriver house in something closer to companionable silence than Wren had felt with anyone outside her own family in years, and it was only afterward, sitting at her own workbench with the wyrm scale cooling under her hand, that she let herself notice how thoroughly Vesper had answered a question about anger without once mentioning her own.

Vesper came again the following evening, this time to the dye-shed rather than the Quiet House, carrying nothing but a small, worn case that turned out to hold a second scale — older than Wren's, darker, its surface so dense with accumulated resonance that Wren's hand actually trembled, slightly, the first time she held it.

"That's not a scale," Wren said, turning it over once with the specific reverence she usually reserved for equipment that had earned it. "That's a small, extremely judgmental grandmother. I can feel it disapproving of my workbench already."

"It's had longer to develop opinions than yours has," Vesper said, the closest thing to actual lightness Wren had heard from her yet. "I'd introduce you properly, but I've never named mine, which I suspect you'll find a moral failing."

"I absolutely do. Bartholomew would be appalled on its behalf."

"Bartholomew."

"My guild scale. Don't ask. It's a private ritual I have with a dead king's spine, and I'm only telling you because you've already seen my entire personality fall apart in your front room once, so there's very little dignity left to protect at this point."

Something that might, on a less composed face, have been an actual smile crossed Vesper's, there and gone, and for one disorienting moment Wren liked her so plainly and so simply that it frightened her considerably more than the lock-house's eerie tidiness had.

"Your buffer has one real weakness," Vesper said, watching Wren's face as she handled the second scale, businesslike again. "You proved this honestly, which I respect, but I don't think you've fully understood why it nearly failed the way it did in Mari's room. You opened toward the scale cold — asked it to absorb a flood it had never met, with nothing of your own already settled in the channel to give the exchange a shape. That's why the first contact nearly knocked you down before the buffer caught up. A cold channel has nothing to match against. It has to learn the flood's shape in the same instant it's trying to hold it back, which is the most dangerous possible moment to be learning anything."

"What's the alternative?"

"Seed it first. Before you ever open toward the person you're trying to protect, open toward the scale alone, on purpose, with something small and entirely your own — a controlled feeling, contained, nothing you can't immediately afford to lose, the same way you'd prime a vessel before you asked it to hold something volatile. Give the channel a known shape to measure the unknown flood against. It won't make the flood smaller. It will make the first half-second less like falling and more like stepping into water you already know the depth of." Vesper held the older scale out, not quite offering it, just letting Wren see the full density of it. "I have used a version of this for over a decade. I am giving it to you because it is the single most useful thing I know how to teach a Material-opener doing the bravest reckless work I have personally witnessed in longer than I care to specify, and because I am not yet asking you for anything in return. I want to be exact about that, since I suspect you're waiting for the cost to reveal itself. It isn't here yet. When it is, you'll know, because I will tell you plainly, the way I told you everything else."

Wren tried it that same evening, alone in the dye-shed, seeding her own scale with something small and ordinary — gratitude, mostly, for the day going better than she'd feared — before opening toward a deliberately overcharged scrap of waste cloth, and found that Vesper had been exactly right: the first contact came in like wading rather than falling, measured and survivable in a way the cold version never had been. It worked beautifully. It also left her, afterward, sitting on the dye-shed floor with the seeded scale cooling in her lap, turning over a small, specific unease she hadn't expected and couldn't immediately name — not about the technique, which had behaved exactly as promised, but about how easily the seeding had gone, how little it had asked of her, how thoroughly a thing that should have felt like borrowed power had instead felt like being handed something that was already, somehow, hers.

She took the technique to bed with her that night the way she took every good result to bed — turning it carefully, testing it instinctively for the seams a too-good answer usually had — and found, unsettlingly, that she couldn't locate any. Not yet. Not in the technique itself. Only in the small, quiet, traitorous warmth she felt at being taken this seriously, this fully, by someone who clearly understood exactly what she'd built and exactly what it had cost her to build it.

It was, she would understand much later, the precise moment the seduction had actually finished its work — not in anything Vesper asked for, since she had asked for nothing at all, but in the simple, devastating fact that for the first time since the runner's knock three weeks ago, Wren went to sleep that night without once rehearsing all the ways the day's help might secretly be a trap.
