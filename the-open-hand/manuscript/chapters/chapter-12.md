# Chapter Twelve — Reciprocity

Vesper came to the Quiet House two days later, exactly as she'd said she would, and exactly on the hour she'd named, which Wren noted with the small, specific satisfaction she reserved for anything that arrived precisely when predicted — a dye batch finishing at the projected minute, a resonance curve matching the equation on paper down to the second decimal. She had been at the Quiet House since dawn herself, ostensibly to update Sela's chart of the buffer's measured effects, actually because she had not been able to make herself wait at the upriver house for news that might or might not be good. She had brought a list with her, written the night before by candlelight in a hand steadier than she felt: questions to ask, things to watch for in Vesper's hands, the precise wording she intended to use if asked for permission to stay in the room during the examination. She had needed exactly none of the list. Vesper simply assumed she would stay, the way one assumes a colleague will stay, and the assumption did more to settle Wren's nerves than the list had managed in an entire sleepless hour.

The first thing Vesper did — before any examination, before a single question to Mari about how she felt — was apologize to Sela, plainly and without performance, for the inconvenience of her own reputation.

"I know what my presence here costs the house's standing with the Court," she said, in the front room, while Sela stood with her arms folded in the same posture of provisional trust she'd worn for Wren a week earlier. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise, or ask you to. If you'd rather I examine her somewhere less visible, I'll go where you tell me to go. The patient's care is the only thing that should decide this, and I'd rather inconvenience myself than have you spend political capital you didn't choose to spend."

It was, Wren thought, watching Sela's arms unfold a fraction, exactly the right thing to say, delivered in exactly the right tone, and she set the observation down next to the others she'd started keeping about this woman — with the same wary precision she'd have used to log an unusually clean resonance reading, the kind that made you check your own instruments before you trusted the number. *Too clean, almost, for something this complicated.* Even as the rest of her relaxed at the sheer competence of it, some small, watchful part kept its own separate ledger running underneath, noting the exact angle of the apology, the way it had cost Vesper nothing visible to make and bought Sela's good will at a rate that seemed faintly unfair to anyone trying to earn the same good will the slow way.

The examination itself took the better part of an hour, and Wren made herself stay in the room for all of it, watching Vesper's hands rather than her sister's face, because she had decided, somewhere on the walk over, that she trusted hands before she trusted anything a clever person said about her own intentions. Sela had offered her a chair by the window, out of the direct light, and Wren had taken it gratefully, glad to have something to lean against besides her own legs. The room smelled of the willow-bark tincture Sela kept for fevers and, underneath that, faintly, of Mari herself — a smell Wren had known her whole life and had never once had cause to find frightening before this month.

Vesper's hands were precise, unhurried, entirely without the flinching caution Wren had half expected from someone working this close to a Saturation case. She moved methodically down Mari's arm, two fingers resting lightly at the wrist, then the inside of the elbow, then a spot at the base of the throat Wren had never thought to check at all, murmuring numbers to herself under her breath that meant nothing to Wren and everything, clearly, to her — a private vocabulary built over decades, the same way Wren had her own private vocabulary of density and saturation curves that meant nothing to anyone outside the guild. She handled the resonance readings the way Wren handled a wyrm scale, with the specific confident gentleness of someone who had stopped being afraid of the material a long time ago because she'd finally understood it well enough not to need to be. Mari, half-lucid, opened her eyes once during it and said, thickly, "Is this the scary one," and Vesper, without missing a single beat of whatever she was measuring, said, "Apparently, yes," in a tone dry enough that Mari almost laughed before sliding back under.

"Your buffer bought her more than I expected," Vesper said afterward, in the corridor, with Mari resting and Sela hovering at a polite, listening distance. "I want to say that plainly, because I think you've spent the last week assuming it bought less than it did. The deepening is real — she's measurably worse than she was the day you Bled for her — but the rate is slower than an unbuffered Saturation at this density usually runs. You bought her closer to three weeks than the four days you calculated, I think, though I'd want to watch the curve a few more days before I'd commit to that number in writing."

Wren leaned against the corridor wall, the cool plaster a relief against her shoulder blades, and found she had to ask the next question twice in her head before she trusted her own voice with it. "How sure are you. On a scale I'd actually use — not 'reasonably confident,' an actual number, the kind I'd put in a ledger."

"Sure enough to tell you to sleep tonight instead of sitting up rechecking your own arithmetic, which I suspect is what you were planning to do regardless of my answer." Vesper's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "Eight in ten, if you want the number. I've been wrong about that number before, in both directions, and I'd rather tell you the honest eight than a comforting nine that isn't actually mine to give you."

"That's not what Sela's notes said." Wren had brought them, folded twice, from her coat pocket — three days of careful, anxious tracking in Sela's economical hand, numbers trending the wrong direction every single morning.

"Sela's notes are tracking the surface symptoms, which is the right thing for a house healer to track, and not the underlying resonance load, which is the thing that actually determines when she goes under. I'm not correcting her. I'm telling you there's more than one true number, and you've been reading the more frightening one because nobody's shown you the other." Vesper took the folded notes from her, unfolded them once, looked them over with the same unhurried attention she'd given Mari's wrist, and folded them back along their original creases before handing them back, which struck Wren, for reasons she couldn't immediately name, as its own small kindness.

It should have felt like comfort, manufactured to keep Wren pliant and grateful, and some small, watchful piece of her — the same piece that catalogued resonance signatures whether or not anyone had asked it to — noted, almost admiringly, that it managed to feel like comfort and be entirely true at the same time, which was a harder trick to pull off than a simple lie would have been, and a great deal harder to argue with afterward. She tucked the notes back into her coat and found her hands had stopped, for the first time in three days, wanting something to hold onto.

They walked out together along the footbridge afterward, Confluence's lesser channel running fast and bright beneath them in the late light, the water carrying the particular clean cold smell it always had this time of year, mineral and faintly green, like the inside of a jar that had held something growing. A heron stood motionless on one of the piers, entirely unbothered by either of them, and Wren watched it for a few paces, grateful for something to look at that wasn't her own feet or Vesper's unreadable, composed profile.

Vesper, unprompted, said the thing that would turn out to matter more than the examination had.

"You're angry," she said. "I noticed it the first afternoon, and I've watched it sit under everything you've said to me since, very carefully managed, the way you manage everything. You're allowed to say it to me plainly. I promise you I am the single least shockable person you are likely to meet this year."

"I'm not angry. I'm worried about my sister."

"You're both, and the second one is making the first one harder to admit, because admitting you're angry at a system that produced your sister feels, to you, disloyal to her specifically, when actually it's the opposite — it's the only form of loyalty that doesn't require you to also let her keep dying for it quietly." Vesper said it without heat, the way she seemed to say everything, and it landed harder for the lack of heat than any accusation could have.

Wren opened her mouth to deny it again — the denial had its own groove worn into her by now, well-practiced, easy to reach for — and found, instead, that nothing came out at all. They walked half the length of the footbridge in silence, their footsteps not quite matching, Vesper's slower and more even than Wren's quick, slightly uneven stride.

"Say it once, to me, where it costs you nothing," Vesper said finally, quietly. "What are you actually angry about."

"I don't know how to do this with someone who isn't Mari," Wren said, which was not an answer, and they both seemed to understand that, and Vesper said nothing, simply waited, the particular patient waiting of someone with nowhere else she needed to be.

Wren walked another dozen paces before she answered properly, and when she did, it came out faster than she meant it to, the way water came when a gate finally gave.

"She was nine years old," she said, "the first time anyone praised her for not crying. *Nine.* I watched it happen — I didn't understand what I was watching at the time, I just knew Mama looked at her like she'd done something wonderful and looked at me, the same afternoon, like I'd done something merely fine, because I'd cried about something ordinary and Mari hadn't cried about something that should have flattened her. And it kept happening, every year after that, this whole — this whole *machine* the Court built and the guild reinforced and our own mother, without meaning a single bit of harm, kept feeding her into, until being beloved and being competent fused into the exact same thing in her head, and now she can't stop because stopping reads to her as proof she was never any good at all. And nobody built that machine on purpose. That's the part that makes me angriest, I think. Nobody sat down and decided to grind her down for parts. They just kept rewarding the thing that was killing her because it looked, from outside, exactly like virtue."

"Yes," Vesper said, simply, and the single word carried more weight than the entire careful examination had. "That is precisely the machine, and you have just described it more exactly than the Council's own reform faction has managed in the eleven years I've been listening to them try. The prestige ideal does not need anyone to be cruel. It only needs everyone to keep applauding the people who never say no, and the people who keep saying no — your Bram Coll, your Corvin Ashe, every unglamorous Material-opener doing useful, unwatched work nobody writes ledgers about — get treated, at best, as the necessary background to somebody else's heroism." She stopped on the bridge, turning to face Wren fully. "I am not telling you this to make you angrier than you already are. I am telling you because you are the first person in three weeks who has said it to me in those exact words instead of the Court's softer, more forgivable version, and I find I have very little patience left for the softer version."

"Doesn't it ever exhaust you," Wren asked, surprising herself, "listening to the softer version, over and over, from people who think they're being brave just for saying it at all."

"It used to. I've found other uses for the exhaustion since." Vesper's gaze went, briefly, to the water below them, the current pulling fast and bright around the bridge's old piling, and for a moment Wren thought she might say something more, something that would finally answer the question she'd been too careful to ask outright — *what happened to your own anger, where did it go, did the Severance take that too* — but the moment passed, and Vesper simply resumed walking, and Wren let the question go unasked, telling herself there would be time for it later, the way she told herself a great many things lately that she hadn't yet tested for whether they were true.

They walked the rest of the way to the upriver house in something closer to companionable silence than Wren had felt with anyone outside her own family in years. The lanes had emptied out for the evening meal, lamplight starting to show yellow in windows they passed, and somewhere a few streets over someone was practicing scales on a reed-pipe, badly, with the dogged persistence of a child who had been told to practice and intended to be done with it as fast as possible. Wren found the sound absurdly comforting. It was only afterward, sitting at her own workbench with the wyrm scale cooling under her hand, the day's accumulated tension finally draining out through her shoulders in the particular way it always did once she was alone with her instruments again, that she let herself notice how thoroughly Vesper had answered a question about anger without once mentioning her own.

Vesper came again the following evening, this time to the dye-shed rather than the Quiet House, carrying nothing but a small, worn case that turned out to hold a second scale — older than Wren's, darker, its surface so dense with accumulated resonance that Wren's hand actually trembled, slightly, the first time she held it.

The dye-shed at that hour smelled the way it always smelled in early evening — woad and madder and the specific sour-green tang of a mordant bath two days past its prime, which Wren had been meaning to refresh and hadn't, because every spare hour for three weeks had gone somewhere more urgent. She'd cleared a space on the workbench for the occasion, moving aside a half-finished ledger entry and a cup gone cold with the morning's tea still in it, and she was abruptly, briefly self-conscious about the state of the place in a way she hadn't been about anything in days — not the lock-house's eerie tidiness, not the magistrate's unreadable hands, just the dye-shed's ordinary mess, suddenly visible to her the way it never was when she was alone in it.

"It's not usually this bad," she said, before Vesper had said anything at all about the state of the bench.

"I wasn't going to comment on it," Vesper said, mild. "I keep a worse one. Mine's simply better hidden, which isn't the same thing as better kept." She set the worn case down with the same unhurried care she gave everything, and opened it.

"That's not a scale," Wren said, turning it over once with the specific reverence she usually reserved for equipment that had earned it, weighing it experimentally in her palm the way she'd weigh any unfamiliar sample before committing a number to paper. It was heavier than its size accounted for, the resonance packed so densely into it that the edges of her palm prickled faintly even through the contact of her own skin, a sensation she associated, usually, with material three or four times this scale's age. "That's a small, extremely judgmental grandmother. I can feel it disapproving of my workbench already."

"It's had longer to develop opinions than yours has," Vesper said, the closest thing to actual lightness Wren had heard from her yet. "I'd introduce you properly, but I've never named mine, which I suspect you'll find a moral failing."

"I absolutely do. Bartholomew would be appalled on its behalf. He has very strong feelings about names being earned, not assigned, but he'd also agree that going thirty-some years without bothering at all is its own kind of statement, and not a flattering one."

"Bartholomew."

"My guild scale. Don't ask. It's a private ritual I have with a dead king's spine, and I'm only telling you because you've already seen my entire personality fall apart in your front room once, so there's very little dignity left to protect at this point. He's thirty miles away in his cradle right now, judging me in absentia, which is somehow worse than judging me to my face."

"Does he judge you often."

"Constantly. Mostly about my filing system, which he considers beneath both our dignities. He's never said so, obviously, being a scale, but I've had four years to learn his moods and I stand by the read." Wren set the older scale down on a clean square of cloth, with more care than she'd given anything all week, the small reverent precision she reserved entirely for material that had earned it. "What would you call this one, if you had to."

Vesper considered the question with more seriousness than Wren had expected her to give it. "Something that wouldn't sound foolish said aloud in a courtroom, which I suspect rules out most of the good options."

"That's the worst possible criterion for naming anything. Bartholomew would never have survived a courtroom test. Half the joy of him is that he sounds exactly as foolish as he is."

Something that might, on a less composed face, have been an actual smile crossed Vesper's, there and gone, and for one disorienting moment Wren liked her so plainly and so simply that it frightened her considerably more than the lock-house's eerie tidiness had.

"Your buffer has one real weakness," Vesper said, watching Wren's face as she handled the second scale, businesslike again. "You proved this honestly, which I respect, but I don't think you've fully understood why it nearly failed the way it did in Mari's room. You opened toward the scale cold — asked it to absorb a flood it had never met, with nothing of your own already settled in the channel to give the exchange a shape. That's why the first contact nearly knocked you down before the buffer caught up. A cold channel has nothing to match against. It has to learn the flood's shape in the same instant it's trying to hold it back, which is the most dangerous possible moment to be learning anything."

Wren turned this over with the specific, hungry attention she gave any correction to her own theory, already reaching for the half-finished ledger she'd pushed aside earlier, wanting to write it down before the precise shape of the sentence left her. "That's — yes. That tracks. I built the whole buffer assuming the scale's job was absorption, full stop, and I never once asked what state the scale needed to already be in before the absorption started. I was so busy proving the channel could hold the flood at all that I never checked whether it was holding the flood *well*, versus just surviving it." She made a face. "That's an embarrassing gap for someone who writes 'precondition' in the margin of every other experiment she runs."

"It's not embarrassing. It's the gap everyone misses the first time, because the first time is always built under exactly the kind of pressure that makes careful preconditions feel like a luxury you can't afford." Vesper's voice carried no judgment in it at all, which somehow made the correction easier to hear than if she'd softened it. "What's the alternative?"

"That was my question," Wren said, mildly, and Vesper's mouth twitched, the closest thing to being caught out that Wren had seen from her yet.

"Seed it first. Before you ever open toward the person you're trying to protect, open toward the scale alone, on purpose, with something small and entirely your own — a controlled feeling, contained, nothing you can't immediately afford to lose, the same way you'd prime a vessel before you asked it to hold something volatile. Give the channel a known shape to measure the unknown flood against. It won't make the flood smaller. It will make the first half-second less like falling and more like stepping into water you already know the depth of." Vesper held the older scale out, not quite offering it, just letting Wren see the full density of it. "I have used a version of this for over a decade. I am giving it to you because it is the single most useful thing I know how to teach a Material-opener doing the bravest reckless work I have personally witnessed in longer than I care to specify, and because I am not yet asking you for anything in return. I want to be exact about that, since I suspect you're waiting for the cost to reveal itself. It isn't here yet. When it is, you'll know, because I will tell you plainly, the way I told you everything else."

Wren practiced the shape of it twice, out loud, before she tried it for real — *open to the object, not the person, something small, something I can afford* — the way she always talked herself through a new technique before her hands attempted it, narrating to the empty bench the way she'd have narrated to Bartholomew if he'd been there to hear it. Vesper watched her do this without comment, no flicker of impatience at the rehearsal, which Wren noticed and filed alongside everything else she was filing about this woman without yet knowing what shelf it belonged on.

It needled at her longer than the lesson itself did. "I want to pay you for this," she said, before Vesper could leave, surprised at how flatly the words came out. "Not future thanks. Not some debt I settle later once you finally name a price. Something now — coin, or a season's dye work, or Bartholomew's full notes on saturation curves, whatever you'd actually use. I don't like owing anyone something I can't measure, and I especially don't like owing you, because I've started to like you, and the two things are getting harder to keep separate."

"I don't want your coin," Vesper said, gently, "or your dye work, or your notes, however generous the offer."

"That's not an answer. That's a refusal dressed up as flattery."

"It's both, I'll admit, but it's also true." Something almost like amusement crossed Vesper's face. "You're allowed to be frustrated with me about this. I'd be frustrated with me too, in your position."

"I am frustrated with you," Wren said, and meant it, surprised at the heat in her own voice. "I don't know how to be the kind of person who just *takes*. I've never once been that person, with anyone, and I don't actually know if I like finding out I apparently can be, given the right circumstances. So will you please just let me pay you something, so I can stop feeling like I'm the only one keeping a ledger in this entire arrangement."

Vesper didn't answer that one directly either, and the gap where the answer should have been sat between them the whole walk back to the house, unresolved — the first thing in three weeks of careful generosity that didn't behave the way comfort was supposed to behave once you set it down.

Wren tried the seeding technique that same evening, alone in the dye-shed, the lamp turned low and the door latched the way she always latched it when she meant to risk something she wasn't yet ready to explain to anyone who might walk in. She set the great old scale on the cleared square of cloth and sat across from it on an overturned dye-pot, the way she'd sit across from any new piece of equipment for the first time, taking its measure before asking anything of it. Her own scale — Bartholomew, properly, though she only used the name out loud when no one could hear her — sat warm against her hip where it always sat, and she touched it once for luck, an old habit from her earliest apprentice days that she'd never managed to shake and had stopped trying to.

She seeded her own scale with something small and ordinary — gratitude, mostly, for the day going better than she'd feared, threaded through with a thinner note of something almost like hope, the first hope she'd let herself feel in weeks that didn't immediately curdle into worry about jinxing it — and felt the seed settle into the channel the way a key settles into a lock built for it, smooth, certain, no resistance at all. She held it there a moment, turning it over, checking its edges the way she'd check any reading before trusting it, and found nothing wrong with it. It simply worked.

Then she opened toward a deliberately overcharged scrap of waste cloth she'd been saving for exactly this purpose — three days steeped in a discarded mordant bath, its resonance gone sour and concentrated the way only ruined dye-work ever did — and found that Vesper had been exactly right: the first contact came in like wading rather than falling, measured and survivable in a way the cold version never had been. She felt the seeded shape in the channel do precisely what Vesper had promised, give the unfamiliar flood something to be measured against instead of simply crashing into an empty space and filling it however it pleased. Her shoulders, braced without her noticing for the old familiar jolt, stayed loose. Her breath didn't catch. She actually laughed, once, short and startled, at how undramatic the whole thing turned out to be, and reached automatically for her ledger to record the exact sequence before the memory of it could soften at the edges the way memories of successful technique always seemed to soften once the danger had passed.

It worked beautifully. It also left her, afterward, sitting on the dye-shed floor with the seeded scale cooling in her lap, the ledger open on her knee with three lines written and the pen gone still in her hand, turning over a small, specific unease she hadn't expected and couldn't immediately name — not about the technique, which had behaved exactly as promised, but about how easily the seeding had gone, how little it had asked of her, how thoroughly a thing that should have felt like borrowed power had instead felt like being handed something that was already, somehow, hers. She tried, for a while, to talk herself out of the unease using the same tools she'd use to talk herself out of a bad reading — check the instrument, check the method, check whether the result was simply too good to be the whole story — and got nowhere with any of it, because the method had been sound, and the result had been real, and the only thing that didn't add up was the faint, persistent sense that she had been given something valuable for a price she hadn't yet been asked to name, and that not being asked yet was its own kind of debt accumulating somewhere she couldn't see the ledger for.

She took the technique to bed with her that night the way she took every good result to bed — turning it carefully, testing it instinctively for the seams a too-good answer usually had — and found, unsettlingly, that she couldn't locate any. Not yet. Not in the technique itself. Only in the small, quiet, traitorous warmth she felt at being taken this seriously, this fully, by someone who clearly understood exactly what she'd built and exactly what it had cost her to build it.

It was, she would understand much later, the precise moment the seduction had actually finished its work — not in anything Vesper asked for, since she had asked for nothing at all, but in the simple, devastating fact that for the first time since the runner's knock three weeks ago, Wren went to sleep that night without once rehearsing all the ways the day's help might secretly be a trap.
