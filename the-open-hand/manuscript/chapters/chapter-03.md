# Chapter Three — The Name Nobody Says at Council

The rest cure did not work, and it took eleven days for Wren to admit that the slow, terrible arithmetic she'd been doing in her head since the cart ride had been right all along.

Mari slept. Mari drank Petra's willowbark tea and sat by clean running water and was, by every outward measure Hesper's old house upriver could offer, given exactly the rest Petra had prescribed. And for the first four days she did seem better — color back, hands steady, the warm easy version of herself returning enough that Hesper started, cautiously, to relax, and Wren started, less cautiously and more guiltily, to think about the Hallorin order sitting unfinished back on Lantern Street.

Then, on the fifth day, Mari stopped being able to tell Wren and their mother apart for about four seconds, and laughed it off as exhaustion, and nobody believed her, including, this time, Mari.

By the eleventh day she had stopped arguing that she could walk on her own, which frightened Wren more than anything that had happened in the sickroom in Lowmarket, because Mari arguing that she could walk on her own had been, up to that point, the single most reliable fact in Wren's universe.

"It's not a Bleed anymore," Petra said, on the eleventh day, in the front room of the upriver house, in a voice that had given up entirely on softening anything. "Or it's not *only* a Bleed. I think we're past that. I think we've been past that for some days, and I am sorry, because I told you two weeks, and I believed it when I said it."

"What is it, then," Hesper said. Not a question, really. A demand for a word, the way you demanded a word for a diagnosis even when you already knew, in your body, that you weren't going to like it.

"Saturation," Petra said. "Early. I want to be precise about that — early, and there is real distance between early Saturation and anything past it, and I don't want either of you doing the kind of arithmetic in your heads that gets you to the worst possible number before we're anywhere near it. But it's Saturation, not Bleed, and Saturation doesn't resolve the way a Bleed does. Rest helps. Rest alone doesn't fix it. She needs proper discharge work, sustained, by someone who knows what they're doing with a case this far along, and I'll be honest with you both — that's not me. I've handled Bleeds my whole career. I've handled exactly one Saturation case in fifteen years, and the woman who actually pulled that patient back wasn't me, it was a specialist out of Confluence proper, and I don't know if she's even still taking cases."

"Then we find someone who is," Wren said.

"I'd start with the guild registries," Petra said, "and the Court's healing roster, and I'd start soon. Saturation doesn't run on the kind of clock that rewards waiting to see."

---

The guild registries had nothing. Wren spent two days in the Lantern Street archive room going through every case file the alchemists' guild had ever cross-filed with the healers' guild on the subject of advanced Saturation, and found exactly four entries in thirty years, three of which ended in Dissolution and one of which simply stopped, mid-file, with a note in someone's careful hand reading *case transferred, see Court records* and nothing else.

The Court's healing roster had more, on paper, and less in practice. She spent a full afternoon in the public record-hall at Confluence's heart, under the slow patient light of the rotunda's high windows, going through register after register of practicing Person-openers credentialed for advanced casework, and found a dozen names, and wrote to nine of them by the end of the week, and heard back from four, all of whom said, in various careful phrasings, the same thing: a Saturation that far along, caught that late, in a healer who'd been running it for three weeks before anyone intervened, was outside what any of them had personally handled, and they were sorry, and they wished her family well.

It was the fifth one — a retired magistrate's clerk named Oswin Thale, who'd spent forty years filing Court records and had therefore, Wren suspected, simply seen more than the practicing healers themselves — who finally said the name.

She'd gone to him almost by accident, chasing down a reference in an old case file to a "Reed protocol" that appeared exactly twice in thirty years of Court healing records and was never once explained. Oswin received her in the cramped, paper-stacked front room of his retirement cottage on the quiet side of the river, peered at her over a pair of spectacles that had clearly outlived several prescriptions, and went very still when she read him the name from the file.

"Where did you find that," he said. Not sharply. Just carefully, the way you asked a question you already half wished you hadn't.

"In a Court healing file from about twenty-six years back. It's the only place I've seen it. The case notes mention it once, like everyone reading it already knew what it meant, and then never explain it again."

"That's because everyone reading it did know what it meant, at the time," Oswin said. "It's not a protocol anyone wrote down properly, you understand. It's a name people used because saying the actual name made certain rooms go quiet."

"Whose name."

Oswin took his spectacles off, cleaned them on his sleeve with the unhurried deliberation of a man buying himself a few seconds, and put them back on.

"Vesper Reed," he said. "You won't find her in the current roster, and you won't find a current address in any guild record, and if you ask most people my age who still sit on Council committees, they will tell you, quite sincerely, that they don't know what happened to her, and they will be lying to you out of long habit rather than malice, because nobody decided to lie about it on purpose, exactly. It's just become the kind of thing nobody says out loud anymore."

"What did happen to her?"

"She was the best magistrate the Court of Open Truth produced in my lifetime," Oswin said. "Youngest ever seated, by some distance. Beloved — and I use that word carefully, I watched whole districts turn out just to see her sit a case, the way you'd turn out for a wedding. And then, twenty-six years ago, in open session, in front of the full Council, she went past Saturation in the middle of an inquest, in front of everyone, and what happened next is the part nobody agrees how to tell you."

"Did she Dissolve?"

"No," Oswin said. "That's rather the point. Somebody pulled her back. Nobody official — the Court's own healers on duty that day couldn't, or wouldn't, or simply weren't fast enough, and whatever happened in the minutes after that, it left her able to walk out of that hall under her own power, which by every account should not have been possible at that stage. And then she was censured anyway, formally, within the month, and quietly disappeared from every roster the Court keeps, and has lived since then — as far as anyone admits to knowing — somewhere along the old mill channel south of the city, alone, and is, depending entirely on who in this city you ask, either a disgrace, a danger, or the only person currently breathing who actually knows how to do the thing your sister needs done."

Wren sat with that for a long moment, in the close, paper-smelling quiet of Oswin's front room, and felt something settle into place that she did not, yet, have a name for either.

"Why was she censured," she said finally, "if she pulled herself back? If it worked?"

"Because the Court was never quite able to decide," Oswin said, "whether what she did to come back counted as a miracle or as the thing every magistrate in that hall had spent her whole career being warned against. Both readings are still argued at Council, twenty-six years on, by people who were in the room and people who weren't. I'll tell you plainly, since you've come all this way and asked me a direct question: I don't know which reading is true. I only know that the woman who walked out of that hall was never quite the same woman who walked in, and that whatever she did, she has apparently done it for one or two others since, very quietly, very rarely, and never once for the Court's benefit again."

---

She told her mother that night, in the kitchen of the upriver house, with Mari asleep two rooms away and the lamp turned low, because she had decided, somewhere on the walk back from the river ward, that she was not going to do the thing Mari had done — was not going to decide alone, quietly, that telling people would only worry them, and call that kindness.

Hesper's face did something complicated when Wren said the name. Not surprise, exactly. Hesper had lived in Confluence her whole life; she had clearly heard the name before, in whatever careful, half-finished way it got passed around among people her age, and had clearly already done some of her own arithmetic about what it might mean before Wren ever said it out loud.

"No," she said.

"Mama —"

"I know what you're about to say, and the answer is no. Not because I don't love your sister enough to try anything. Because I love both my daughters, and I am not sending the second one into a house at the end of the mill channel to go knock on the door of a woman the entire Court of Open Truth has spent twenty-six years deciding not to talk about. There is a reason nobody says her name, Wren. There is always a reason, even when nobody can agree on what it is."

"Petra said herself she's never handled a Saturation this far along. The Court roster's got nothing. The guild's got four old case files and three of them ended in Dissolution. I'm not going because it sounds exciting, Mama, I'm going because I have spent two weeks asking every credentialed, respectable, properly licensed person in this city, and they have all, every one of them, told me they don't know how, and there is exactly one person left whose name keeps coming up who apparently does."

"And if she's dangerous?"

"Then I'll be careful," Wren said, and heard, as she said it, how thin that sounded, and said it anyway, because it was the truest thing she had. "I'm good at careful. It's the one thing I'm actually good at."

Hesper looked at her for a long moment — the particular look, Wren realized, that she'd been getting from her mother in smaller doses her whole life, ever since the day an assessing magistrate had asked an eleven-year-old to open toward a stranger's grief and watched her flinch shut instead, the look that was love and worry and something else Wren had never quite had a name for, something that might have been *I don't understand you, and I am trying very hard not to let that stop me from being proud of you anyway.*

"You won't be talked out of this," Hesper said. It wasn't a question either.

"No," Wren said. "I don't think I will."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment, and then reached across the kitchen table and took Wren's hand — not the careful, professional grip Wren used on Bartholomew, not the automatic warm clasp Mari had always had for everyone, but something else, something Wren didn't have nearly enough practice receiving to know quite what to do with.

"Then go careful," Hesper said. "And go in the morning. Not tonight. I'd like one more night where both my daughters are asleep under a roof I can see."

Wren agreed to that. It was, she thought, climbing the stairs sometime later with the lamp guttering low behind her, very possibly the last perfectly reasonable, perfectly careful thing she was going to manage for some time, and she intended, for one more night, to enjoy it.
