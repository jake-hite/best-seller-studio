# Chapter Three — The Name Nobody Says at Council

The rest cure did not work, and it took eleven days for Wren to admit that the slow, terrible arithmetic she'd been doing in her head since the cart ride had been right all along.

The upriver house had a particular quality of quiet that Wren had forgotten, in four years of living above the Lantern Street workshop, how completely she missed and how completely it could also wear on a person. Their grandfather had built it two generations back on a low rise above the river's first real bend outside the city proper, where the water ran fast and clean and audible from every room with its windows open, and where the loudest sound most mornings was a moorhen arguing with another moorhen over a stretch of reed bank neither of them owned. Wren spent the first several days rediscovering the house's small mechanical idiosyncrasies — the third stair that creaked no matter how carefully you placed your weight, the kitchen door that swelled in damp weather and had to be lifted slightly on its hinges to close properly, the exact angle of morning light that woke her a full hour before she wanted it to, falling directly across the narrow bed that had been hers on childhood summers and was hers again now, smaller than she remembered, the ceiling beam six inches lower over her head than it had any right to be.

Mari slept. Mari drank Petra's willowbark tea and sat by clean running water and was, by every outward measure Hesper's old house upriver could offer, given exactly the rest Petra had prescribed. And for the first four days she did seem better — color back, hands steady, the warm easy version of herself returning enough that Hesper started, cautiously, to relax, enough that she let herself laugh, once, at something Mari said over breakfast, a real laugh, surprised out of her, that made the kitchen feel for a moment like an ordinary kitchen on an ordinary summer morning rather than a sickroom with better windows. Wren started, less cautiously and more guiltily, to think about the Hallorin order sitting unfinished back on Lantern Street, and to draft, in her head, the kind of apologetic note to Master Ashe that explained an absence without quite explaining why it might run long, and to notice, with the specific shame of someone caught wanting two things at once, that some small ungenerous corner of herself was already missing her own workbench, missing the particular silence of a room where nothing was actively, slowly, going wrong.

Then, on the fifth day, Mari stopped being able to tell Wren and their mother apart for about four seconds, standing in the kitchen doorway with a teacup in one hand, calling Wren "Mama" in a voice gone strangely young, and then blinking, and laughing it off as exhaustion, and nobody believed her, including, this time, Mari. Wren had been close enough to see her sister's eyes during those four seconds — unfocused in a way that had nothing to do with sleepiness, the pupils gone wide and slow to track — and she had said nothing about it afterward, because Mari had recovered so quickly, had reached for the right name within the same breath, that naming the four seconds out loud felt like it would somehow make them realer than they already were.

By the eleventh day she had stopped arguing that she could walk on her own, which frightened Wren more than anything that had happened in the sickroom in Lowmarket, because Mari arguing that she could walk on her own had been, up to that point, the single most reliable fact in Wren's universe — more reliable than the river running downhill, more reliable than a wyrm scale resenting the brass cradle, the kind of fact you built other, smaller certainties on top of without ever examining the foundation. There was no dramatic moment to it. Mari simply stopped offering to cross the room unassisted, stopped waving away the arm Hesper held out, accepted help getting from the bed to the chair by the window with a quiet, unprotesting *thank you* that landed in Wren's chest like a dropped weight, because a thank-you, from Mari, about something this small, was its own kind of alarm bell — the sound of a woman who had run out of the specific energy required to insist she was fine.

Wren spent that morning doing what she always did with a fact that had stopped being reliable, which was take careful notes on exactly how it had failed, as though precision about the failure might, by itself, constitute a kind of progress. She sat at the kitchen table with her ledger open to a fresh page, ruled it into columns the way she'd rule a humidity log, and made herself write down things she did not want to be writing down: the four seconds of confusion, timed as best she could against the kitchen clock. The two short naps that had stretched, without anyone quite deciding to let them, into four hours each. The way Mari's left hand had developed a small, persistent tremor that hadn't been there even at the healing house, visible now even at rest, even when she wasn't reaching for anything. By noon she had three pages of dated observations — color, pulse as best she could estimate it without proper instruments, hours slept, words slurred or repeated, the specific phrasing of each repeated word in case the pattern of *which* words mattered as much as the fact of repeating them — and absolutely no idea what to do with any of it, which was, she was beginning to suspect, going to be the defining experience of this entire summer.

Petra had come upriver herself that morning, on a borrowed horse she clearly rode badly and without much enthusiasm, judging by the relief on her face when she finally swung down from it in the yard. She'd brought her own bag this time, heavier than the one she'd carried in Lowmarket, and she set it down by the front room door with a small grunt before she even said good morning, the particular all-business briskness of someone who had already decided, on the ride over, exactly how this conversation needed to go and didn't want her own nerves talking her out of it.

"It's not a Bleed anymore," she said, on the eleventh day, in the front room of the upriver house. She had the flat, used-up voice of someone who had stopped trying to make bad news sound smaller than it was, because she'd noticed, somewhere in fifteen years of practice, that softening it only ever bought you a worse conversation later. "Or it's not only a Bleed. We're past that. We have been for some days, I think, and I'm sorry — I told you two weeks, and I believed it when I said it."

"What is it, then," Hesper said. Not a question, really. A demand for a word, the way you demanded a word for a diagnosis even when you already knew, in your body, that you weren't going to like it. She had gone very still where she stood by the window, the morning light catching the same blue-black dye stain still faintly visible under one fingernail, days old now, never quite scrubbed all the way out.

"Saturation," Petra said. "Early — and there's distance between early Saturation and anything past it, so don't run the numbers in your heads straight to the worst one, not yet. But it's Saturation, not Bleed, and Saturation doesn't resolve the way a Bleed does. Rest helps. Rest alone doesn't fix it. She needs sustained discharge work from someone who knows what they're doing with a case this far along. That's not me. I've handled Bleeds my whole career. I've handled exactly one Saturation case in fifteen years, and the woman who pulled that patient back wasn't me — a specialist out of Confluence proper, and I don't know if she's even still taking cases."

Wren found her hands had curled into fists at her sides without her noticing, and made herself unclench them, finger by finger, a small deliberate exercise she'd taught herself years ago for exactly this kind of moment — not because anyone was watching, but because the fists themselves felt like a want she hadn't given permission to exist yet, and giving it nothing to hold onto had become, by now, less a technique than a reflex.

"Then we find someone who is," Wren said.

"Start with the guild registries," Petra said, "and the Court's healing roster. Start soon. Saturation doesn't run on the kind of clock that rewards waiting to see." She picked her bag back up, hefted it onto her shoulder, and paused at the door a moment, looking back at Mari's closed bedroom door down the hall rather than at either of the two women in the room with her. "I wish I had better news than a list of places to start looking. I've wished that about a great many things this summer. It doesn't seem to be helping anyone, but there it is."

---

The guild registries had nothing, which was its own kind of education in exactly how the world treated problems that mostly happened to people without much standing. Wren spent two days in the Lantern Street archive room going through every case file the alchemists' guild had ever cross-filed with the healers' guild on the subject of advanced Saturation, in a low-ceilinged back room that smelled of dust and binding glue and the particular dry mustiness of paper nobody had asked to see in years, the only light a row of high, grimy windows that turned the whole room the color of weak tea by mid-afternoon. She worked through box after box with the same careful method she used on a wyrm scale's resonance log — date, source, outcome, no detail too small to record — watched over the whole time by an archivist named Dell who clearly considered Wren's presence a personal insult to a filing system he had spent thirty years perfecting, and who refiled each box the instant she set it down, sometimes before she'd fully finished with it, with the wounded precision of a man putting his house back in order after a minor natural disaster.

She found exactly four entries in thirty years, three of which ended in Dissolution and one of which simply stopped, mid-file, with a note in someone's careful hand reading *case transferred, see Court records* and nothing else. She copied the note out twice, in case the first copy was wrong, which it wasn't, and sat for a long minute afterward doing the specific, useless thing she did whenever a fact refused to resolve into anything more useful than its own existence: rereading it, as if rereading might make it longer. Her eyes had started to ache from the strain of close reading in poor light, a dull pressure behind both brows that no amount of blinking seemed to relieve, and she pressed the heels of her hands briefly against her closed lids, counted to ten, and went back to the page anyway, because stopping felt, at the moment, like a luxury that belonged to people whose sisters weren't dying.

"You're going to wear a groove in that page with your eyes," Dell said, from across the room, in the tone of a man who disapproved of enthusiasm on principle but had clearly decided, somewhere in the last hour, that hers was at least the disciplined kind. He had the look of a man who had spent thirty years in rooms like this one and had the dust to prove it, ink-stained at the second and third fingers of his right hand, spectacles pushed up onto a balding head he seemed to have forgotten he was wearing them on.

"It's a good note," Wren said. "It's a terrible note. It's the best terrible note I've found all week."

"Most people who come in here wanting Saturation files are wanting them for a thesis." He said this the way a man recites a fact he's recited many times before, a groove of his own worn smooth by repetition. "I get two, three a year. Apprentices, mostly. Want to write something clever about failure rates and call it scholarship."

"I'm wanting it for my sister."

Dell didn't say anything to that for a moment. He looked at her properly for the first time since she'd arrived, the kind of long, assessing look that made Wren resist the urge to fidget, and then he set down the box he'd been about to refile and didn't refile it, which from Dell, who'd had an opinion about everything else Wren had touched in two days, was its own kind of answer.

"There's a difference," he said finally, "between wanting a thing for a thesis and wanting it because you've got no other choice left. I can usually tell which is which inside the first hour. You've been in here two days and you haven't once asked me where the tea room is." He found her, without being asked, two more boxes she hadn't known to request, setting them down with rather more care than he'd shown the others. "Those are the cross-filed Court referrals. Most people don't know to ask for those by name. I'd have told you eventually. You've saved us both some time."

The Court's healing roster had more, on paper, and less in practice. She spent a full afternoon in the public record-hall at Confluence's heart, under the slow patient light of the rotunda's high windows — a building she'd visited perhaps twice before in her life, both times for guild business that had nothing to do with anything personal, and that she now found herself noticing properly for the first time: the worn groove in the marble threshold where two centuries of petitioners' feet had passed before her, the faint echo every footstep produced under that high domed ceiling, the way the whole building seemed designed to remind anyone who entered it how small their individual business was against the weight of all the business that had come before. She went through register after register of practicing Person-openers credentialed for advanced casework, copying names into her own ledger in the same careful hand she used for humidity readings, because a name, she had decided, was a kind of measurement too, and measurements were the one thing she still knew, with total confidence, how to take correctly — and found a dozen, and wrote to nine of them by the end of the week, on guild paper, in her most careful hand, with her credentials listed plainly at the bottom because she'd learned young that a letter from "W. Wickern, journeywoman, Lantern Street" got read with considerably more attention than one from "Mari's sister."

She heard back from four, all of whom said, in various careful phrasings, the same thing: a Saturation that far along, caught that late, in a healer who'd been running it for three weeks before anyone intervened, was outside what any of them had personally handled, and they were sorry, and they wished her family well. The letters arrived over the course of nine days, each one a small fresh disappointment she made herself open immediately rather than let sit unread on the kitchen table, because she had decided, somewhere in the second week of this, that the only thing worse than bad news was bad news delayed by her own cowardice. One added a postscript suggesting prayer, in a careful, well-meaning hand, which Wren read twice, set down in the same place she was starting to set down most things she didn't have time to feel properly yet, and did not mention to her mother.

It was the fifth one — a retired magistrate's clerk named Oswin Thale, who'd spent forty years filing Court records and had therefore, Wren suspected, simply seen more than the practicing healers themselves — who finally said the name.

She'd gone to him almost by accident, chasing down a reference in an old case file to a "Reed protocol" that appeared exactly twice in thirty years of Court healing records and was never once explained. His cottage sat on the quiet side of the river, past the last bridge, where the lanes narrowed and the houses settled into the specific unhurried shabbiness of a neighborhood full of people who had finished doing whatever they were going to do with their lives and had decided, collectively, that this was a fine way to spend what remained of them. A cat watched her from a windowsill two doors down with the same proprietary disdain Bram's neighborhood cats specialized in, as if cats the city over had all attended the same training.

Oswin received her in the cramped, paper-stacked front room of his retirement cottage — a room that smelled like dust and pipe-smoke and forty years of other people's official business, with stacks of bundled correspondence arranged on every flat surface in an order Wren suspected made perfect sense to exactly one person alive. He moved slowly, with the careful economy of a man who had learned which joints to favor, and gestured her toward the room's second chair, clearing a stack of papers off it first by simply transferring the stack, in its entirety, to an already towering pile on the floor beside him, the whole operation performed without any apparent anxiety about whether the new pile might topple. He peered at her over a pair of spectacles that had clearly outlived several prescriptions, and went very still when she read him the name from the file.

"Where did you find that," he said. Not sharply. Just carefully, the way you asked a question you already half wished you hadn't. He had set his pipe down on a small dish at his elbow the moment she'd read the name, unlit, and hadn't picked it back up.

"In a Court healing file from about twenty-six years back. It's the only place I've seen it. The case notes mention it once, like everyone reading it already knew what it meant, and then never explain it again."

"That's because everyone reading it did know what it meant, at the time," Oswin said. "It's not a protocol anyone wrote down properly, you understand. It's a name people used because saying the actual name made certain rooms go quiet." He folded his hands together on the table, an old clerk's gesture, the posture of a man settling in to dictate something he wanted recorded accurately. "I clerked that hall for forty-one years. I have a fairly reliable memory for which rooms go quiet, and why."

"Whose name." Wren heard her own voice come out smaller than she meant it to, and didn't try to fix it.

Oswin took his spectacles off, cleaned them on his sleeve with the unhurried deliberation of a man buying himself a few seconds, and put them back on, and when he spoke again his voice had settled into something Wren recognized from forty years of careful clerking — the cadence of a man who had spent his whole career making sure every fact he stated could survive being checked against a record later.

"Vesper Reed," he said. "You won't find her in the current roster. You won't find a current address in any guild record. Ask most people my age who still sit on Council committees and they'll tell you, quite sincerely, that they don't know what happened to her, and they'll be lying to you out of long habit rather than malice — nobody decided to lie about it on purpose, exactly. It's simply become the kind of thing nobody says out loud anymore. There's a difference between a secret and a silence everyone's agreed to by not agreeing to anything at all. This is the second kind."

Wren wrote that down, the exact phrasing, before she'd consciously decided to, because it seemed like the kind of sentence that deserved to survive intact rather than get smoothed over in her own later paraphrase.

"What did happen to her?"

Oswin was quiet for a moment, the particular quiet of a man assembling a story he had clearly told to himself many times in his own head and never once told out loud to another living person, deciding now, apparently, where exactly to begin it.

"She was the best magistrate the Court of Open Truth produced in my lifetime," he said. "Youngest ever seated, by some distance. Beloved — whole districts turned out just to watch her sit a case, the way you'd turn out for a wedding, three deep at the rotunda doors on a sitting day, which I never once saw happen for anyone else in forty years of clerking that hall. People brought their children to watch her work, the way you'd bring a child to see something rare and fine, a comet, a coronation. I clerked perhaps thirty of her sessions myself, in the years before it happened, and I can tell you honestly I never once saw her flinch from a case, no matter how raw it came into that room." He paused, and for a moment something that might have been old fondness moved across his face, quickly suppressed. "And then, twenty-six years ago, in open session, in front of the full Council, she went past Saturation in the middle of an inquest, in front of everyone, and what happened next is the part nobody agrees how to tell you."

"Did she Dissolve?"

"No. That's rather the point." Oswin reached for his pipe at last, turned it over once in his fingers without lighting it, a small fidget that seemed entirely out of character for a man who had otherwise sat through the whole conversation with his hands folded and still. "Somebody pulled her back — nobody official, the Court's own healers on duty that day couldn't, or wouldn't, or simply weren't fast enough, and whatever happened in the minutes after that, it left her able to walk out of that hall under her own power, which by every account should not have been possible at that stage. I was there, you understand. In the room. I have spent twenty-six years trying to describe to people what that looked like and have never once managed it to my own satisfaction, so I won't insult you by trying again now — only that it was not what anyone expects a rescue to look like, and that the Council that watched it happen never quite forgave her for needing it, even the ones who'd have died in her place gladly the week before."

"And then she was censured anyway, formally, within the month, and quietly disappeared from every roster the Court keeps. She's lived since then — as far as anyone admits to knowing — somewhere along the old mill channel south of the city, alone, and is, depending entirely on who in this city you ask, either a disgrace, a danger, or the only person currently breathing who actually knows how to do the thing your sister needs done."

Wren sat with that for a long moment, in the close, paper-smelling quiet of Oswin's front room, listening to a clock somewhere behind a stack of bundled correspondence tick through several seconds she didn't fill with anything, and felt something settle into place that she did not, yet, have a name for either. A fly knocked itself once, twice, against the window glass, the only other sound in the room besides the clock, and Wren found herself absurdly grateful for it, for any sound at all that wasn't the weight of what Oswin had just told her.

"Why was she censured," she said finally, "if she pulled herself back? If it worked?"

"Because the Court could never quite decide whether what she did to come back counted as a miracle or as the thing every magistrate in that hall had spent her whole career being warned against. Both readings are still argued at Council, twenty-six years on, by people who were in the room and people who weren't, usually after the third glass at whatever dinner brings it up." He took his spectacles off again, folded them, set them on the stack nearest his hand, a small gesture that had the weight of a man closing a file he didn't enjoy reopening. "You've come all this way and asked me a direct question, and you've earned a direct answer: I don't know which reading is true. I only know the woman who walked out of that hall was never quite the same woman who walked in, and that whatever she did, she's apparently done it for one or two others since, very quietly, very rarely, and never once for the Court's benefit again."

"Do you know how to find her?"

"I know where the mill channel runs," Oswin said. "I know she hasn't moved in twenty-six years, because the one time the Court tried to formally summons her for a committee review, eleven years back, the courier found the house without any trouble at all, which suggests a woman who isn't hiding so much as simply not expecting to be asked back." He got up, slowly, joints clearly protesting the movement, and crossed to a low writing desk crowded with yet more paper, where he found, after a moment's searching that seemed to cost him more patience than the search itself warranted, a small scrap with a few words inked on it in a hand much shakier than the one that had written everything else in the room, and held it out to her. "Third bend past the old grain mill, south bank. There's no marker. There's a gate that doesn't look like it leads anywhere, and it does."

Wren took the scrap and folded it once, carefully, and put it in the same inner pocket where she kept the things she didn't want to lose by accident.

He considered her for a moment, the particular consideration of a man who had spent four decades watching people walk into his front room wanting things, and had developed, over that time, a fairly reliable sense for which ones meant it. "I'll tell you one more thing, for free, since you've been patient with an old clerk's filing habits this whole hour. People in this city are very fond of saying her name like a warning. In forty years I never once heard anyone who'd actually known her say it that way. Make of that whatever you like."

"Did you know her? Personally, I mean. Not just from the rotunda floor."

Oswin's mouth did something complicated, not quite a smile. "Once," he said, "she remembered my daughter's name, eight months after she'd only heard it said once, in passing, in an entirely unrelated case. I don't know what that's worth to you. It's worth a great deal to me, still, all these years on. People aren't only the worst thing that ever happened to them, Miss Wickern, whatever the rest of this city has decided to believe about her. I'd keep that in your pocket too, on your way south."

---

The walk back from the quiet side of the river took the better part of an hour, and Wren spent most of it doing the thing she always did with a piece of information too large to hold all at once, which was breaking it into smaller pieces she could carry one at a time: the name first, plain, unornamented. Then the location, folded into her pocket. Then, last and heaviest, the shape of the actual choice now in front of her, which she made herself look at directly rather than around, the way she'd trained herself to look directly at a wyrm scale's resonance spike instead of flinching from it. By the time the upriver house came into view, low and dark against the last of the evening light except for one lit window in the kitchen, she had at least arrived at the question she needed to ask her mother, even if she hadn't yet found a way to ask it that didn't sound, out loud, exactly as frightening as it actually was.

She told her mother that night, in the kitchen of the upriver house, with Mari asleep two rooms away and the lamp turned low, the wick needing trimming, throwing a small unsteady light across the table that made both their faces look older than they were. The kettle Hesper had put on out of old habit, the habit of a woman who believed almost any conversation went better with something hot in your hands, sat forgotten and gone cold between them, because she had decided, somewhere on the walk back from the river ward, that she was not going to do the thing Mari had done — was not going to decide alone, quietly, that telling people would only worry them, and call that kindness.

Hesper's face did something complicated when Wren said the name. Not surprise, exactly. Hesper had lived in Confluence her whole life; she had clearly heard the name before, in whatever careful, half-finished way it got passed around among people her age, and had clearly already done some of her own arithmetic about what it might mean before Wren ever said it out loud. Her hands, which had been folded loosely on the table, went still and then, after a moment, gripped each other tightly enough that Wren could see the knuckles whiten in the low lamplight.

"No," she said.

"Mama —"

"I know what you're about to say, and the answer is no." Hesper's voice had gone tight and fast in a way Wren rarely heard from her, the voice of a woman holding a great deal of fear behind a very thin door. "Not because I don't love your sister enough to try anything. Because I love both my daughters, and I am not sending the second one into a house at the end of the mill channel to go knock on the door of a woman the entire Court of Open Truth has spent twenty-six years deciding not to talk about. There is a reason nobody says her name, Wren. There is always a reason, even when nobody can agree on what it is."

"I talked to a man today who knew her personally. He didn't say it like a warning."

"One man," Hesper said, "out of an entire city that's spent twenty-six years agreeing, very quietly, not to say it any other way. I'm not asking you to weigh those evenly, because they aren't even."

"Petra said herself she's never handled a Saturation this far along. The Court roster's got nothing. The guild's got four old case files and three of them ended in Dissolution. I'm not going because it sounds exciting, Mama, I'm going because I have spent two weeks asking every credentialed, respectable, properly licensed person in this city, and they have all, every one of them, told me they don't know how, and there is exactly one person left whose name keeps coming up who apparently does."

Hesper was quiet for a moment, looking down at her own joined hands rather than at Wren, and when she spoke again some of the tightness had gone out of her voice, replaced by something that sounded, to Wren's ear, much more like exhaustion than anger.

"And if she's dangerous?"

"Then I'll be careful," Wren said, and heard, as she said it, how thin that sounded, and said it anyway, because it was the truest thing she had. "I'm good at careful. It's the one thing I'm actually good at."

"That's not the one thing," Hesper said, sharper than she'd meant to, and then, more quietly, like she'd surprised herself: "You've never once, in twenty-three years, given yourself enough credit to know the difference between the one thing and one of several things, and I have never known how to argue you out of that without sounding like I'm handing you a compliment you'll just turn down."

Wren didn't have an answer for that. It sat in the kitchen between them for a moment, oddly heavier than anything else either of them had said that night, and Hesper looked, briefly, as though she wished she could take it back and say something smaller instead. The lamp wick chose that moment to gutter, throwing a brief, unsteady shadow across the table, and neither of them moved to trim it, both apparently willing to sit a moment longer in the imperfect light rather than break whatever fragile thing was happening between them to fix it.

"I used to think it was a phase," Hesper said, eventually, in a different voice altogether, lower, more careful, the voice Wren associated with her mother talking about the dye vats late at night when she thought nobody else was listening. "The scales. The ledgers. I thought, she'll grow out of it, the way you grow out of collecting river stones, and then I watched you not grow out of it for four years running, and I still don't entirely understand what it is you see in all that careful, patient, unglamorous work, but I have stopped believing it's a phase. I want you to know I've stopped believing that, before you go anywhere tomorrow. I don't want it sitting unsaid between us if something happens that I can't take back."

"Mama —"

"Let me finish. I'm not good at this part, and if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve and we'll both go to bed with it unsaid, and I've decided I don't want that either." She reached across the table and covered Wren's hand with her own, the dye long since scrubbed from her nails but the faint blue-black shadow of it still visible if you knew where to look, the way it never quite came out after thirty years in the trade. "You are careful in a world that doesn't reward carefulness nearly often enough. I have watched you be careful your whole life and get noticed for it exactly never, and I am ashamed, some days, that I let that go on as long as it has without saying something. I'm saying something now. Go careful tomorrow. But know that I see the carefulness. I have always seen it. I simply didn't always say so, and that was my failure, not yours."

Wren felt something move in her chest at that, something she didn't have a ready name for and didn't try, this once, to immediately starve into silence — not the old hot want of being compared unfavorably to her sister, nothing like that, something slower and warmer and harder to hold still long enough to examine, and she let it sit there a moment, unexamined, because for once examining it felt less important than simply letting it be true.

Hesper looked at her for a long moment after that — the particular look, Wren realized, that she'd been getting from her mother in smaller doses her whole life, ever since the day an assessing magistrate had asked an eleven-year-old to open toward a stranger's grief and watched her flinch shut instead, the look that was love and worry and something else Wren had never quite had a name for, something that might have been *I don't understand you, and I am trying very hard not to let that stop me from being proud of you anyway.*

"You won't be talked out of this," Hesper said. It wasn't a question either.

"No," Wren said. "I don't think I will."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment, and then reached across the kitchen table and took Wren's hand — not the careful, professional grip Wren used on Bartholomew, not the automatic warm clasp Mari had always had for everyone, but something else, something Wren didn't have nearly enough practice receiving to know quite what to do with.

"Then go careful," Hesper said. "And go in the morning. Not tonight. I'd like one more night where both my daughters are asleep under a roof I can see."

Wren agreed to that. It was, she thought, climbing the stairs sometime later with the lamp guttering low behind her, very possibly the last perfectly reasonable, perfectly careful thing she was going to manage for some time, and she intended, for one more night, to enjoy it.

She lay awake longer than she meant to in the narrow childhood bed, listening to the river running steady and unbothered past the windows, and made herself, before she let herself sleep, do one final accounting of the day — not the frightening parts, not yet, those could wait for the walk south tomorrow, but the ordinary parts, the parts that had nothing to do with Saturation or censure or a name nobody said out loud: the cat on the windowsill two doors from Oswin's cottage. The particular smell of his front room. Her mother's hand over hers on the kitchen table, warmer than she'd expected, and not at all like the careful, professional grip she gave Bartholomew, and not at all, either, like the easy automatic warmth Mari handed out to everyone — something else, something that had taken her mother twenty-three years to learn how to offer and Wren, perhaps, just as long to learn how to receive. She fell asleep somewhere in the middle of trying to find the right word for it, and did not, in the end, find one, and decided, drifting off, that this was probably allowed.
