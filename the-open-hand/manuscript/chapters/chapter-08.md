# Chapter Eight — The House on the Quiet Ward

They had moved Mari to the Quiet House while Wren was shut up in the dye-shed losing track of days, and nobody had thought to send word until it was already done, which told Wren most of what she needed to know about how the last four days had actually gone.

The Quiet House sat on its own narrow island where two of Confluence's lesser channels split and rejoined, the way the city's oldest civic buildings tended to — built, three centuries back, by people who'd understood that a practitioner too saturated to discharge safely needed more than rest. They needed *current*, clean and constant and close enough to draw the worst of it off without anyone having to lift a finger, and the old architects had simply built a house around that fact and let the water do work no healer's hands could match. It was, Wren thought, crossing the narrow footbridge in the late afternoon light, a genuinely beautiful building, all low eaves and open channels running directly beneath the floor, and she hated it on sight anyway, the specific helpless hatred of a building that meant her sister had gotten worse.

The keeper at the door was a thin, unhurried woman named Sela who introduced herself as senior attendant and asked, with the patient firmness of someone who had clearly had this exact conversation with a great many frightened family members, whether Wren had brought anything with her that the house's physician hadn't cleared.

"I have a theory," Wren said, "and a piece of confirmed wyrm-sign material, and I'm not going to pretend either of those things to you, because I think you'd see through it in about four seconds and I'd rather not waste the four seconds."

Sela looked at her for a long moment with an expression Wren couldn't immediately place, and then sighed in the specific way of someone revising an opinion mid-breath.

"You're the sister," she said. "The alchemist."

"I am."

"Petra Vance sent word about you three days ago. Said you'd gone looking for Vesper Reed's old work and might turn up here with something unsanctioned in your bag, and that we should hear you out fully before turning you away, because — and I'm quoting her directly, since I found it a strange thing for a healer to say about an alchemist — *she's the only one who's actually tried anything in two weeks, and everyone else has just been very competently doing nothing.*"

It was, Wren thought, possibly the kindest thing anyone outside her own family had said about her in years, and it came wrapped in exactly enough professional caution that she didn't let herself feel it too much.

"I'm not asking the house to sanction it," Wren said. "I'm asking to see my sister, and to explain to her, honestly, what I think I can do and what I don't know, and to let her decide. That's all. If she says no, I'll go, and I won't argue."

"And if she says yes?"

"Then I'd ask you to stay in the room. Not to stop me — to watch, and to pull me back if I go further than I mean to. I'd rather have someone competent in the room who doesn't trust me yet than nobody at all."

Sela considered that for longer than Wren expected, and then, finally, stood aside.

---

Mari's room was small and bright and built around a section of open channel running visibly beneath a grate in the floor, the water close enough that Wren could feel its low, steady pull on the air the moment she stepped inside — not painful, just present, the way a held breath was present even when nobody mentioned it.

Mari was awake, propped up against a stack of pillows, looking thinner than four days should have accounted for, and her face, when she saw Wren in the doorway, did the thing it had always done since they were children: lit up first, worried second, the order never once reversing no matter how bad things got.

"You look terrible," Mari said, by way of greeting, which from her was almost affectionate.

"I've been in a shed for four days arguing with a piece of pottery. I'm allowed to look terrible."

"Did you win the argument?"

"Eventually." Wren came and sat on the edge of the bed, the way she hadn't quite let herself do in Lowmarket, and took her sister's hand, and found it steadier than it had been that first terrible afternoon, though still too warm, still carrying that low metallic patience under the skin that Wren had learned, this past fortnight, to read the way she read a bad resonance signature. "I want to tell you something, and I want you to actually hear the whole thing before you say anything, because I think you're going to want to say yes before I finish, and I need you to wait."

"That's an alarming way to start a sentence."

"It's an alarming thing I'm about to propose." Wren told her — all of it, plainly, the way she'd promised her mother she would do things from now on: the ledger entry Corvin had copied out, the four days in the dye-shed, the working theory and its careful, hard-won numbers, and the gap, the real gap, between what she'd proven on a cracked clay vessel and what she had no way of testing short of trying it on an actual person who loved her back. She told her about the risk to herself — a real Bleed, possibly worse, if the buffer failed the way the dye-runoff had nearly failed on the third attempt. She did not soften any of it, and watched, the whole time, for the version of Mari that would say *no, absolutely not, I won't let you risk yourself for me* before any of the actual facts had finished landing, because that version of Mari existed, and Wren had spent four days bracing for her.

She didn't appear. Mari listened all the way through, quiet and focused in the particular way she went quiet for a patient's hardest news, and when Wren finally finished, she was silent for a long moment, looking down at their joined hands.

"You built this in four days," she said finally. "Alone. With no guild behind you."

"Corvin gave me the ledger. Mama gave me the journal. I built the rest."

"That's not nothing, Wren. That's not — " Mari stopped, started again, and Wren watched her sister do something she had genuinely never once seen her do in twenty-six years, which was visibly struggle to find words for her own feelings instead of immediately, gracefully, finding them for someone else's. "I have spent three years being the person who fixes things for this ward. Do you understand what it's like to lie in this bed and watch my little sister, who I have spent her whole life quietly worrying never wanted to be needed by anyone, build an entirely new theory out of nothing because she refused to let me be the one thing in the world she couldn't fix?"

"I'm not trying to fix you," Wren said, more roughly than she meant to. "I'm trying to buy you time. There's a difference, I think, though I'm still working out exactly where the line is."

"I know there's a difference," Mari said. "I've spent three years not believing there was one, for myself, and look where that's gotten me." She squeezed Wren's hand, the old warmth in it for a moment cutting clean through the fever-heat underneath. "Yes. Do it. I'm not saying yes because I think it's safe — you've just told me, very thoroughly, that it isn't. I'm saying yes because I think you've earned the chance to try the thing you actually built, instead of being talked out of it by people who are, with the best intentions in the world, still doing nothing."

Sela, by the door, made a small sound that might have been disapproval and might have been something closer to recognition.

"I'll stay in the room," she said, to no one in particular. "And I'll have the house's own healer standing by in the corridor, whether either of you likes it or not. Those are my terms, and they are not negotiable, and beyond that — " she looked at Wren for a long moment, the professional caution in her face giving ground, finally, to something more like respect " — I'll allow it."

Wren looked back down at her sister's hand in hers, too warm, steadier than it had any right to be, and felt the whole shape of the next hour arrive in her chest at once — fear, and something that was almost, despite everything, relief, the specific relief of finally being allowed to try the only thing she actually knew how to do.

"All right," she said. "Let's see if four days in a shed was worth anything."
