# Chapter Eight — The House on the Quiet Ward

They had moved Mari to the Quiet House while Wren was shut up in the dye-shed losing track of days, and nobody had thought to send word until it was already done, which told Wren most of what she needed to know about how the last four days had actually gone. She found this out from a boy at the upriver house's gate, breathless from running, who delivered the news and then stood there a moment looking faintly betrayed when Wren didn't immediately fall apart in front of him, as though he'd been promised a more dramatic audience for his errand and felt cheated of it. She gave him a coin and her best impression of calm and went straight to the river road without going inside to change, which meant she arrived at the Quiet House four days unwashed, smelling distinctly of madder root and lye, carrying a travel case in one hand and her grandmother's scale wrapped in cloth in the other, looking, by her own private assessment, considerably less like a woman about to perform an unsanctioned medical theory and considerably more like someone who had recently lost an argument with a dye vat.

The Quiet House sat on its own narrow island where two of Confluence's lesser channels split and rejoined, the way the city's oldest civic buildings tended to — built, three centuries back, by people who'd understood that a practitioner too saturated to discharge safely needed more than rest. They needed *current*, clean and constant and close enough to draw the worst of it off without anyone having to lift a finger, and the old architects had simply built a house around that fact and let the water do work no healer's hands could match. It was, Wren thought, crossing the narrow footbridge in the late afternoon light, a genuinely beautiful building, all low eaves and open channels running directly beneath the floor, and she hated it on sight anyway, the specific helpless hatred of a building that meant her sister had gotten worse. She also, despite herself, despite everything, spent a full thirty seconds on the footbridge cataloguing exactly how the channel grates were spaced beneath the floor joists, working out the flow rate from the sound alone, and only caught herself doing it when she registered how absurd it was to be doing structural appraisal of a building her sister was dying in. *You could not, apparently,* she thought, *turn the noticing off, even now. Not even for this.*

The keeper at the door was a thin, unhurried woman named Sela who introduced herself as senior attendant and asked, with the patient firmness of someone who had clearly had this exact conversation with a great many frightened family members, whether Wren had brought anything with her that the house's physician hadn't cleared.

"I have a theory," Wren said, "and a piece of confirmed wyrm-sign material, and I'm not going to pretend either of those things to you, because I think you'd see through it in about four seconds and I'd rather not waste the four seconds."

Sela looked at her for a long moment with an expression Wren couldn't immediately place, and then sighed in the specific way of someone revising an opinion mid-breath.

"You're the sister," she said. "The alchemist."

"I am."

"Petra Vance sent word about you three days ago. Said you'd gone looking for Vesper Reed's old work and might turn up here with something unsanctioned in your bag, and that we should hear you out fully before turning you away, because — and I'm quoting her directly, since I found it a strange thing for a healer to say about an alchemist — *she's the only one who's actually tried anything in two weeks, and everyone else has just been very competently doing nothing.*"

It was, Wren thought, possibly the kindest thing anyone outside her own family had said about her in years, and it came wrapped in exactly enough professional caution that she didn't let herself feel it too much.

"What exactly do you do here, if you don't mind my asking," Wren said, mostly to have something to say that wasn't the actual subject, "when an alchemist doesn't show up with a theory in her bag?"

"Mostly I keep people alive long enough for the channel to do the slow part of the work," Sela said. "I've sat with eleven Bleed cases in nine years. Three came back all the way. Four came back enough to manage. Four didn't." She said it without any visible weight on it, the flat economy of someone who had long ago stopped pretending the numbers could be softened by tone, and Wren understood, hearing it stated that plainly, that this was likely the single most honest sentence anyone had said to her in two weeks. "I tell you that so you understand exactly what kind of room you're walking into, and what kind of woman is about to decide whether to let you walk into it."

"I'd rather have the numbers than the comfort," Wren said. "I've had two weeks of comfort. It hasn't done much."

"No," Sela agreed. "I don't expect it has."

"I'm not asking the house to sanction it," Wren said. "I'm asking to see my sister, and to explain to her, honestly, what I think I can do and what I don't know, and to let her decide. That's all. If she says no, I'll go, and I won't argue."

"And if she says yes?"

"Then I'd ask you to stay in the room. Not to stop me — to watch, and to pull me back if I go further than I mean to. I'd rather have someone competent in the room who doesn't trust me yet than nobody at all."

Sela considered that for longer than Wren expected, studying her the way Wren imagined a person studied a bridge they were about to decide whether to cross, weighing weeks of secondhand reports against four unwashed days standing in front of her.

"Most people who come to me with an unsanctioned theory," she said finally, "spend the next ten minutes telling me why I should trust them. You haven't done that once."

"I don't think you should trust me," Wren said. "I think you should watch me closely and decide for yourself, which is a different thing, and the only honest version of trust I've got to offer anyone right now."

Something in Sela's face shifted, fractionally, in the direction of decision.

"Stand aside isn't quite the word for what I'm about to do," she said, "but it's close enough." And she stood aside.

---

Mari's room was small and bright and built around a section of open channel running visibly beneath a grate in the floor, the water close enough that Wren could feel its low, steady pull on the air the moment she stepped inside — not painful, just present, the way a held breath was present even when nobody mentioned it. There was a chair drawn up close to the bed that had clearly been slept in more nights than not, and a single half-finished piece of mending on the windowsill that Wren recognized, with a small ache, as her mother's habit transplanted wholesale into a room thirty miles from home.

Mari was awake, propped up against a stack of pillows, looking thinner than four days should have accounted for, and her face, when she saw Wren in the doorway, did the thing it had always done since they were children: lit up first, worried second, the order never once reversing no matter how bad things got.

"You look terrible," Mari said, by way of greeting, which from her was almost affectionate.

"I've been in a shed for four days arguing with a piece of pottery. I'm allowed to look terrible."

"Did you win the argument?"

"Eventually." Wren came and sat on the edge of the bed, the way she hadn't quite let herself do in Lowmarket, and took her sister's hand, and found it steadier than it had been that first terrible afternoon, though still too warm, still carrying that low metallic patience under the skin that Wren had learned, this past fortnight, to read the way she read a bad resonance signature. "I want to tell you something, and I need you to actually hear the whole thing before you say anything, because I think you're going to want to say yes before I finish, and I need you to wait."

"That's an alarming way to start a sentence."

"It's an alarming thing I'm about to propose." Wren told her — all of it, plainly, the way she'd promised her mother she would do things from now on: the ledger entry Corvin had copied out, the four days in the dye-shed, the working theory and its careful, hard-won numbers, and the gap, the real gap, between what she'd proven on a cracked clay vessel and what she had no way of testing short of trying it on an actual person who loved her back. She unwrapped the scale and set it on the blanket between them, and watched Mari look at it the way Mari looked at everything unfamiliar — with immediate, open curiosity rather than fear, which made it worse, somehow, not easier. She told her about the risk to herself, too: the third attempt, the dye-runoff leaning toward her own open hand instead of the vessel's, the small hungry tug that every text she owned called the exact failure mode that killed people. She did not soften any of it, and watched, the whole time, for the version of Mari that would say *no, absolutely not, I won't let you risk yourself for me* before any of the actual facts had finished landing, because that version of Mari existed, and Wren had spent four days bracing for her.

She didn't appear. Mari listened all the way through, quiet and focused in the particular way she went quiet for a patient's hardest news, her eyes moving once, briefly, to the scale on the blanket and then back to her sister's face, and when Wren finally finished, she was silent for a long moment, looking down at their joined hands.

"You built this in four days," she said finally. "Alone. With no guild behind you."

"Corvin gave me the ledger. Mama gave me the journal. I built the rest."

"That's not nothing, Wren. That's not — " Mari stopped, started again, and Wren watched her sister do something she had genuinely never once seen her do in twenty-six years, which was visibly struggle to find words for her own feelings instead of immediately, gracefully, finding them for someone else's. "I have spent three years being the person who fixes things for this ward. Do you understand what it's like to lie in this bed and watch my little sister, who I have spent her whole life quietly worrying never wanted to be needed by anyone, build an entirely new theory out of nothing because she refused to let me be the one thing in the world she couldn't fix?"

"I'm not trying to fix you," Wren said, more roughly than she meant to. "I'm trying to buy you time. Those aren't the same thing, and I'd like it noted that I figured that out somewhere around hour sixty, alone, talking to a beetle, which is not how I pictured learning it."

That got something out of Mari that was almost a laugh, thin and startled, the first genuine one Wren had heard from her in two weeks. "A beetle."

"A dye-beetle. Don't ask. It kept me company and didn't once try to talk me out of anything, which by the standards of my last two weeks made it the single most supportive presence in my life."

"I am going to need the full story of the beetle at some point. After this."

"You'll get the full story of the beetle," Wren said, "after this," and let the *after* sit between them a moment, doing more work than either of them said out loud.

"I know there's a difference," Mari said, more quietly now, sobering again. "Between fixing and buying time. I've spent three years not believing there was one, for myself, and look where that's gotten me." She squeezed Wren's hand, the old warmth in it for a moment cutting clean through the fever-heat underneath. "Yes. Do it. I'm not saying yes because I think it's safe — you've just told me, very thoroughly, that it isn't. I'm saying yes because I think you've earned the chance to try the thing you actually built, instead of being talked out of it by people who are, with the best intentions in the world, still doing nothing."

"Will it hurt you?" Mari asked, before Wren could answer, in a smaller voice than she'd used for any of the rest of it. "If it goes wrong. I need to know that part too, not just the part about you."

"I don't know," Wren said, because she'd promised herself, walking over the footbridge, that she would not give her sister a comforting lie dressed up as an answer. "I think — if it fails the way the third test almost failed — it goes toward me, not you. That's the entire design. You're the flood. I'm the hand on the other side of the buffer. If the buffer holds, you lose some of the force and I take the rest, in a controlled, survivable amount. If it doesn't hold, the worst of it still has to go somewhere, and I built this so that somewhere is me, on purpose, because I would rather it be me than you and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make either of us feel better about it."

Mari was quiet for a long moment, searching her sister's face for something, and whatever she found there seemed, finally, to settle her.

Sela, by the door, made a small sound that might have been disapproval and might have been something closer to recognition.

"I'll stay in the room," she said, to no one in particular. "And I'll have the house's own healer standing by in the corridor, whether either of you likes it or not. Those are my terms, and they are not negotiable, and beyond that — " she looked at Wren for a long moment, the professional caution in her face giving ground, finally, to something more like respect " — I'll allow it."

Wren looked back down at her sister's hand in hers, too warm, steadier than it had any right to be, and felt the whole shape of the next hour arrive in her chest at once — fear, and something that was almost, despite everything, relief, the specific relief of finally being allowed to try the only thing she actually knew how to do.

"Give me a few minutes to set the room the way I need it," she said to Sela, already moving, already reaching for the travel case, narrating the checklist under her breath the way she narrated everything that mattered to her — cradle-cloth, the brass measuring rule, the scale itself, repositioned twice before she was satisfied with the angle of the light on it. "And someone should probably go find whoever's in charge of the corridor and warn them that if they hear shouting, it's most likely just me arguing with the equipment again. It's a habit. I'd rather they not break the door down over it."

"Noted," Sela said, dry, already moving to do exactly that, and Mari, behind her, managed one more thin, real laugh before the room went, by careful degrees, quiet.

"All right," Wren said, when it was. "Let's see if four days in a shed was worth anything."
