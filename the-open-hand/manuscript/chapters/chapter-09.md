# Chapter Nine — What It Costs to Hold Open a Person

She set the scale on the small table beside the bed and arranged everything the way four days of testing had taught her to arrange it — within easy reach, nothing between her hand and the scale's surface, the channel water audible through the floor grate as a low constant reminder of where the room's own discharge ran if anything went wrong. Sela took up a position by the door, arms folded, watching with the particular stillness of someone who had decided to trust a thing she didn't yet understand and intended to revoke that trust instantly if it proved foolish. The house's own healer waited in the corridor, close enough to be inside the room in four steps.

"Tell me if you change your mind," Wren said, to Mari, one more time, because she had promised herself she would offer the door open until the exact moment it had to close. "Any point before I actually start. After that I don't know how clean a stop looks."

"I won't change my mind," Mari said, and there was something in her voice — steadier than the fever-warmth in her hand had any business allowing — that told Wren her sister had spent the last twenty minutes doing her own version of the careful work Wren had spent four days on, quietly making peace with a risk on her own terms rather than simply accepting Wren's.

"All right." Wren picked up the scale.

She had done this nine times on a cracked clay vessel and never once, in any of those nine times, felt anything resembling what happened the instant she opened toward her sister instead.

A clay vessel's flood, even the angriest, most deliberately overcharged version she'd built in the dye-shed, was a clean thing — directionless, unintelligent, just pressure looking for the nearest open channel. Mari's flood, when Wren opened the smallest, most careful possible channel toward it, was not clean at all. It came with shape. It came, impossibly, almost instantly, with twenty-six years of specific memory riding inside it — not as images, nothing so legible as that, but as *texture*, the particular density of a feeling that had been felt and set aside and felt again ten thousand times without ever once being allowed to finish, every patient Mari had ever sat with, every grief she'd ever absorbed and never put down properly, all of it pressing forward at once the second the door cracked open even a finger's width, the way water presses against a lock gate the instant the gate gives at all.

Wren got the scale between herself and that pressure on instinct rather than plan, faster than she'd practiced, and felt the old, patient saturation in it rise to meet the flood the way it had nine times before — and felt, in the same half-second, that nine cracked clay vessels had not remotely prepared her for the scale of what it was now being asked to hold.

It worked. That was the strange, vertiginous truth of the next several minutes: the theory held. The scale drank the worst of it, the way a long shallow reef drank the worst of a wave, and what reached Wren's own open hand, on the other side of all that old draining weight, was a fraction of what Mari was actually carrying — and the fraction alone nearly put her on the floor.

She had never felt anything like it. Not in four years of careful, controlled, eleven-measurements-first practice; not in any single moment of her entire deliberately narrow professional life. It was not pain, exactly, though her whole body reacted as though it were — chest tight, pulse going fast and uneven, a heat behind her eyes that had nothing to do with the room's temperature. It was *weight*. The specific, crushing weight of being, for one unguarded moment, the only thing standing between a drowning woman and the part of herself that was trying, gently and without malice, the way the wyrm scale's old saturation pressed without malice, to simply let go.

*This is what she carries every day,* some still-functioning corner of Wren's mind noted, with the same dispassionate precision she'd have used to log a humidity reading, even as the rest of her strained against the actual weight of it. *This is what she has carried, alone, for three years, and called it an ordinary Tuesday, and never once told anyone how much of herself it actually cost, because telling anyone would have meant admitting she needed the telling to stop.*

She felt her own pulse climb past anything she'd ever logged in a controlled session. She felt, distantly, her hand shaking against the scale's surface, the specific telltale tremor she'd diagnosed in other people's case files a hundred times without ever once feeling it arrive in her own. She felt — and this was the part that frightened her most, the part she would spend a long time afterward trying to find honest words for — the edges of herself begin to blur against the edges of what she was holding, not vanishing, not yet, but softening, the careful difference between *Wren feeling this* and *Wren being this* growing thinner with every second she stayed open.

"Wren." Sela's voice, from very far away, or perhaps from exactly where it had always been, with Wren simply no longer entirely certain how far away anything was. "You're Bleeding. I need you to hear me say that plainly. You are Bleeding, right now, and I need you to tell me you can hear the difference between that word and the alternative."

*Bleed.* The word had a shape, somewhere in the dispassionate logging-corner of her mind that hadn't entirely drowned. Bleed was recoverable. Bleed was the stage with a door still open behind it. She held onto the word the way she'd have held onto a confirmed measurement in a session gone wrong, a known fixed point in a field that had stopped, briefly, behaving the way fields were supposed to behave.

"I hear it," she said, or thought she said, and wasn't entirely sure, until Sela's hand closed around her shoulder, exactly how much of it had actually made it out of her mouth.

"Then close," Sela said. "Not all at once — I watched what you did with the scale, I think all at once would do more damage than good right now. Slow. The way you'd have done it with the clay, before any of this was real."

*The way you'd have done it with the clay.* It was, Wren understood even through the blur, exactly the right instruction, the only instruction that could actually reach her at this depth, because it asked her to return to something true and small and already proven rather than to simply will the unwillable. She found, somewhere under the crushing unfamiliar weight of her sister's whole uncounted grief, the same careful technique she'd used on Bartholomew a hundred times — *want it less, want it less, give it nothing to lean on* — and applied it, with hands that would not entirely stop shaking, to the channel she had opened on purpose for the first time in her adult life.

It closed slowly, in stages, the way a wound closes rather than the way a door slams — first the worst of the pressure, then the texture underneath it, then, last and hardest, the strange softened edge where she had briefly, frighteningly, not been entirely sure where she ended and her sister's exhaustion began. By the time it was fully shut she was on her knees on the floor of the Quiet House's small bright room, not entirely sure when she'd gotten there, with Sela's hand still steady on her shoulder and the house's own healer crouched in front of her checking her pulse with quick, professional fingers.

"There," Sela said, quietly, in the voice of someone who had clearly done this before, for someone else, at some other cost. "There you are. Stay on the floor a minute. The floor isn't going anywhere, and neither, I think, is your sister, which is rather the point of all this."

Wren turned her head, slow and unsteady, and found Mari watching her from the bed — awake, alert, color in her face that had not been there an hour ago, the metallic patient heat under her skin measurably, undeniably reduced.

"It worked," Mari said, wonderingly, and then, seeing Wren's face properly, considerably less wonderingly: "Wren. You're shaking."

"I'm fine," Wren said, in the exact tone, she realized even as she said it, that Mari had used in the Lowmarket sickroom three weeks ago, and heard, with a kind of horrified clarity, exactly how little that sentence had ever actually meant coming out of either of their mouths.

"You're not fine. You're Bleeding, Sela just said the word out loud, and you're sitting on a floor because your legs wouldn't hold you." Mari's voice cracked, finally, on something that wasn't composure at all. "I told you to tell me if you needed to stop, and you didn't, and now look at you."

"I didn't need to stop," Wren managed. "I needed to finish. There's a difference, I think — I'm fairly sure there's a difference, I'm just having some trouble right now explaining exactly where the line is." She found, despite everything, something close to a laugh in her own chest, thin and shaky but real. "You're better. Not fixed. Better. I bought you time, I didn't fix you, I want to be honest about that the second I can string a full sentence together, but you're better right now than you were this morning, and that's — " her voice gave out entirely for a moment, and she let it, on the floor of a Quiet House with the channel water running steady beneath the grate and her own pulse only now beginning to slow toward something normal " — that's the whole job. That's what I built. That's what you do, every single day, and never once let any of us see what it actually costs you to do it."

Nobody said anything for a long moment. Sela's hand stayed steady on her shoulder. The healer in the corridor finished checking her pulse and pronounced it, quietly, recovering. Mari, on the bed, was crying — openly, without composure, without performing reassurance for anyone in the room, for what Wren realized, watching her through her own blurred vision, might have been the first time in longer than either of them could probably name.

"I'm so tired," Mari said, to no one, to the room, to herself. "I don't think I've said that out loud in three years."

"I know," Wren said, and reached up, with a hand that had finally mostly stopped shaking, to find her sister's. "I know. I think I might be starting to understand exactly how tired, and I bought you maybe a week, and it is not going to be enough, and I am going to go find out what is."

She did not say the name. She didn't need to. Mari's hand tightened around hers, frightened and grateful in the same grip, and somewhere in that small bright room above the running water, Wren understood that the easy part — if any of the last four days could fairly be called easy — was already finished.
