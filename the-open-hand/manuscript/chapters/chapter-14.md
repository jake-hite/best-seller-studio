# Chapter Fourteen — The Reform Faction

Iris Hale sent for her two days later, in the unceremonious way the Councilwoman did everything — a single runner, a single line of text, *come to my office above the third bridge when you can, no urgency stated meaning considerable urgency intended* — and Wren went, still faintly buoyant from the quarry trip, still turning the great dark scale's resonance signature over in her mind like a puzzle she hadn't finished solving.

Iris's office was small, cluttered with petitions and placement-order files stacked in towers that looked structurally unwise, and Iris herself looked, as she always did, like a woman perpetually one committee meeting behind where she needed to be.

"You found Vesper Reed," she said, without preamble, the moment the door shut. "I know because Sela's contact is also, as it happens, one of mine, and because the reform faction makes a point of knowing exactly who goes looking for her and how soon. I'm not angry. I want to say that plainly before anything else, because I expect you've spent three weeks bracing for someone to be angry with you about this and I'd rather not waste either of our time confirming that fear."

"You knew the Court roster wouldn't have anything," Wren said. "Back when I first asked. You could have just told me her name."

"I could have. I chose not to, because I wanted you to arrive at her the way you did — through your own work, your own theory, your own desperation, rather than through a Council recommendation that would have made you, in her eyes and everyone else's, my project instead of your own." Iris pushed a stack of files aside and sat, gesturing Wren into the room's one other chair. "That's not the only reason I asked you here, though it's part of it. I have a case I think you can help with, and a piece of information I think you need to hear regardless of whether you take the case, and I'm honestly not certain which one matters more this afternoon."

The case, it turned out, had a name and a face: Cae Rookwood, eleven years old, sitting in the outer office with his mother when Iris finally brought Wren through to meet him, a quiet, watchful boy with the unmistakable loose, unfocused permeability of a true Natural — gifted from birth, never armored, never trained, the kind of raw openness the Armoring Houses existed specifically to contain.

"The placement order goes before the magistrate in nine days," Iris said, low, while Cae's mother spoke quietly to her son a few feet away. "The Houses' position is the usual one — an unarmored Natural is a danger to himself and anyone he opens toward without meaning to, and the only responsible thing is institutional containment until he's old enough to be properly trained. The reform position is that the Houses have spent three centuries treating *unarmored* as a synonym for *untreatable*, when half of what they actually do once a child is inside is exactly the kind of careful, patient resonance work your own guild does with inanimate material every day, just never offered to children because nobody important enough has bothered to ask whether a different method might work as well outside an institution as inside one. I need an alchemist's reading. A Material-opener's technical assessment of his actual resonance load, not a Person-opener's, because the magistrate trusts your guild's numbers in a way she's stopped trusting the Court's on cases like this one."

"Why me specifically?"

"Because you're the only Material-opener in Confluence with a documented theory for buffering an overextended Person-opener using nothing but trained material contact," Iris said. "If that theory holds for an adult in crisis, I'd put money on a careful version of it holding, far more gently, for a child who's simply never been taught to manage what he already has. You'd be the first person to actually offer him a method instead of a cage."

Wren crossed the room and crouched, without quite deciding to, to Cae's actual height rather than speaking down at him the way she suspected most adults in his recent life had managed without noticing they were doing it.

"Nobody's asked you yet, probably," she said. "What you actually want. I'd like to, if that's all right. Not what your mother wants, not what the Houses want, not even what I think might help you — just, plainly, what you want, since it's apparently your life everyone else keeps deciding about in rooms you're not allowed into."

Cae looked at her for a long moment with the careful, assessing wariness of a child who had learned, recently and thoroughly, that adults asking him questions usually already had the answer decided. "I want to stop feeling everyone in the building at once," he said finally. "Not to stop feeling things. Just — not all of it, all the time, with no wall anywhere. I don't want to go somewhere that locks the door to make that happen. I just want someone to teach me where the wall actually goes."

It was, Wren thought, possibly the single clearest sentence anyone involved in this entire matriarchal machine had said to her in three weeks, delivered by an eleven-year-old nobody official had apparently bothered to ask.

"I think I can help with that," she said. "Not today, and not by myself, probably, but I think the thing I'm building for someone else right now might actually work even better, gentler, for exactly what you just described. I'd like to try, if your mother's willing and you are. No locked doors. Just the wall."

Cae's mother's face, watching from a few feet away, did something complicated and grateful and exhausted all at once, and Iris, behind them, made a small satisfied note in the margin of a file that Wren suspected would become, within the week, a piece of formal testimony.

It was on the walk back through the outer office, with the case settled enough for one afternoon, that Iris caught Wren's sleeve and lowered her voice to something private.

"The other thing," she said. "I told you there was one. I want to be careful how I say this, because I respect what Vesper Reed has done for cases the Court abandoned, and I don't want you to hear this as a warning to abandon her, because I don't think that's the right move either, not with the clock you're on. But I'd be doing you a disservice if I let you walk further into this without telling you that she has a history. Documented, in the reform faction's own quiet files, the ones the Court won't touch and we won't make public because half the families involved asked us not to. There have been at least four young practitioners over the years who came to her exactly the way you did — desperate, out of official options, grateful for the first real help anyone offered them — and ended up Severed by her own hand, with her own sincere conviction that it was mercy. Two of them, as far as we've been able to track, are doing well now, by their own account. One won't speak to anyone from the reform faction at all, which we've chosen to read as her right rather than a verdict. And one—" Iris paused, picking her words with visible care. "One came to my office four years ago and asked me, very quietly, whether there was any way to undo it. I had to tell her there wasn't. I think about that conversation more than I'd like to admit."

Wren stood very still in the cluttered office doorway, the great dark scale's resonance still humming faintly somewhere in the back of her mind from yesterday, and felt something small and cold settle into her chest alongside it — not disbelief, exactly. Something closer to a fact arriving at a door that had been, until this moment, very thoroughly propped open.

"She told me herself she'd never force anyone," Wren said. "That whatever she asked for, she'd ask for plainly, and I'd be free to say no."

"I believe that's true," Iris said. "I believe she believes every word of it, completely, every time she says it. I also believe four people went into a room with her certain they were free to say no, and at least one of them came out the other side wishing she'd understood, while she still had the capacity to understand it fully, what saying yes would actually cost her to take back." She let go of Wren's sleeve, gently. "I'm not telling you to stop trusting her. I don't think that's a decision I get to make for you, and I think, given what's actually at stake for your sister, it might not even be the wrong choice. I'm telling you because I think you're the kind of person who'd rather carry an uncomfortable fact and decide what to do with it yourself than be protected from it by someone who's decided, on your behalf, that you can't handle knowing."

Wren thanked her, and meant it, and walked the long way home along the river rather than the short way through the lanes, turning the fact over the entire distance the way she'd turn over an inconclusive resonance reading — real, noted, not yet disproven, not yet acted on either, filed carefully alongside the quarry's gold afternoon light and Vesper's unguarded, wistful voice saying *I noticed*, in the specific quiet corner of her mind where she kept things she had decided, for now, not to look at directly.
