# Chapter Fourteen — The Reform Faction

Iris Hale sent for her two days later, in the unceremonious way the Councilwoman did everything — a single runner, a single line of text, *come to my office above the third bridge when you can, no urgency stated meaning considerable urgency intended* — and Wren went, still faintly buoyant from the quarry trip, the great dark scale's resonance signature still humming somewhere at the back of her attention like a held note she hadn't finished listening to.

Iris's office was small, cluttered with petitions and placement-order files stacked in towers that looked structurally unwise, and Iris herself looked, as she always did, like a woman perpetually one committee meeting behind where she needed to be.

"You found Vesper Reed," she said, without preamble, the moment the door shut. "I know because Sela's contact is also, as it happens, one of mine, and because the reform faction makes a point of knowing exactly who goes looking for her and how soon. I'm not angry, so you can put that back down — I watched you carry it in with you from the doorway, and I genuinely don't have the spare hour today for both of us to perform around it."

"You knew the Court roster wouldn't have anything," Wren said. "Back when I first asked. You could have just told me her name."

"I could have. I chose not to, because I wanted you to arrive at her the way you did — through your own work, your own theory, your own desperation, rather than through a Council recommendation that would have made you, in her eyes and everyone else's, my project instead of your own." Iris pushed a stack of files aside and sat, gesturing Wren into the room's one other chair. "That's not the only reason I asked you here, though it's part of it. I have a case I think you can help with, and a piece of information I think you need, and I'm honestly not certain which one matters more this afternoon. The case first. It's better company."

The case, it turned out, had a name and a face: Cae Rookwood, eleven years old, sitting in the outer office with his mother when Iris finally brought Wren through to meet him, a quiet, watchful boy with the unmistakable loose, unfocused permeability of a true Natural — gifted from birth, never armored, never trained, the kind of raw openness the Armoring Houses existed specifically to contain.

"The placement order goes before the magistrate in nine days," Iris said, low, while Cae's mother spoke quietly to her son a few feet away. "The Houses' position is the usual one — an unarmored Natural is a danger to himself and anyone he opens toward without meaning to, and the only responsible thing is institutional containment until he's old enough to be properly trained. The reform position is that the Houses have spent three centuries treating *unarmored* as a synonym for *untreatable*, when half of what they actually do once a child is inside is exactly the kind of careful, patient resonance work your own guild does with inanimate material every day, just never offered to children because nobody important enough has bothered to ask whether a different method might work as well outside an institution as inside one. I need an alchemist's reading. A Material-opener's technical assessment of his actual resonance load, not a Person-opener's, because the magistrate trusts your guild's numbers in a way she's stopped trusting the Court's on cases like this one."

"Why me specifically?"

"Because you're the only Material-opener in Confluence with a documented theory for buffering an overextended Person-opener using nothing but trained material contact," Iris said. "If that theory holds for an adult in crisis, I'd put money on a careful version of it holding, far more gently, for a child who's simply never been taught to manage what he already has. You'd be the first person to actually offer him a method instead of a cage."

Wren crossed the room and crouched, without quite deciding to, to Cae's actual height rather than speaking down at him the way she suspected most adults in his recent life had managed without noticing they were doing it.

"I'm Wren," she said. "I work with old, complicated objects that hold onto feelings the way you apparently do, except mine don't argue with me about bedtime or have opinions about anything, which I'll admit is restful but also a little boring some days. Nobody's asked you yet, probably. What you actually want. Not what your mother wants, not what the Houses want. Just you."

Cae looked at her for a long moment with the careful, assessing wariness of a child who had learned, recently and thoroughly, that adults asking him questions usually already had the answer decided.

"I don't want to stop feeling things," he said, finally, like it was important she get that part exactly right before anything else. "Just — everybody. All the people in the building, all at once, all the time. It's not even like feelings, really. It's more like noise. Like being at the bridge market on the loudest day, except it doesn't stop, and you can't go home and shut the door." He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve, not looking at her. "I don't want a locked door. I had a dream once about a locked door and it didn't help anything, it just meant I couldn't get out either, so it was worse, actually, not better." A pause, smaller. "I just want somebody to show me how to turn some of it down. Like — I don't know. A volume thing. Does that make sense? Probably not. Nobody else thought it made sense either."

"It makes more sense than almost anything anyone's said to me in three weeks," Wren said, "and I've been talking to guild masters and Court officials this entire time, so that's a genuinely low bar you just cleared by a wide margin." That got the smallest flicker of something from him, not quite a smile, but close enough that she counted it. "I work with a wyrm scale at my own bench. Three hundred years old. It used to belong to an actual dead king's spine, which I promise is exactly as strange to say out loud as it sounds, and it has never once, not one single time, told me how to feel about it, which is the single best quality any coworker has ever had. I think the thing I'm building for someone else right now might work for exactly what you just described — gentler, smaller, no cage, no locked door. Just the volume thing. I'd like to try, if your mother's willing and you are."

Cae's mother's face, watching from a few feet away, did something complicated and grateful and exhausted all at once, and Iris, behind them, made a small satisfied note in the margin of a file that Wren suspected would become, within the week, a piece of formal testimony. Cae himself studied Wren for one more long moment, with the particular gravity of a child deciding whether an adult had passed some private, unstated test, and then nodded, once, like a contract being signed.

"Okay," he said. "But I want to see the scale first. Before anything. I want to know what it feels like before you put it anywhere near me."

"That," Wren said, "is the single most sensible request anyone involved in this entire case has made, including me, and I'll bring it myself, next time, and you can hold it for as long as you want before either of us does anything else."

It was on the walk back through the outer office, with the case settled enough for one afternoon, that Iris caught Wren's sleeve and lowered her voice to something private.

"Here's the other thing," she said, "and I'd rather hand it to you straight than wrap it in committee language, because you've earned that much today. I respect what Vesper Reed has done for cases the Court abandoned. This isn't a vote to drop her — not with the clock you're on, and I wouldn't presume to cast that vote for you regardless. But you should have the file before you go any further, the same as I'd want it handed to me if our positions were reversed." She glanced once toward the door, as though checking old habit rather than any real fear of being overheard. "There have been at least four young practitioners over the years who came to her exactly the way you did — desperate, out of official options, grateful for the first real help anyone offered them — and ended up Severed by her own hand, with her own sincere conviction that it was mercy. Two of them, as far as we've been able to track, are doing well now, by their own account. One won't speak to anyone from the reform faction at all, which we've chosen to read as her right rather than a verdict. And one came to my office four years ago and asked me, very quietly, whether there was any way to undo it. I had to tell her there wasn't. I think about that conversation more than I'd like to admit."

Wren stood very still in the cluttered office doorway, the great dark scale's resonance still humming faintly somewhere at the back of her attention, and felt something small and cold settle into her chest alongside it — not disbelief, exactly. Something closer to a fact arriving at a door that had been, until this moment, very thoroughly propped open.

"She told me herself she'd never force anyone," Wren said. "That whatever she asked for, she'd ask for plainly, and I'd be free to say no."

"I believe that's true," Iris said. "I believe she believes every word of it, completely, every time she says it. I also believe four people went into a room with her certain they were free to say no, and at least one of them came out the other side wishing she'd understood, while she still had the capacity to understand it fully, what saying yes would actually cost her to take back." She let go of Wren's sleeve, gently. "I'm not telling you to stop trusting her. I don't think that's a decision I get to make for you, and given what's actually at stake for your sister, it might not even be the wrong choice. I'm telling you because you strike me as the kind of person who'd rather carry an uncomfortable fact and decide what to do with it herself than be protected from it by someone who's decided, on her behalf, that she can't handle knowing."

Wren thanked her, and meant it, and walked the long way home along the river rather than the short way through the lanes, working the fact the whole distance the way she'd work a stubborn snarl out of wet thread — pulling at it gently, getting nowhere useful, refusing to cut it and refusing, just as stubbornly, to set it down and leave it alone. She did not arrive at a conclusion by the time the upriver house came into view, gold afternoon light still caught somewhere behind her eyes from the quarry, Vesper's unguarded, wistful *I noticed* still sitting warm in her chest alongside the new, colder weight of *one came to my office four years ago and asked me whether there was any way to undo it.* She put the whole knotted mess of it down anyway, the way a person sets aside work they can't finish in one sitting, and told herself, without quite believing it yet, that she'd pick it back up tomorrow with steadier hands.
