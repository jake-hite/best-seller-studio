# Chapter Fourteen — The Reform Faction

Iris Hale sent for her two days later, in the unceremonious way the Councilwoman did everything — a single runner, a single line of text, *come to my office above the third bridge when you can, no urgency stated meaning considerable urgency intended* — and Wren went, still faintly buoyant from the quarry trip, the great dark scale's resonance signature still humming somewhere at the back of her attention like a held note she hadn't finished listening to. She'd spent the walk over composing, out of habit, a short list of things she expected the summons to be about — Mari's case, the buffer's official guild standing, some new wrinkle in the Court's silence — and arrived at Iris's building having covered none of the actual subject, which she was beginning to suspect was simply how every important conversation in her life went lately.

The stairs up to Iris's office smelled of old paper and the particular mineral tang of river-damp that crept into every building this close to the third bridge no matter how often anyone aired the rooms out, and the third step from the top creaked in a way Wren had learned, after one visit, to step around rather than through. Iris's office was small, cluttered with petitions and placement-order files stacked in towers that looked structurally unwise — one tower in particular had acquired a precarious lean since Wren's last visit, and she found herself eyeing it the entire time she spoke, half-convinced she was about to witness its collapse — and Iris herself looked, as she always did, like a woman perpetually one committee meeting behind where she needed to be, ink on two fingers and her hair escaping its pins on one side only, as though she'd been interrupted mid-thought by someone else's crisis and never quite finished her own grooming afterward.

"You found Vesper Reed," she said, without preamble, the moment the door shut. "I know because Sela's contact is also, as it happens, one of mine, and because the reform faction makes a point of knowing exactly who goes looking for her and how soon. I'm not angry, so you can put that back down — I watched you carry it in with you from the doorway, and I genuinely don't have the spare hour today for both of us to perform around it."

"I wasn't aware I was carrying anything."

"You were carrying your shoulders somewhere up around your ears the entire walk up my stairs, which I could hear, incidentally, because that third step announces every visitor in this building whether they want it to or not." Iris said it without unkindness, already clearing a space on her desk with the brisk efficiency of someone who did this dozen times a day and had stopped finding it tedious purely through repetition. "You knew the Court roster wouldn't have anything. Back when I first asked. You could have just told me her name."

"I could have. I chose not to, because I wanted you to arrive at her the way you did — through your own work, your own theory, your own desperation, rather than through a Council recommendation that would have made you, in her eyes and everyone else's, my project instead of your own." Iris pushed a stack of files aside and sat, gesturing Wren into the room's one other chair, which wobbled faintly on an uneven leg the moment Wren's weight settled into it. There was a folded scrap of parchment wedged under the short leg already, Wren noticed, evidence of some previous visitor's attempt at a fix that had only partially taken, and she found herself absurdly tempted to ask whether she could improve on it, the way she was tempted to fix any small mechanical wrongness that crossed her attention, before reminding herself that she had not, in fact, been summoned to repair Iris Hale's furniture. "That's not the only reason I asked you here, though it's part of it. I have a case I think you can help with, and a piece of information I think you need, and I'm honestly not certain which one matters more this afternoon. The case first. It's better company."

"That's an ominous way to introduce the second thing."

"It is. I'm aware. I've found, over eleven years on this Council, that telling someone good news first and bad news second doesn't actually make the bad news any smaller, it just gives them a nicer memory of the five minutes before they heard it. I'd rather be efficient about which order I do it in than pretend the order changes anything." Iris said this with the particular brisk fondness she seemed to reserve for her own cynicism, the tone of someone who had made peace with being right about unpleasant things a long time ago.

The case, it turned out, had a name and a face: Cae Rookwood, eleven years old, sitting in the outer office with his mother when Iris finally brought Wren through to meet him, a quiet, watchful boy with the unmistakable loose, unfocused permeability of a true Natural — gifted from birth, never armored, never trained, the kind of raw openness the Armoring Houses existed specifically to contain. He sat very still in the outer office's one good chair, knees together, hands folded in his lap in the particular careful posture of a child who has been told, repeatedly and by several different adults, to sit nicely while the grown-ups talk, and who has decided, with visible effort, to comply rather than fight it. His mother sat beside him, a worn satchel of what Wren guessed were the family's collected paperwork balanced on her knees, and the look she wore — exhausted past the point exhaustion usually stopped, the specific exhaustion of someone who had been fighting the same fight for longer than anyone outside the fight understood — was one Wren recognized immediately, because she'd worn a version of it herself for three solid weeks.

"The placement order goes before the magistrate in nine days," Iris said, low, while Cae's mother spoke quietly to her son a few feet away, smoothing a crease out of his collar that didn't strictly need smoothing, the kind of small unnecessary tending a parent does with their hands when there's nothing larger left to do. "The Houses' position is the usual one — an unarmored Natural is a danger to himself and anyone he opens toward without meaning to, and the only responsible thing is institutional containment until he's old enough to be properly trained. The reform position is that the Houses have spent three centuries treating *unarmored* as a synonym for *untreatable*, when half of what they actually do once a child is inside is exactly the kind of careful, patient resonance work your own guild does with inanimate material every day, just never offered to children because nobody important enough has bothered to ask whether a different method might work as well outside an institution as inside one. I need an alchemist's reading. A Material-opener's technical assessment of his actual resonance load, not a Person-opener's, because the magistrate trusts your guild's numbers in a way she's stopped trusting the Court's on cases like this one."

"Why me specifically?"

"Because you're the only Material-opener in Confluence with a documented theory for buffering an overextended Person-opener using nothing but trained material contact," Iris said. "If that theory holds for an adult in crisis, I'd put money on a careful version of it holding, far more gently, for a child who's simply never been taught to manage what he already has. You'd be the first person to actually offer him a method instead of a cage."

"Nine days isn't very long to build anything new."

"No. It isn't. I'm not asking you to build anything new today — I'm asking you to go and say hello to a frightened child and tell me honestly afterward whether what you've already built has any business near him at all. If the answer's no, I need to know that today, not eight days from now." Iris said it plainly, no softening, which Wren had already come to recognize as the particular shape of her kindness — never less than direct, even when directness cost something. "Come on. He's been waiting long enough."

Wren crossed the room and crouched, without quite deciding to, to Cae's actual height rather than speaking down at him the way she suspected most adults in his recent life had managed without noticing they were doing it. Up close, he had a faint scattering of ink on one knuckle, the kind that came from a leaking pen rather than any actual writing practice, and his shoes were laced in the lopsided, over-tightened way of a child who tied his own shoes and hadn't quite mastered the knack of it yet, small details that made him, for one disarming moment, simply a boy rather than a case file.

"I'm Wren," she said. "I work with old, complicated objects that hold onto feelings the way you apparently do, except mine don't argue with me about bedtime or have opinions about anything, which I'll admit is restful but also a little boring some days. Nobody's asked you yet, probably. What you actually want. Not what your mother wants, not what the Houses want. Just you."

Cae looked at her for a long moment with the careful, assessing wariness of a child who had learned, recently and thoroughly, that adults asking him questions usually already had the answer decided. Behind him, his mother's hand had stilled on his collar, as though she too had noticed that this particular adult had asked the question in a way that expected an actual answer rather than a performance of one, and was waiting, same as Wren, to see what he'd do with that. Cae glanced once at his mother, a quick checking look, the kind a child gives before deciding whether the room is actually safe to say a true thing in, and something in her face — Wren couldn't see what, exactly, only the back of it from where she crouched — seemed to settle the matter for him.

"I don't want to stop feeling things," he said, finally, like it was important she get that part exactly right before anything else. "Just — everybody. All the people in the building, all at once, all the time. It's not even like feelings, really. It's more like noise. Like being at the bridge market on the loudest day, except it doesn't stop, and you can't go home and shut the door." He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve, not looking at her. "I don't want a locked door. I had a dream once about a locked door and it didn't help anything, it just meant I couldn't get out either, so it was worse, actually, not better." A pause, smaller. "I just want somebody to show me how to turn some of it down. Like — I don't know. A volume thing. Does that make sense? Probably not. Nobody else thought it made sense either."

"It makes more sense than almost anything anyone's said to me in three weeks," Wren said, "and I've been talking to guild masters and Court officials this entire time, so that's a genuinely low bar you just cleared by a wide margin." That got the smallest flicker of something from him, not quite a smile, but close enough that she counted it, and she added it, on instinct, to the small running tally she kept of anyone she meant to work with — *responds to dry humor delivered flatly, does not respond to being talked down to, has clearly already decided adults are not to be trusted on the first pass* — the same kind of working notes she'd take on an unfamiliar scale before she touched it. "I work with a wyrm scale at my own bench. Three hundred years old. It used to belong to an actual dead king's spine, which I promise is exactly as strange to say out loud as it sounds, and it has never once, not one single time, told me how to feel about it, which is the single best quality any coworker has ever had." She paused, considering how much technical truth an eleven-year-old actually wanted handed to him versus how much he wanted handed to him plainly, and decided, on no evidence beyond his face, that he was the kind of child who'd rather have the plain version even if it cost him some comfort. "I think the thing I'm building for someone else right now might work for exactly what you just described — gentler, smaller, no cage, no locked door. Just the volume thing. I'd like to try, if your mother's willing and you are. I won't promise you it'll work the first time, because I don't make promises about things I haven't tested yet, not even to be kind. But I'll tell you the truth about whether it's working, every single time you ask me, and you can ask me as often as you want."

Cae considered this with the same gravity he'd given everything else, weighing, Wren suspected, not the offer itself but the unusual experience of an adult declining to oversell it to him. A clock somewhere in Iris's outer office ticked through several full seconds of him doing nothing but think, visibly, the way some children thought with their whole bodies rather than just their faces — a small frown, a slight forward lean, one hand coming up to worry at the same loose thread on his sleeve he'd picked at before, as though the thread itself were doing some of the work his mind hadn't finished yet.

Cae's mother's face, watching from a few feet away, did something complicated and grateful and exhausted all at once — the particular expression, Wren thought, of someone who has spent so long bracing for the next disappointment that an actual offer of help arrives almost as a physical shock, something the body has to catch up to before the face can sort out what to do with it — and Iris, behind them, made a small satisfied note in the margin of a file that Wren suspected would become, within the week, a piece of formal testimony. Cae himself studied Wren for one more long moment, with the particular gravity of a child deciding whether an adult had passed some private, unstated test, and then nodded, once, like a contract being signed.

"Okay," he said. "But I want to see the scale first. Before anything. I want to know what it feels like before you put it anywhere near me."

"That's fair," his mother said quietly, the first thing she'd said since Wren had crouched down, and there was something in the careful neutrality of her voice — not pushing him toward yes, not pushing him toward no, simply confirming that his condition was a reasonable one to set — that told Wren more about why this particular woman had kept fighting this particular fight for as long as she had than anything Iris's case file could have summarized.

"That," Wren said, "is the single most sensible request anyone involved in this entire case has made, including me, and I'll bring it myself, next time, and you can hold it for as long as you want before either of us does anything else. I'll even let you ask it questions, if you want, though I should warn you it doesn't answer back. Mostly it just sits there being old and dignified, which I've found is two-thirds of what makes a good coworker anyway."

That earned the smallest huff of something that wasn't quite a laugh but lived in the same neighborhood, and Cae's mother reached over and smoothed his collar one more time, the same unnecessary gesture, though this time her hand didn't quite shake the way it had a few minutes before.

It was on the walk back through the outer office, with the case settled enough for one afternoon and Cae's mother already gathering the satchel of paperwork she would, Wren suspected, carry to every meeting for the rest of this process whether or not anyone ever asked to see it again, that Iris caught Wren's sleeve and lowered her voice to something private.

"Here's the other thing," she said, "and I'd rather hand it to you straight than wrap it in committee language, because you've earned that much today. I respect what Vesper Reed has done for cases the Court abandoned. This isn't a vote to drop her — not with the clock you're on, and I wouldn't presume to cast that vote for you regardless. But you should have the file before you go any further, the same as I'd want it handed to me if our positions were reversed." She glanced once toward the door, as though checking old habit rather than any real fear of being overheard, the kind of small instinctive caution Wren imagined got built into a person after enough years of saying things in this particular building that weren't meant to travel past the room they were said in. "There have been at least four young practitioners over the years who came to her exactly the way you did — desperate, out of official options, grateful for the first real help anyone offered them — and ended up Severed by her own hand, with her own sincere conviction that it was mercy. Two of them, as far as we've been able to track, are doing well now, by their own account. One won't speak to anyone from the reform faction at all, which we've chosen to read as her right rather than a verdict. And one came to my office four years ago and asked me, very quietly, whether there was any way to undo it. I had to tell her there wasn't. I think about that conversation more than I'd like to admit."

Iris let that sit for a moment, unhurried, the way someone hands over a heavy parcel and then waits, out of plain courtesy, for the other person's arms to actually settle around the weight of it before letting go entirely.

Wren stood very still in the cluttered office doorway, the great dark scale's resonance still humming faintly somewhere at the back of her attention, and felt something small and cold settle into her chest alongside it — not disbelief, exactly. Something closer to a fact arriving at a door that had been, until this moment, very thoroughly propped open. She thought, with the specific unhelpful clarity of a mind reaching for the wrong thing to hold onto first, of the great dark scale still resting in its case at the lock-house, and of Vesper's hands closing carefully around it two days ago, and could not, for one disorienting moment, decide whether the memory had gotten colder in the retelling or whether it had always carried this exact temperature and she simply hadn't been looking at it closely enough to notice.

"She told me herself she'd never force anyone," Wren said. "That whatever she asked for, she'd ask for plainly, and I'd be free to say no."

"I believe that's true," Iris said. "I believe she believes every word of it, completely, every time she says it. I also believe four people went into a room with her certain they were free to say no, and at least one of them came out the other side wishing she'd understood, while she still had the capacity to understand it fully, what saying yes would actually cost her to take back." She let go of Wren's sleeve, gently. "I'm not telling you to stop trusting her. I don't think that's a decision I get to make for you, and given what's actually at stake for your sister, it might not even be the wrong choice. I'm telling you because you strike me as the kind of person who'd rather carry an uncomfortable fact and decide what to do with it herself than be protected from it by someone who's decided, on her behalf, that she can't handle knowing."

"How do you know that about me? We've met three times."

"Because you walked into my office today already braced for bad news you hadn't been told yet, and you let me give you the good news first without once trying to rush past it to find out what the bad news was," Iris said. "Most people do the opposite. Most people would rather have the dread over with quickly than enjoy five honest minutes first. You didn't. That tells me plenty." She gathered the files on her desk back into their towers, restoring the precarious lean Wren had been watching all afternoon as though it were a piece of familiar furniture rather than a structural risk. "Go home. Think about Cae if you want something useful to think about instead. He's a better use of an afternoon than I am, most days."

Wren thanked her, and meant it, and walked the long way home along the river rather than the short way through the lanes, working the fact the whole distance the way she'd work a stubborn snarl out of wet thread — pulling at it gently, getting nowhere useful, refusing to cut it and refusing, just as stubbornly, to set it down and leave it alone. The river ran low and brown with late-season runoff, unhurried, indifferent to whatever anyone walking alongside it was carrying, and she found herself watching its surface more than she watched the path, the way she sometimes watched a resonance reading scroll past on a gauge when she didn't yet trust herself to look directly at the number it would eventually settle on. She passed the dye-works, shuttered for the evening, and the lamplighter's boy working his way along the embankment rail with his long pole and his bored, methodical competence, and a woman selling the last of the day's river-fish off a barrow who called out a price Wren didn't hear properly and didn't ask to have repeated. None of it helped. She had not expected it to. She catalogued, instead, without quite meaning to, every small ordinary kindness Vesper had shown her across four visits — the unhurried patience in the kitchen, the careful hands around an injured wrist, the *I noticed* offered like a confession nobody had asked her to make — and set each one, one at a time, next to *four young practitioners* and *one came to my office four years ago* and *I had to tell her there wasn't,* and found that none of them cancelled any of the others out, which was somehow worse than if one side had simply won.

She did not arrive at a conclusion by the time the upriver house came into view, gold afternoon light still caught somewhere behind her eyes from the quarry, Vesper's unguarded, wistful *I noticed* still sitting warm in her chest alongside the new, colder weight of *one came to my office four years ago and asked me whether there was any way to undo it.* She put the whole knotted mess of it down anyway, the way a person sets aside work they can't finish in one sitting, and told herself, without quite believing it yet, that she'd pick it back up tomorrow with steadier hands. Inside, Bartholomew met her at the door with the specific outraged dignity of a cat who believed dinner to be overdue by a criminal margin, and she fed him before she did anything else, including light a single lamp, because some problems could wait and an indignant cat, in her experience, could not.

Bram found her on the dye-shed step a little before full dark, a covered dish balanced in one hand that she suspected, correctly, held more food than she'd managed to eat in the last two days combined, and didn't ask what was wrong before settling himself on the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

"Heard you went up to see Iris Hale today," he said, plain, the way he said most things. "Heard it from three separate people before noon, which by the time it reaches me usually means it arrived a good deal stranger than whatever actually happened."

"This one didn't need the exaggeration." She gave him the compressed, honest shape of it — not the whole conversation, just the four names she didn't have and the one sentence she couldn't put down afterward. *I had to tell her there wasn't.* Bram listened the way he always did, without once reaching to fix it or make it smaller than it was, and when she finished he sat with her a while in the dark without saying anything at all, which was, she'd learned over two years, the thing he was actually good at and never once advertised.

"I've got a bad afternoon of my own, if it helps to hear somebody else's," he said eventually. "The dye commission for the guild's autumn order. Mordant won't set past half-strength no matter what I do to it, due in two days, and if it's still doing this tomorrow night I'm explaining to the guild hall why an entire run came out the wrong color." He said it plainly, no real complaint in it, the way he reported most disasters — a fact to be solved rather than a feeling to be managed. "Two nights running on it alone. I think I've stopped being able to see what's wrong with it, the way you stop being able to read your own handwriting if you stare at it too long."

She thought, with the particular precision she brought to every decision that actually mattered, of the practice sessions she'd allotted herself that night and the next morning, the coat-scrap waiting on her own bench, the careful four-day schedule she'd built for Cae with no margin left anywhere inside it. She thought about saying so. She thought about how easily she could have said it a year ago, and meant it, and gone back inside to her own bench and her own larger problem, because it was, by any honest measure, the larger problem, and being right about that would have been its own kind of relief.

"Show me the bath," she said instead, "and bring the dish in with you. I'm not going to be much use to either of us as the kind of person who only shows up when nothing else happens to be asking for her at the same time."

It took until well past midnight — a stuck ratio that wanted nothing more complicated than half a degree's more heat and the kind of patience neither of them had been bringing to it alone — and she walked home afterward under a sky gone fully dark, doing the arithmetic she hadn't let herself do in front of him: one practice session lost outright, the other compressed into whatever hours were left before she was due at Cae's, not nearly enough sleep in between to make up the honest difference. She did not regret it, exactly. She set it down instead in the small, specific category of costs she'd have paid again anyway, and let herself, for once, stop pretending the ledger had balanced when it plainly hadn't.
