# Chapter Seventeen — The Offer

Mari slept through the whole of the following day, properly, the kind of sleep that didn't flinch at footsteps or lower voices, and Wren sat with her for most of it doing nothing more demanding than watching her sister's chest rise and fall, grateful in a way that had no room left over for anything else.

She kept a private, useless ledger of it anyway, because that was apparently what she did with gratitude now when it had nowhere else to go: in at four breaths to the count of five, out at five to the count of six, a rhythm so ordinary it would have meant nothing at all written down on any other day of her life and meant, on this one, everything. She had brought actual paper down at one point, early in the afternoon, fully intending to use the stillness to catch up the inventory she owed the guild, and had instead spent forty minutes simply watching the rise and fall of the blanket over her sister's ribs, the small domestic miracle of a chest that kept doing the unglamorous thing it was built to do without anyone in the room having to fight for it. Sela brought her bread at midday and took away the same plate, untouched, without comment, which Wren understood, distantly, to be its own kind of kindness — the kind that didn't require her to perform appetite she didn't have in order to be left alone.

Her own body, she noted somewhere in the dull middle hours, had not fully recovered from two days ago either, whatever the calendar said about it. Her hands had a faint, persistent unsteadiness when she held them out flat that hadn't been there before the Bleed, gone within a few seconds if she gave it nothing to feed on, the same trick she used on every other unwanted thing, but present, every time, the first half-second she checked. Her appetite had come back wrong-shaped — ravenous at odd hours, indifferent at meals — and the base of her skull held a low, persistent ache that wasn't quite pain so much as the memory of pain, deciding whether to reassert itself. She catalogued all of it the way she catalogued everything, out of habit more than diligence, and decided none of it was urgent enough to mention to anyone whose actual job was tracking symptoms more dangerous than her own.

It was evening before Vesper found her again, in the Quiet House's small kitchen, nursing tea she'd long since let go cold, having reheated it twice already out of the specific stubborn refusal to admit a cup of tea had defeated her on top of everything else the week had managed. The kitchen at that hour smelled of the dried mint Sela kept strung along the rafter beam and, underneath that, the same patient mineral coolness that ran through the whole house — channel-damp, Wren had started calling it privately, the smell of a building that had made peace, generations ago, with the fact that it would never be entirely dry.

"I want to talk to you properly," Vesper said, sitting across from her without being invited, which Wren found she didn't mind nearly as much as she once would have. She moved a chair the careful half-inch it needed to clear the table leg before she sat in it, an oddly domestic precision that didn't match the size of what she was about to say, and Wren found herself noticing the mismatch the way she noticed an instrument reading one number too clean to trust. "Not about yesterday's crisis, which is past, for now. About tomorrow's, and the one after that, because yesterday is going to keep happening, on a shortening schedule, until something fundamental changes rather than something temporary improving."

"I know," Wren said. "I've been doing the arithmetic all day, same as I did the first week. The buffer's better than it was, even refined, even seeded properly. It's still not a cure. It's a slower bridge to the same river." She turned the cold cup once on its saucer, a small mechanical fidget she allowed herself because the alternative was sitting with her hands as still and as obviously braced as they wanted to be. "I keep doing the same sum and getting the same answer, which is the thing about arithmetic that I used to find comforting and have started, this week specifically, to find almost unbearable. Two more buffers, maybe three, before the curve outruns whatever I can seed into a scale fast enough to keep up with it. I've checked the numbers four times tonight alone, hoping I'd done one of them wrong. I hadn't."

"There is a cure," Vesper said. "I want to tell you about it plainly, the way I've told you everything else, and I want you to hear the whole shape of it before you let yourself feel anything about it, because I think what I'm about to offer you is going to feel, the instant you understand it, like the easiest yes you've ever been asked to give, and I would rather you make that decision with your eyes fully open than out of relief."

Wren set down the cold tea, and made herself notice, deliberately, the precise way her stomach had gone tight at the word *cure* — not hope exactly, something closer to the particular dread of standing at the top of a ladder someone has just told you is perfectly sound. "Tell me."

"Not yet," Vesper said, and something in her voice had gone careful in a way Wren hadn't heard from her before, the unhurried warmth replaced for a moment by something that sounded almost like reluctance. "I want to say one thing first, badly enough that I'd rather risk you thinking me evasive than skip it. I have rehearsed versions of this conversation in my head more times than I'd like to admit to you, on the walk over, and every single version of it sounded, even to me, like exactly the kind of thing a person says right before they ask for something enormous. I don't have a way to make it sound like anything else. I only have the choice of saying it plainly anyway, or not saying it at all, and I've decided plainly is the only version of me worth being tonight."

"Just tell me," Wren said again, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she expected, steadier, certainly, than her stomach currently felt.

"Your sister's Saturation isn't going to stop accelerating. Every honest measurement I've taken says the same thing your own arithmetic does. What she needs isn't another bought week. She needs a permanent open conduit — someone who can stand where you stood in that room yesterday, indefinitely, without it costing them a Bleed every single time, without the risk eventually killing the person doing the holding instead of the person being held." Vesper folded her hands on the table, the same composed, patient gesture Wren had noticed the very first afternoon. "I can teach you to be that person. Properly. Permanently. The way I am for the handful of people I've helped over the years. You would never have to fear a Bleed again. You could open toward her — toward anyone — fully, as often as it was needed, and the flood would simply never be able to drown you, because the part of you that drowning requires would no longer be reachable by anything but your own choosing."

Wren sat with that for a moment, turning the shape of it over the only way she had ever known how to turn anything over — looking for the load-bearing joint, the place the whole structure would fail first if she pressed on it. She found, to her own faint dismay, that she didn't immediately know where to press. "Say the actual word," she said. "Whatever it is you've been calling it in your own head this whole time you've been deciding how to say it to me. I'd rather have the word than the description of the word. I've gotten good, this past month, at telling the difference between the two, and I think you know that about me by now."

"You're talking about Severance."

"I am. I want to say the word myself, plainly, rather than let you arrive at it and wonder if I was hoping you wouldn't notice." Vesper's voice did not change at all, which was, Wren thought distantly, either the most honest or the most practiced thing about her. "I am offering to sever the link between what you feel and what you're obligated to do about it, the same way it was done for me, twenty-six years ago, by someone who understood that the alternative — go on as you are, bleeding a little more of yourself into every rescue until eventually there isn't enough left to bleed — was not actually the safer choice it's always dressed up as."

"And what does it cost," Wren said, "because everything in this world that opens a door also costs something to walk through, you taught me that yourself, in your own kitchen, two weeks ago."

"It costs the part of you that experiences feeling as a debt," Vesper said. "Not the feeling itself — I want to be precise about this, because it is the single most common misunderstanding about what I do and I will not let you carry it. You will still feel everything, completely, in full resolution, the day after as much as the day before. What changes is the obligation underneath it — the part of you that hears your sister's exhaustion and experiences it as a demand you have no right to refuse. That part goes quiet. Not gone. Quiet."

"Define quiet," Wren said. "I'm sorry. I know that sounds like I'm cross-examining you. I think I am cross-examining you, a little, and I think you'd be disappointed in me if I weren't, given what you've spent a month teaching me about not accepting a measurement I haven't pushed on myself."

"I would be considerably more worried if you weren't." Something that might almost have been approval crossed Vesper's face, there and gone, before the careful patience underneath it reasserted itself. "Quiet the way a loud room goes quiet once you've stepped outside it and shut the door. The room is still loud. You can still see it through the glass, still know exactly how loud it is if you let yourself think about it. You are simply no longer standing inside the noise, required to respond to every part of it the instant it reaches you. You decide, afterward, every single time, whether to act on what you feel, rather than being pulled along behind it the way a current pulls along anything that hasn't learned to stand apart from it." She held Wren's gaze, steady, unflinching. "I am not telling you this will cost nothing. I have told you from the very first afternoon that it cost me a great deal, and I meant it, and I will not pretend otherwise now simply because the offer is sincere. I am telling you it would let you save her, this time and every time after, without ever again sitting on a floor wondering whether your own legs would hold you. I think that is worth being honest about, even though I also think it is the most dangerous sentence I have said to you yet."

"You keep saying that," Wren said. "That it's the most dangerous sentence so far. You've said some version of it three times tonight already, and we've barely started."

"I expect I'll say it a fourth time before we're finished, and mean it slightly more each time. I'm not performing humility about the size of this, if that's what you're asking. I genuinely don't know a smaller way to hand it to you."

Wren sat with it for a long moment, the kitchen's lamp throwing soft light over the table, the channel water audible through the floor the way it was audible everywhere in this house, patient and constant and entirely unbothered by anything happening above it. She found herself listening to it on purpose, the way she might have listened to a held resonance reading while she waited for the rest of an instrument to settle, and was distantly grateful that something in the room, at least, had absolutely nothing at stake tonight.

The terrible thing — the thing she made herself sit with fully rather than flinch away from, the way she'd promised herself weeks ago she would do with every hard fact from now on — was that it was tempting. Genuinely, completely tempting, in a way no strawman offer from a storybook villain had ever prepared her to recognize in her own chest. She thought of the floor of Mari's first sickroom, her own legs not holding her, the specific crushing weight of being the only thing standing between her sister and the part of herself trying gently to let go. She thought of yesterday, the formless static flood, the scale gone hot in her hands, Sela's voice saying *pull back, there isn't going to be a clean stopping point this time.* She thought of doing this again, and again, for years, every time Mari's clock ran down, every time some other patient nobody official would help needed exactly the kind of hands Wren had built — and never once again being afraid that the cost would be more than she had to spend.

She thought, too, with the specific shame of a thought she would not have said aloud to anyone, including Vesper, of how much simpler her own life would become. No more catching herself mid-flinch at a customer's careless compliment and spending the rest of the afternoon talking herself down from it. No more standing in a doorway deciding whether a crying guildmate was a problem that belonged to her. She could be useful in the exact, bottomless way Mari was useful, without ever paying Mari's price for it — without the part of her that would, eventually, inevitably, also burn down to nothing, the way her sister so nearly had. It was, she thought, turning the cup another quarter-turn on its saucer, an almost unbearably tidy answer to a question she'd been failing to solve cleanly for four years before any of this had even started: *how much of other people am I actually required to let in.* Severance answered it permanently. Severance answered it so permanently that the question simply stopped being a question at all, which was, she suspected, most of what made it dangerous — not that it promised relief, but that it promised the relief of never having to ask again.

"I could keep doing this forever," she said slowly, "and never be afraid of it again."

"Yes."

"That's not a small thing to offer someone who's been afraid every single day for three weeks."

"I know precisely how large a thing it is," Vesper said. "I have spent twenty-six years on the other side of having accepted it, and I have never once, not for a single day, regretted that I am no longer ruled by the fear the way I once was. I want you to hear that as the truth it is, and not assume I'm about to undercut it with some quiet confession of misery, because I won't. The cost was real and I would, faced with the same choice again tonight, make exactly the same one."

"Not even one day," Wren pressed. "Not even a bad one. I'm not trying to catch you in something, I want to say that plainly, the way you'd say it. I'm asking because I think I'd believe you less if you said yes too quickly than if you took a moment to actually check."

Vesper did take the moment — visibly, deliberately, her eyes going somewhere past Wren's shoulder, into whatever private archive twenty-six years had given her to search. "There was a winter, perhaps the eighth year, when a woman I'd helped died anyway, of something entirely unrelated, ordinary, a fever that had nothing to do with anything I'd ever touched in her. I went looking, that week, for the version of grief I remembered having as a younger woman, the kind that used to keep me from sleeping for days at a time, because some old habit in me believed I owed her that much weight. I couldn't find it. I felt sad. I felt it completely, I want to be precise about that. I did not feel *consumed*, the way I once would have been, and for about a month afterward I genuinely did not know whether that was the gift working exactly as intended or whether I had simply become a person incapable of being consumed by anything ever again, and I have never, in eighteen years since, fully resolved which of those two things was true." She looked back at Wren, steady again. "That is the closest thing I have to the day you're asking about. I offer it to you exactly as uncomfortably as it sits in me."

It would have been easier, Wren thought, if Vesper had lied about that — if some crack of obvious regret had shown itself, some tell she could point to and say *there, that's the proof, that's why I'm going to say no.* Vesper gave her nothing so clean. She simply sat, composed and warm and entirely sincere, offering the single most dangerous gift Wren had ever been handed with the plain, unhurried generosity of someone who genuinely believed it was the right thing to give — and underneath the generosity, now that Wren had gone looking for it on purpose, the one small, honest crack Vesper had just shown her without being asked twice.

"I need to think," Wren said finally. "I want to be honest with you the way you've been honest with me. I'm not saying no. I'm not saying yes either. I think if I answered you right now, in this exact minute, sitting across from you with my sister finally asleep down the hall for the first time in days, I'd say yes, and I don't trust an answer that arrives that fast, about something that doesn't come with an undo."

"How long," Vesper asked, "do you think you'll need."

"I don't know yet. Longer than tonight. Possibly longer than tomorrow. I'd rather give you an honest *I don't know* than a number I made up to sound more decisive than I feel, because I think you'd see through the made-up number in about four seconds and then we'd both have wasted the time it took me to say it."

"That," Vesper said, with something that might almost have been pride, "is the single most sensible thing anyone has said to me in this conversation in twenty-six years of having it with other people. Take however long you need. I am not going anywhere, and your sister has, by my own honest measurement, slightly more time than she did yesterday, if not very much more. I would rather you arrive at the right answer slowly than the easy one quickly."

"You're not going to push," Wren said. It came out somewhere between a statement and a question.

"No. I want to be exact about why, since I suspect you're waiting to find the angle in it. I have watched what it costs a person to arrive at this decision pushed, rather than walking to it on her own feet, and I am not willing to spend that cost on you simply because waiting is harder on me than insisting would be. If that sounds like a kindness, you may take it as one. It is also, plainly, the only version of this offer I have ever believed in enough to make twice."

She rose to go, then paused at the kitchen doorway, and for one unguarded moment something crossed her face that Wren hadn't seen there before — not the warmth, not the composure, something underneath both of those things, old and tired and, for just a second, almost afraid.

"For what it's worth," Vesper said, "I would like you to say yes. I want to be honest about that too, since I've asked you to be honest with me about everything else. I think you would be extraordinary at this, more naturally suited to it than I was at your age, and I think I am, in some part of myself I don't examine as often as I should, tired of doing this alone. But I would rather you say no, fully understanding what you'd be refusing, than say yes because you were too exhausted tonight to imagine any other way through tomorrow. That part is not a kindness I am extending to you. It is simply the only kind of yes I have ever found worth having."

She left, and the front door's latch made its small, ordinary click, and the house settled back into the patient channel-sound it had been making all evening, as though nothing in it had just been offered the most dangerous gift of her adult life across a kitchen table.

Wren sat alone in the small kitchen for a long time afterward, the cold tea entirely forgotten this time, testing the offer from every angle she knew how to test anything from — structurally, the way she'd test a vessel for a hairline crack before she trusted it with anything volatile, running her thumb along an imagined seam the way she might have run it along an actual one, looking for the give that meant a thing wasn't what it claimed to be. She made an actual list at one point, on the back of an old guild requisition slip she found in her coat pocket, the paper soft and creased from weeks of being carried around half-forgotten: *Reasons to say yes* on one side, *Reasons to say no* on the other, the way she'd once decided, at nineteen, whether to specialize in metals or organics, because some habits survived even the largest possible stakes completely unchanged. The yes column filled quickly — no more Bleeding, no more sitting on a floor with her legs gone to water, years of Mari's safety bought outright rather than borrowed week by week. The no column sat for nearly ten minutes with nothing written under it at all, which frightened her considerably more than any single argument Vesper had made all evening, until she finally wrote, in handwriting noticeably less steady than her ledger hand, *I don't know who's asking me to say yes anymore. Her, or the part of her that needs someone to say it.* She looked at that sentence for a long time, decided it was the only entry on either side of the page that actually mattered, and found, unsettlingly, that the flaw she went looking for refused, for the moment, to show itself anywhere else she pressed.

She caught herself, somewhere past midnight, narrating the entire shape of the problem out loud to an empty kitchen — gesturing with the requisition slip for emphasis, at one point, to a chair nobody was sitting in, the old four-year habit surfacing exactly the way it always did whenever something mattered too much to think about silently. *Here's the thing, Bartholomew,* she heard herself say, perfectly seriously, to the cold hearth, *she's not lying to me. That's the whole problem. If she were lying I'd have found the seam an hour ago and gone to bed. You'd have found it faster than I did, you're considerably less sentimental than I am about people who compliment my technique.* She got two more sentences into the argument — something about resonance density and the specific dishonesty of an offer that was completely honest and still wrong — before she heard herself doing it and had to laugh, once, short and a little wild, at the specific indignity of explaining the most dangerous decision of her adult life to a dead king's spine that was, this particular evening, thirty miles away in its brass cradle on Lantern Street and entirely unable to argue with her about it, or smugly disapprove of her sentence structure, or do any of the small useless things she'd apparently come to rely on it for more than she'd ever admitted to Corvin. She decided, against all her own better professional habits, that she missed him anyway — missed the workshop, missed the ledger, missed the specific boring safety of a problem that could be solved with eleven measurements and patience rather than a decision that would not undo — and went up to bed no closer to an answer than she'd been at sundown, but considerably less alone in not having one.
