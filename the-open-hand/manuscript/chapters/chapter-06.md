# Chapter Six — The Locked Cabinet

Corvin found her at the river crossing the next morning, which meant he'd either guessed her route or asked someone who'd seen her go, and either way had clearly spent more thought on the matter than a man washing his hands of a problem generally bothered to spend. The crossing was quiet at that hour, just a fisherman two piers down working a line he didn't seem especially invested in and the river doing the unhurried, indifferent thing it did every morning regardless of who was standing at its edge deciding the shape of the rest of her life.

He was on foot, hatless, carrying a satchel that looked, from twenty feet away, exactly like the kind of thing a guildmaster carried when he wanted to be able to say afterward, with a straight face, that he hadn't brought anything official.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said, before she could ask. "Wanted that said first. Saves us both an hour of you bracing for a fight I'm not picking."

"All right."

"I went back to the cabinet last night," he said, "after you'd gone, and sat with it a long while, the way I do most nights, if I'm honest, since Yvet. And I decided — here's exactly where the line sits, so there's no mistaking it later — I still won't hand you confirmed material with the guild's name attached. I stand by every word I said yesterday about what that name's worth and what I'm responsible for spending it on. But I went through the ledger instead. Every confirmed sighting, every provenance record, every note any alchemist in this guild's whole history has ever made about resonance density near an open wound, going back further than either of us has any business knowing." He held the satchel out. "It's copied. In my own hand, not the guild's official register, which means it's mine to give away and nobody else's business if I do. Call it an old man's private correspondence with a former colleague, if anyone ever asks, which they won't, because nobody's going to ask me about my correspondence."

Wren took the satchel and felt, under the weight of it, something in her chest loosen slightly for the first time since the workshop door.

"Why," she said, simply, because she'd learned, this past week, that the plain question usually got her further than the careful one.

"Because I went home last night intending to feel relieved that I'd done the responsible thing," Corvin said, "and instead I lay awake doing the same arithmetic you've apparently been doing for two weeks, and arrived, somewhat against my own preference, at the same answer. There is no one else. I checked, in my own way, same as you did in yours — sent word to two colleagues in Brindle who'd know if anyone else alive had handled a case like this, and got back exactly what you got from the Court roster, which is regretful silence. I don't love the conclusion. I love it considerably less than I loved my comfortable, well-justified refusal yesterday. But refusing to help you find better information isn't caution anymore at that point. It's just leaving you alone with worse information, which I find I can't actually stand behind, whatever it costs my own peace of mind."

"You could come with me."

"I could," Corvin agreed, "and I've thought about it, and I won't, for a reason I'd like you to actually hear rather than just accept. If I come with you, I bring the guild with me whether either of us intends it or not — I am the guild, as far as most of Confluence is concerned, and whatever happens at the end of that mill channel will happen, in everyone's mind, with Lantern Street's hands on it. You need to be able to do this as yourself. Not as my apprentice, not as the guild's representative, not even, forgive me, primarily as Mari's sister. As Wren Wickern, who knows this material better than I do at this point, whatever I said yesterday about hobbies, and who is entitled to find that out on her own ground rather than mine."

Wren opened the satchel right there at the crossing, unable to wait, while the fisherman two piers down reeled in nothing in particular and the morning went on not caring about either of them, and found exactly what Corvin had promised — page after page in his blunt, economical hand, decades of guild knowledge nobody had ever bothered to take out of the locked cabinet and actually compare against itself, cross-referenced now, overnight, by a man who'd apparently decided that one sleepless night of real effort was a fair price for not having to live with having done nothing.

There, near the back, in a section headed simply *Anomalous Resonance — Living Subjects*, was a single entry that hadn't been in any of the Court files or guild registers she'd already combed through: a note, undated, unsigned, in a hand even older and more cramped than her grandmother's earliest volumes, describing a technique for using a saturated material as a *buffer* rather than a conduit — not to absorb a flooding person's overextension directly, which every text agreed was suicidal, but to interpose, to give the flood somewhere old and patient and already saturated to lean on instead of the helper standing nearest.

*The error every prior attempt has made,* the note read, in Corvin's careful transcription of whatever crabbed original hand had written it a century back, *is in believing the helper must be the vessel. A vessel that is already full cannot be filled further without breaking. But a vessel that is already full can be set between the flood and the helper, and the flood, being patternless and seeking only somewhere to go, will choose the path of least resistance — and an old, saturated material, properly woken, offers considerably less resistance than a living person's still-active fear of breaking.* Below it, in the same hand, a single underlined caution: *I have tried this exactly once, on myself, with a vessel I judged sufficiently saturated, and it held. I do not know if it would hold a second time, with a different vessel, or a different flood, or a less careful hand than my own, and I have elected not to find out.*

Wren read it through twice, then a third time, feeling the specific, electric, entirely undignified thrill she associated with finding a genuinely new idea in someone else's hundred-year-old handwriting — the same thrill she'd had at sixteen reading her grandmother's notes on silt and memory, except sharper now, because this one had a shape she could actually use. "Whoever wrote this tried it *once*," she said, mostly to herself, "and then just — stopped. Wrote down that it worked and never touched it again. Do you know how maddening that is? That's the single most interesting sentence I've read in two weeks of research and the person who wrote it apparently decided one success was plenty and went back to filing paperwork."

"Some people," Corvin said, "have the sense to be frightened of their own results. It's a survivable habit. You might try it sometime."

"I'm trying it right now. I'm extremely frightened. I'm also going to test this within the week, possibly within the day, which I understand is not the same thing as being appropriately frightened, but it's the version of frightened I've got."

"It's old," Corvin said, more seriously, nodding at the page in her hands. "Older than the guild's current cabinet protocols, certainly older than me. I found it folded inside the back cover of the eldest confirmed ledger, no attribution, no date past a watermark that puts the paper at sometime in the last century. I don't know who wrote it, or whether it was ever tried again after that one time, or whether the reason it's never been copied into the official register is that it didn't work twice, or that it worked too well and frightened whoever tried it into burying it. I'm giving it to you anyway, because you asked for theory, and this is, as far as I can find, the only theory in three hundred years of this guild's records that even gestures at the shape of what you're trying to do."

"Thank you," she said finally, and meant considerably more by it than the words alone carried.

"Don't thank me yet," Corvin said. "Thank me if it works, and forgive me if it doesn't, and either way, come back to Lantern Street when this is finished, whichever way it finishes. Your bench is still yours. I told you that yesterday and I find I meant it more than I expected to." He hesitated, in the doorway-shaped way of a man unused to saying the next thing out loud, looking out at the river instead of at her. "Yvet would have liked you, I think. She'd have argued with half your conclusions and helped you test the other half anyway, probably before breakfast, probably without asking my permission either." A short, dry breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's the most useful thing I know how to tell you this morning, so I'm telling you, and then I'm going home, because I find I've used up whatever nerve I had for one day."

"Corvin." She said it before he could finish turning, and he stopped, half-away from her already. "You called me the most capable hand to come through Lantern Street in a decade yesterday. You meant that as an argument against letting me go."

"I did."

"It didn't work as an argument. But I'd still like to have heard it from you eventually, on a day that wasn't an argument at all."

Something in his face did the thing it almost never did, which was nearly soften all the way. "Noted," he said. "File it under things I'm bad at saying on purpose, and bring your sister back, and I'll find a calmer day to say it again properly."

He turned and went without waiting for her to answer further, which Wren understood, watching him go, was its own kind of gift — the chance to stand at a river crossing with her grandmother's whole life's work in one hand and her guildmaster's careful, complicated faith in the other, and not have to find the right thing to say about either of them just yet.
