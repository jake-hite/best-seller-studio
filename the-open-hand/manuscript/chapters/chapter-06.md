# Chapter Six — The Locked Cabinet

Corvin found her at the river crossing the next morning, which meant he'd either guessed her route or asked someone who'd seen her go, and either way had clearly spent more thought on the matter than a man washing his hands of a problem generally bothered to spend.

He was on foot, hatless, carrying a satchel that looked, from twenty feet away, exactly like the kind of thing a guildmaster carried when he wanted to be able to say afterward, with a straight face, that he hadn't brought anything official.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said, before she could ask. "I want that said first, plainly, so you don't spend the next hour bracing for an argument I'm not going to have with you again."

"All right."

"I went back to the cabinet last night," he said, "after you'd gone, and sat with it a long while, the way I do most nights, if I'm honest, since Yvet. And I decided — this is the part I want to be exact about, because I think the difference matters — I decided I still won't hand you confirmed material with the guild's name attached, because I stand by every word I said yesterday about what that name is worth and what I'm responsible for spending it on. But I went through the ledger instead. Every confirmed sighting, every provenance record, every note any alchemist in this guild's whole history has ever made about resonance density near an open wound, going back further than either of us has any business knowing." He held the satchel out. "It's copied. In my own hand, not the guild's official register, which means it's mine to give away and nobody else's business if I do. Call it an old man's private correspondence with a former colleague, if anyone ever asks, which they won't, because nobody's going to ask me about my correspondence."

Wren took the satchel and felt, under the weight of it, something in her chest loosen slightly for the first time since the workshop door.

"Why," she said, simply, because she'd learned, this past week, that the plain question usually got her further than the careful one.

"Because I went home last night intending to feel relieved that I'd done the responsible thing," Corvin said, "and instead I lay awake doing the same arithmetic you've apparently been doing for two weeks, and arrived, somewhat against my own preference, at the same answer. There is no one else. I checked, in my own way, same as you did in yours — sent word to two colleagues in Brindle who'd know if anyone else alive had handled a case like this, and got back exactly what you got from the Court roster, which is regretful silence. I don't love the conclusion. I love it considerably less than I loved my comfortable, well-justified refusal yesterday. But refusing to help you find better information isn't caution anymore at that point. It's just leaving you alone with worse information, which I find I can't actually stand behind, whatever it costs my own peace of mind."

"You could come with me."

"I could," Corvin agreed, "and I've thought about it, and I won't, for a reason I'd like you to actually hear rather than just accept. If I come with you, I bring the guild with me whether either of us intends it or not — I am the guild, as far as most of Confluence is concerned, and whatever happens at the end of that mill channel will happen, in everyone's mind, with Lantern Street's hands on it. You need to be able to do this as yourself. Not as my apprentice, not as the guild's representative, not even, forgive me, primarily as Mari's sister. As Wren Wickern, who knows this material better than I do at this point, whatever I said yesterday about hobbies, and who is entitled to find that out on her own ground rather than mine."

Wren opened the satchel right there at the crossing, unable to wait, and found exactly what he'd promised — page after page in Corvin's blunt, economical hand, decades of guild knowledge nobody had ever bothered to take out of the locked cabinet and actually compare against itself, cross-referenced now, overnight, by a man who'd apparently decided that one sleepless night of real effort was a fair price for not having to live with having done nothing.

There, near the back, in a section headed simply *Anomalous Resonance — Living Subjects*, was a single entry that hadn't been in any of the Court files or guild registers she'd already combed through: a note, undated, unsigned, describing a technique for using a saturated material as a *buffer* rather than a conduit — not to absorb a flooding person's overextension directly, which every text agreed was suicidal, but to interpose, to give the flood somewhere old and patient and already saturated to lean on instead of the helper standing nearest.

"This is new," she said. "I haven't seen this anywhere."

"It's old," Corvin said. "Older than the guild's current cabinet protocols, certainly older than me. I found it folded inside the back cover of the eldest confirmed ledger, no attribution, no date past a watermark that puts the paper at sometime in the last century. I don't know who wrote it, or whether it was ever tried, or whether the reason it's never been copied into the official register is that it didn't work, or that it worked too well and frightened whoever tried it into burying it. I'm giving it to you anyway, because you asked for theory, and this is, as far as I can find, the only theory in three hundred years of this guild's records that even gestures at the shape of what you're trying to do."

Wren read it through twice, there at the crossing, while the river ran past beneath them and Corvin waited with the patient, slightly pained expression of a man who had decided to be useful and was finding the decision more uncomfortable in practice than it had been in principle.

"Thank you," she said finally, and meant considerably more by it than the words alone carried.

"Don't thank me yet," Corvin said. "Thank me if it works, and forgive me if it doesn't, and either way, come back to Lantern Street when this is finished, whichever way it finishes. Your bench is still yours. I told you that yesterday and I find I meant it more than I expected to." He hesitated, in the doorway-shaped way of a man unused to saying the next thing out loud. "Yvet would have liked you, I think. She'd have argued with half your conclusions and helped you test the other half anyway. That's the most useful thing I know how to tell you this morning, so I'm telling you, and then I'm going home, because I find I've used up whatever nerve I had for one day."

He turned and went without waiting for her to answer, which Wren understood, watching him go, was its own kind of gift — the chance to stand at a river crossing with her grandmother's whole life's work in one hand and her guildmaster's careful, complicated faith in the other, and not have to find the right thing to say about either of them just yet.
