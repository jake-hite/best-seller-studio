# Chapter Six — The Locked Cabinet

Wren had left the upriver house before full light, having decided, somewhere around her third hour of failing to sleep, that lying in her childhood bed running the same three unproductive thoughts in a loop was a worse use of the dark than simply walking it off. The road back down to the river crossing was empty at that hour in the specific, slightly eerie way roads were empty right before a city remembered it existed — no carts yet, no gulls yet, just the grey, undecided light and the sound of her own boots, blister and all, finding a rhythm against the packed dirt that was almost, if she didn't think about it too hard, restful. She had her grandmother's journal wrapped against her chest under her coat, which was not, strictly speaking, the safest place to carry a thing that dense with old feeling, but it had felt, leaving the house, like the kind of thing that ought to ride close rather than in a bag at arm's length, the way you'd carry something fragile rather than something merely valuable.

Corvin found her at the river crossing not long after, which meant he'd either guessed her route or asked someone who'd seen her go, and either way had clearly spent more thought on the matter than a man washing his hands of a problem generally bothered to spend. The crossing was quiet at that hour, just a fisherman two piers down working a line he didn't seem especially invested in and the river doing the unhurried, indifferent thing it did every morning regardless of who was standing at its edge deciding the shape of the rest of her life. The water here ran fast and shallow over a gravel bed, loud enough that she heard the crossing before she saw it, and she stood a moment at the rail just breathing the cold mineral smell of it, grateful, in the specific way she was usually only grateful for ledgers, for one more minute of nobody asking anything of her.

He was on foot, hatless, carrying a satchel that looked, from twenty feet away, exactly like the kind of thing a guildmaster carried when he wanted to be able to say afterward, with a straight face, that he hadn't brought anything official. He looked, up close, like a man who hadn't slept properly either — the same grey undertone to his face she suspected was visible on her own, the same careful, deliberate quality to his movements that came from a body running on willpower rather than rest.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said, before she could ask. "Wanted that said first. Saves us both an hour of you bracing for a fight I'm not picking."

"All right." She studied him a moment, the satchel, the set of his shoulders, the particular stiffness of a man who had clearly rehearsed an opening line and was now uncertain what to do with the silence after it landed. "You look terrible."

"I've heard," Corvin said, "that it runs in families, looking terrible after a bad week. I wasn't aware it was contagious across guild lines as well, but here we apparently are."

"I went back to the cabinet last night," he said, "after you'd gone, and sat with it a long while, the way I do most nights, if I'm honest, since Yvet. And I decided — here's exactly where the line sits, so there's no mistaking it later — I still won't hand you confirmed material with the guild's name attached. I stand by every word I said yesterday about what that name's worth and what I'm responsible for spending it on. But I went through the ledger instead. Every confirmed sighting, every provenance record, every note any alchemist in this guild's whole history has ever made about resonance density near an open wound, going back further than either of us has any business knowing." He held the satchel out, and then, for a second, didn't quite let go of it, his hand still closed around the strap a beat longer than the gesture required. "I want one thing understood before you take this. I copied every page myself, last night, by my own hand, because I wasn't willing to let anyone else in that building know this search had happened at all. That cost me the whole night and most of what was left of my own composure, and I am telling you that not so you'll thank me more than you already intend to, but so you understand I'm not handing you something that came easily. It came at the price of a night I don't get back, and a guild ledger I've now got to account for being short a few hours of my own absence from my own bed, which somebody at some point is going to ask me about, and I'm going to have to lie, badly, to a guild I've spent thirty years not lying to. That's the actual cost of this satchel. I'd like it counted."

"I'll count it," Wren said, and meant it, feeling something in her chest pull tighter rather than looser at hearing the price stated so plainly, where she'd expected, walking down to the crossing, only relief.

He let go of the strap, finally, and she took the satchel and felt, under the weight of it, something in her chest loosen slightly after all, the two feelings arriving close enough together that she couldn't have said, afterward, which one had actually been louder.

"Why," she said, simply, because she'd learned, this past week, that the plain question usually got her further than the careful one.

"Because I went home last night intending to feel relieved that I'd done the responsible thing," Corvin said, "and instead I lay awake doing the same arithmetic you've apparently been doing for two weeks, and arrived, somewhat against my own preference, at the same answer. There is no one else. I checked, in my own way, same as you did in yours — sent word to two colleagues in Brindle who'd know if anyone else alive had handled a case like this, and got back exactly what you got from the Court roster, which is regretful silence. I don't love the conclusion. I love it considerably less than I loved my comfortable, well-justified refusal yesterday. But refusing to help you find better information isn't caution anymore at that point. It's just leaving you alone with worse information, which I find I can't actually stand behind, whatever it costs my own peace of mind."

"You said the cabinet stays locked. You didn't say the ledger would too. Those feel like they should have been the same sentence."

"They very nearly were, and I lay there at two in the morning arguing with myself about exactly that distinction, and lost the argument to the part of me that's apparently still capable of losing arguments at sixty-one, which I find oddly reassuring about my own character, even at this hour." He nodded once at the satchel, as though that settled the matter for both of them. "The cabinet's the guild's name and the guild's liability. The ledger, copied in my own hand, off the official register, is an old man's correspondence, and an old man is allowed to correspond with whoever he pleases about whatever bothers him at night. I'm not pretending the distinction would survive a hard look from the Court. I am telling you it's the distinction I've decided to live inside, because it's the only one that lets me sleep at all, and at my age that's worth something."

"There's one more thing." Corvin's voice had dropped, the easy rhythm of a moment ago gone tight again. "If anyone ever sits you down properly — under oath, in front of the Court, not just a guild inquiry over tea — and asks how you came to have guild material with no provenance attached, I need you to have an answer ready that isn't my name. Found it yourself. Worked it out from your grandmother's journal and your own four years on the bench. I don't much care how implausible it sounds to you right now. I care that it's the answer you give."

"You're asking me to lie under oath for you."

"I'm asking you to lie under oath for me," Corvin agreed, without softening it, "which I recognize is a considerably larger thing to ask of someone than handing over a satchel, and I'd rather ask it plainly than dress it up as something smaller than it is. The guild's kept that cabinet locked for thirty years because the one time anybody tried anything new with what's inside it, it went badly enough to frighten three decades of guildmasters out of trying again. I would like, if it's at all avoidable, not to be remembered as the second time it went wrong under my own seal. That's not a small thing I'm asking you to help me protect. It's not a comfortable thing to ask, either, and I won't pretend otherwise."

Wren considered this for longer than was strictly polite, the satchel heavy and real against her chest. "All right," she said finally. "I'll lie for you, if it comes to that. I'm told I'm a passably convincing liar, when the stakes are real enough — which, I gather, they now are."

"They are." Something in his face eased, fractionally, though not all the way. "I'd rather you hear that cost now, plainly, than work it out for yourself later and feel managed by me instead of trusted. That's not a comfortable thing to have just agreed to. It's the honest one, and I thought you'd rather have the honest one."

"I would," Wren said. "I generally do."

"You could come with me."

"I could," Corvin agreed, "and I've thought about it — properly thought about it, not just said no out of habit — and I won't, for a reason I'd like you to actually hear rather than just accept." He looked out at the river a moment, the fisherman's line catching the early light, before he went on. "If I come with you, I bring the guild with me whether either of us intends it or not — I am the guild, as far as most of Confluence is concerned, and whatever happens at the end of that mill channel will happen, in everyone's mind, with Lantern Street's hands on it. You need to be able to do this as yourself. Not as my apprentice, not as the guild's representative, not even, forgive me, primarily as Mari's sister. As Wren Wickern, who knows this material better than I do at this point, whatever I said yesterday about hobbies, and who is entitled to find that out on her own ground rather than mine."

"That's a very generous way of saying you'd rather not watch."

"It's both things at once," Corvin said, without apologizing for it. "I'd rather not watch, and I think you're better off without me watching, and I've found, over sixty-one years, that the two are very rarely as separate as people like to pretend. I'm not going to dress mine up as pure principle. Some of it's principle. Some of it's an old man who's already buried one apprentice and isn't sure his nerve would hold up to standing that close to it twice."

Wren opened the satchel right there at the crossing, unable to wait, while the fisherman two piers down reeled in nothing in particular and the morning went on not caring about either of them, and found exactly what Corvin had promised — page after page in his blunt, economical hand, decades of guild knowledge nobody had ever bothered to take out of the locked cabinet and actually compare against itself, cross-referenced now, overnight, by a man who'd apparently decided that one sleepless night of real effort was a fair price for not having to live with having done nothing. He had organized it, too, in a way that made her chest do something complicated — not by date, which was how the guild's own register filed everything, but by resonance density, low to high, the exact taxonomy she'd have used herself if anyone had ever asked her to. She said as much, out loud, before she'd decided whether saying it was wise.

"You sorted this by density."

"I sorted it the way that made sense to read it in," Corvin said, with the particular flatness of a man pretending not to have done something thoughtful. "I don't see what else there was to organize it by."

"Most people would organize it by date. Or alphabetically, or by which House commissioned the original assessment, because that's how everyone else's filing makes itself feel important. You organized three hundred years of guild paperwork the one way that's actually useful for the specific question I'm asking, at two in the morning, after you'd already decided not to help me." She looked up at him, and something in her own throat had gone tight in a way she hadn't expected from a filing system. "That's not nothing, Corvin."

"It's filing," Corvin said. "Don't make it into a parable." But he didn't quite meet her eyes saying it, which she counted, privately, as its own kind of answer.

She went through it standing at the rail, leaning the satchel's pages against the crossing's low wooden post, working her way through the low-density entries first out of sheer ingrained habit — she had never in her life been able to skip to the back of anything, not a report, not a novel, not, apparently, a guildmaster's hastily transcribed midnight research, and she wasn't about to develop the skill now under time pressure. Most of it was exactly what she'd expected: confirmed sightings of minor residue buildup in inherited objects, a dispute from sixty years back about whether a particular merchant family's heirloom ring had been improperly assayed, a startlingly detailed account of a Material-opener in the last century who'd spent four years trying to prove that certain wyrm scales preferred specific keepers, which the guild had apparently rejected as sentimental nonsense and which Wren found herself wanting, very badly, to go back and read properly at some later, calmer point in her life. None of it was what she needed. She kept turning pages anyway, methodically, the fisherman's line going taut and then slack again twice over while she worked, because the alternative to methodical was panicked, and panicked had never once found her anything useful.

There, near the back, in a section headed simply *Anomalous Resonance — Living Subjects*, was a single entry that hadn't been in any of the Court files or guild registers she'd already combed through: a note, undated, unsigned, in a hand even older and more cramped than her grandmother's earliest volumes, describing a technique for using a saturated material as a *buffer* rather than a conduit — not to absorb a flooding person's overextension directly, which every text agreed was suicidal, but to interpose, to give the flood somewhere old and patient and already saturated to lean on instead of the helper standing nearest.

*The error every prior attempt has made,* the note read, in Corvin's careful transcription of whatever crabbed original hand had written it a century back, *is in believing the helper must be the vessel. A vessel that is already full cannot be filled further without breaking. But a vessel that is already full can be set between the flood and the helper, and the flood, being patternless and seeking only somewhere to go, will choose the path of least resistance — and an old, saturated material, properly woken, offers considerably less resistance than a living person's still-active fear of breaking.* Below it, in the same hand, a single underlined caution: *I have tried this exactly once, on myself, with a vessel I judged sufficiently saturated, and it held. I do not know if it would hold a second time, with a different vessel, or a different flood, or a less careful hand than my own, and I have elected not to find out.*

Wren read it through twice, then a third time, feeling the specific, electric, entirely undignified thrill she associated with finding a genuinely new idea in someone else's hundred-year-old handwriting — the same thrill she'd had at sixteen reading her grandmother's notes on silt and memory, except sharper now, because this one had a shape she could actually use. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone very still around the page, the particular stillness she usually reserved for the moment right before she touched something that might still remember being alive. "Whoever wrote this tried it *once*," she said, mostly to herself, "and then just — stopped. Wrote down that it worked and never touched it again. Do you know how maddening that is? That's the single most interesting sentence I've read in two weeks of research and the person who wrote it apparently decided one success was plenty and went back to filing paperwork."

She read the caution a fourth time, more slowly, the way she'd have re-checked a measurement that mattered. *I do not know if it would hold a second time.* It was, she thought, the single most honest sentence in the entire satchel, more honest by far than anything the official guild register had ever printed, because the official register never once admitted to not knowing something — it simply declined to mention the things it hadn't tested, which she was beginning to suspect was its own quiet form of lying.

"Some people," Corvin said, "have the sense to be frightened of their own results. It's a survivable habit. You might try it sometime."

"I'm trying it right now. I'm extremely frightened. I'm also going to test this within the week, possibly within the day, which I understand is not the same thing as being appropriately frightened, but it's the version of frightened I've got."

"That's not frightened. That's frightened with a deadline, which in my experience produces exactly the same actions as not being frightened at all, just with worse sleep beforehand."

"I'll take worse sleep. I've had worse sleep all week, I'm fairly well broken in by now."

"It's old," Corvin said, more seriously, nodding at the page in her hands. "Older than the guild's current cabinet protocols, certainly older than me. I found it folded inside the back cover of the eldest confirmed ledger, no attribution, no date past a watermark that puts the paper at sometime in the last century. I don't know who wrote it, or whether it was ever tried again after that one time, or whether the reason it's never been copied into the official register is that it didn't work twice, or that it worked too well and frightened whoever tried it into burying it. I'm giving it to you anyway, because you asked for theory, and this is, as far as I can find, the only theory in three hundred years of this guild's records that even gestures at the shape of what you're trying to do."

"Thank you," she said finally, and meant considerably more by it than the words alone carried.

"Don't thank me yet," Corvin said. "Thank me if it works, and forgive me if it doesn't, and either way, come back to Lantern Street when this is finished, whichever way it finishes. Your bench is still yours. I told you that yesterday and I find I meant it more than I expected to."

They stood a while without saying anything further, the fisherman two piers down finally hauling in something small and silver and entirely unremarkable, holding it up to the light for a moment with the specific satisfaction of a man whose morning had at least produced one tangible result, before tossing it back. Wren watched him do it, grateful again for the small ordinary distraction, while Corvin seemed to be deciding something with his jaw, the muscle working once, twice, the way it did when he was choosing between two true things and not entirely sure either was wise to say out loud.

"I went to her grave last night," he said finally, "before I went to the cabinet. Hadn't planned to. Found myself walking that way regardless, the way your feet sometimes decide a thing your head hasn't agreed to yet. Told her about you, a little. Felt foolish doing it. Did it anyway." He hesitated, in the doorway-shaped way of a man unused to saying the next thing out loud, looking out at the river instead of at her. "Yvet would have liked you, I think. She'd have argued with half your conclusions and helped you test the other half anyway, probably before breakfast, probably without asking my permission either." A short, dry breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's the most useful thing I know how to tell you this morning, so I'm telling you, and then I'm going home, because I find I've used up whatever nerve I had for one day."

"Corvin." She said it before he could finish turning, and he stopped, half-away from her already. "You called me the most capable hand to come through Lantern Street in a decade yesterday. You meant that as an argument against letting me go."

"I did."

"It didn't work as an argument. But I'd still like to have heard it from you eventually, on a day that wasn't an argument at all."

Something in his face did the thing it almost never did, which was nearly soften all the way. "Noted," he said. "File it under things I'm bad at saying on purpose, and bring your sister back, and I'll find a calmer day to say it again properly."

"What if there isn't a calmer day?"

"Then I'll have said it once, badly, at a river crossing at an unreasonable hour, which will have to do," Corvin said, "and you'll have heard it, which is the part that actually matters. The rest is delivery, and I've never once in sixty-one years been any good at delivery, as you yourself have just pointed out to my face."

He turned and went without waiting for her to answer further, satchel-strap shoulder rising and falling with the particular unhurried gait of a man who had decided, walking away, not to look back and risk undoing whatever the last five minutes had cost him to say. Wren stood at the rail a while longer after he'd gone, the satchel heavy and real in her arms, the fisherman two piers down recasting his line with the patient indifference of a man for whom this had been an entirely ordinary morning, and found, watching Corvin's shape grow smaller along the river road, that she understood, finally, the particular gift he'd just given her twice over without once calling it a gift — the ledger, and the going, both of them costing him visibly, both of them handed over anyway, with no ceremony to soften either transaction and no real expectation, on his part, of being thanked properly for either. It was, she thought, the chance to stand at a river crossing with her grandmother's whole life's work in one hand and her guildmaster's careful, complicated faith in the other, and not have to find the right thing to say about either of them just yet.

She read the buffer note twice more before she finally folded the satchel closed, committing the unsigned hand's exact phrasing to memory the way she'd once memorized her grandmother's lines about silt and weight — *a vessel that is already full can be set between the flood and the helper* — turning it over against everything she already knew about saturation thresholds, about how the old dye-shed's stone floor would hold steady under repeated testing, about how many days she had before four months of theory had to become something she'd actually tried. The walk back upriver felt shorter than the walk down had, the way a road always did once you had somewhere specific to be on it, and she found herself counting not bridges this time but variables — what she'd need from the shed, what she still didn't know, how long she could reasonably ask her own body to go without proper sleep before sleep deprivation started looking, on paper, like exactly the kind of unmanaged variable Corvin would have flagged in a heartbeat. She had, she decided, about four days before she ran out of either patience or daylight. She intended to use every one of them.
