# Chapter Five — Wickern & Daughters

The house upriver had been her grandfather's, and then briefly, uncomfortably, no one's, and now it was the place the Wickern women went when something had gone wrong enough to need clean water and distance from everyone else's feelings, which Wren supposed made it the closest thing their family had to a temple. She got there by midmorning, footsore and underslept and still turning Corvin's face over in her mind — not his refusal, which she'd expected, but the particular gentleness underneath it, the way he'd handed her his own dead the way other men handed over a weapon, hoping it would do the job of stopping her that his actual authority hadn't.

She had walked the whole way rather than waiting for the morning ferry, partly because waiting meant standing still long enough to think, and partly because her feet had developed, somewhere around the second mile, their own private grievance against her that she didn't have the energy to argue with. Her boots had not been built for a six-mile road; they had been built for a workshop floor and the occasional careful walk to the guild-house's own front step, and by the time the upriver bridge came into view she had a blister forming at each heel with the patient, methodical inevitability of a Bleed she could actually do something about, which was almost funny, in the specific tired way everything had started being almost funny since the boy showed up soaked through at her door. She had not eaten since the evening before — there had been a heel of bread in her travel case she kept meaning to get to and kept forgetting in favor of the next thought, the next mile, the next thing Corvin had said that needed turning over — and her stomach had gone past hunger sometime around the first bridge into the flat, hollowed-out feeling that wasn't quite a complaint anymore, just a fact she was carrying alongside the satchel and the blisters and the long list of things she hadn't slept enough to process properly.

The house itself hadn't changed in the three weeks since she'd last seen it, which was either a comfort or an accusation depending on the hour: the same low stone wall going soft and green at the base where the channel ran past it, the same crooked apple tree her grandfather had refused, on principle, to ever prune properly, the same porch boards that had learned, decades ago, exactly which footsteps belonged to which Wickern and creaked accordingly. The gate latch was the same, too — a stiff iron thing that needed lifting and pushed at the same time, a small two-handed negotiation Wren's body remembered before her mind caught up to needing it, and she stood there a moment with both hands on the warm metal, struck by how strange it was that her hands could hold a piece of three-hundred-year-old wyrm-bone steady through a full resonance assessment without a tremor and still fumble, every single time, this one stupid gate.

She thought, lifting it, of Bartholomew, sitting in his cradle on Lantern Street with nobody to narrate his gauge readings to for however many days this took, and felt a small, specific, entirely irrational pang of guilt about it, the kind she'd have mocked mercilessly in anyone else — the scale did not know how long she'd be gone, the scale did not, strictly speaking, know anything, the scale was a very old, very dignified piece of dead king's spine that had outlasted several Houses and would go on outlasting things long after Wren herself was a ledger entry in someone else's archive — and felt the pang anyway, because four years of talking to a thing that couldn't talk back had apparently rewired something in her that she was not currently equipped to argue with on a six-mile walk.

Mari was asleep on the porch now, in a chair angled to catch the channel breeze, with their mother sitting nearby doing the kind of small, repetitive handwork — mending, today, a basket of it balanced on her knee — that Wren had come, over twenty-three years, to recognize as Hesper Wickern's version of pacing the floor. Up close, in daylight, her sister looked smaller than Wren had built her up to be in three weeks of carrying her around in memory — paler, the bones of her wrist more visible than they should have been, a faint sheen of fever-sweat at her hairline despite the cool morning — and Wren had to look away from that for a moment before she trusted her own face not to show it. She made herself catalogue it instead, the way she'd have catalogued a resonance signature she didn't like the look of: pulse visible at the throat, a little fast for sleep; color poor at the lips but not yet grey; breathing even, which was something, which she decided to let herself count as something. It was a useless exercise. It steadied her anyway, the way counting bridges from a cart had steadied her at twelve, and she let it do its small useless job.

"She had a good night," Hesper said, before Wren could ask, not looking up from the seam she was turning. "Slept through till dawn. Knew my name this morning without having to think about it first."

"That's good."

"It's something," Hesper said, which from her was the more honest phrase. She set the mending down, finally, and looked at her younger daughter properly, taking in, Wren suspected, considerably more than the dust on her boots — the state of her hem, the particular grey under her eyes, the way she was favoring her left heel without seeming to notice she was doing it. "Sit down before you fall down. There's bread in the kitchen and a jug of last week's cider gone properly cold in the springhouse, and you will eat something before you say one more sentence to me, because I can see from here that you haven't, and I refuse to have this conversation with a daughter who's running on nothing but nerve and a six-mile walk."

"I'm fine."

"You're upright. That's a different thing entirely, and you of all people ought to know the difference by now." Hesper nodded at the kitchen door without getting up herself, which Wren understood to mean the matter wasn't actually open for debate, and went, because arguing with her mother about food had never once, in twenty-three years, ended in anyone's favor but Hesper's. She came back a few minutes later with a heel of bread, a wedge of hard cheese gone slightly sweaty in the morning warmth, and the cider, and ate standing at the porch rail because sitting down properly felt, just then, like it might mean not getting up again for a while. The bread was a day old and good for it, the crust gone the particular chewy stage her mother always let it reach before serving it, and the cider was sharp enough to make her eyes water pleasantly, and she ate faster than was strictly polite, aware of her mother watching her do it with the specific satisfaction of a woman whose love language had always run more through full stomachs than through words.

"You went to the guild before you came here," Hesper said, once the worst of the bread was gone.

"I asked Master Ashe for leave and access to the cabinet. He refused both."

"And you're still going."

"I'm still going." Wren sat down on the porch step, because her legs had apparently decided, now that she'd stopped moving, that they were owed some say in the matter. "He told me about Yvet. His old apprentice-sister. I didn't know he'd lost someone that way."

"Most people don't," Hesper said. "He doesn't tell it for sympathy, and I don't think he'd thank you for offering any. He tells it when he's trying to talk someone out of something, which means he must think you a great deal more capable than he's ever let on, because in my experience Corvin Ashe doesn't waste his dead on people he doesn't already believe could actually do the thing he's warning them off of."

"He thinks I'm reckless."

"He thinks you're capable and reckless, which from a man like Corvin Ashe is nearly the same sentence as saying he's frightened for you specifically, rather than frightened of you generally. There's a difference, and I think you heard it, or you wouldn't be sitting on my porch instead of halfway down the river road already."

Wren considered that, and considered, too, the particular shape of her own boots, which had picked up the porch's old grey dust in the last few minutes the way they picked up everything, faithfully and without comment. She had not told her mother yet about the scale folded into the lining of her travel case, the one that had never belonged to the guild's cabinet at all, and some part of her — the same part that had narrated four years of careful measurements under her breath to an audience of nobody — was already composing, privately, the explanation she would give if asked, in case being asked turned out to matter later. It did not turn out to matter just yet. Hesper didn't ask, and Wren, for now, didn't offer.

Wren turned that over for a moment, surprised, as she often was with her mother, by how much careful watching went on behind a face that mostly chose to look unbothered. Out on the porch, Mari shifted in her sleep, murmured something that wasn't quite a word, and settled again. Neither of them moved toward her. They had both, Wren understood without either of them saying so, gotten very practiced this week at telling the difference between the kind of stirring that needed someone and the kind that just needed to be let alone. It was, Wren thought, its own small specialized competence, the kind nobody gave out certificates for: the art of sitting four feet from someone you loved while they suffered something private, and knowing, by some calculus that had nothing to do with training, exactly when to stay seated.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "that I think I've been afraid to ask for about fifteen years?"

"You can ask me anything you've waited fifteen years for. I'd be more worried about the things you haven't worked up to yet."

"Was Mari always like this? Before the Court track, before any of it. Was she always the one who couldn't stop, or did somebody teach her that, the way — " she didn't quite know how to finish the sentence without admitting more than she'd planned to, so she let it sit unfinished and trusted her mother to follow the shape of it anyway.

Hesper was quiet for a long moment, watching the channel water move past at the bottom of the garden, clean and unhurried, exactly the kind of running water this whole family kept fleeing toward whenever something broke. A dragonfly worked its way along the surface near the bank, landed, lifted again, apparently dissatisfied with every single option the channel had to offer it, and Wren found herself watching it instead of her mother's face, because watching her mother's face while she decided whether to answer felt, suddenly, like an intrusion on a kind of privacy she didn't have a name for. The basket of mending sat between them, half-finished, a torn cuff of Mari's own work-shirt waiting its turn under Hesper's needle, the same cuff, Wren noted with the specific dry attention she gave everything, that had probably torn from three years of being yanked at by frightened patients' hands and never once mended properly because there was never quite enough of an evening for it. When Hesper finally set the mending fully aside, she did it the way she set aside anything she'd decided required both hands.

"She wasn't born selfless," Hesper said finally. "Nobody is. I used to believe that about her, the way everyone did — Mari's just naturally good, Mari's just naturally giving, as though it were a color she came out of the womb wearing. But I raised her, Wren, same as I raised you, and I can tell you exactly when it started, because I was the one standing there when it did."

"When?"

"She was nine. You'd have been five, you won't remember it. There was a small flood that spring, nothing like the bad one a few years later, just enough to back up half the lower channel and frighten everyone on the block for a week. And your sister — sweet, ordinary, nine-year-old Mari, who up to that point had been exactly as selfish and exactly as generous as any other child her age — went down to the channel with the rest of the street children to watch, and came home in tears because old Mrs. Aldous two doors down had lost her cat in the flood and nobody, not one single adult on that street, had thought to sit with the woman and let her be sad about it properly. And I told her — God forgive me, I told her, because I thought I was teaching her something good — I told her *that was very grown-up of you to notice, Mari, most people don't notice things like that.*" Hesper's hands had gone still in her lap. "I praised the noticing. I praised it again, two weeks later, when she sat with a different neighbor's grief. And again. And by the time she was twelve I had, without ever once meaning to, taught my own daughter that the fastest, surest way to be loved in this house was to be the one who noticed everyone else's pain before they had to ask, and never, not once, to need the same thing handed back."

"That's not your fault," Wren said, and meant it, though she could see in her mother's face that Hesper hadn't fully forgiven herself the point regardless.

"It's not anyone's fault, and it's also entirely true that I set it. The way you set a dye." Hesper turned one hand over, the old blue-black stain still ghosted faintly into the creases of two fingers, a stain Wren suspected hadn't fully washed out of her mother's hands in thirty years of the trade. "You don't get a color like that in one pass through the vat. You get it a little deeper every single time, until one day it isn't sitting on top of the fiber being admired anymore — it's the fiber." She held the hand up a moment longer, turning it in the porch light as though she were checking it against a sample card, the way Wren had watched her check a hundred finished bolts of cloth across a childhood spent underfoot in the dye-shed. "You can't pull a set dye back out once it's taken, not without ruining the cloth along with the color. You can only ever choose, after, what you run through the vat next time, and hope the next girl coming up behind it doesn't sit in the same bath as long. There's not a House on the Riverbed Council that sat down forty years ago and voted on which kind of daughter ought to run things. I'd stake the whole dye-shed on every fine mother in this city doing some version of exactly what I did, one praised noticing at a time, generation on generation, until you've got a Council dyed that color clean through, and not one woman sitting on it who remembers anybody choosing the dye." She picked her mending back up, mostly, Wren suspected, to have something to do with her hands, and Wren watched her mother's fingers find the needle's eye on the first try without looking, thirty years of the same small motion making the searching unnecessary. "And you. You tested permeable in that same assessment, same year nearly, and you flinched, and nobody in this house ever once praised you for flinching, because flinching isn't the kind of thing anyone thinks to praise. But hear this, plainly: I was never disappointed in you for it. I was disappointed in myself — for not noticing fast enough that the praise I kept handing your sister was costing her something, while the silence I left around you cost you something just as real, the other direction."

Something old and small and not at all kind moved in Wren's chest at that, underneath the part of her that was genuinely moved by her mother's honesty — some thirteen-year-old corner of herself she did not examine often and did not examine now, either, noting, with a sharpness that startled her, that it had taken her sister nearly dying to get their mother to say any of this out loud, and that twenty-three years of quiet, unpraised, perfectly adequate competence had apparently not been worth saying on its own merits. She did not let it show. She had gotten very good, over four years of practicing on a wyrm scale, at not letting things show, and she put the feeling exactly where she put every other want she hadn't invited — somewhere airless, where it could sit without anyone, including her, having to look at it directly.

"I don't know how to be the kind of person who opens toward people the way she does," she said finally, instead of any of that. "I've never known how. I'm not sure I want to be exactly that — I think what it cost her is real, and I don't want to pay it the same way. But I think I might have spent four years building a version of careful that's really just a longer way of saying *closed*, and I don't entirely know how to be anything else."

"No one's asking you to become your sister," Hesper said. "God knows one of those in a household is plenty." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth, not quite a smile, that suggested she'd heard more in Wren's answer than Wren had meant to put there. "I think — " she hesitated, in a way Wren had rarely seen her mother hesitate about anything " — I think I'm asking you to find a third thing. Something that isn't her flood and isn't your wall. I don't know what that looks like. I'm fairly sure nobody in this family has managed it yet, which might be exactly why you're the one who has to."

"That's a very large thing to ask someone over cold cider."

"I'm aware. I'd have liked to ask it of you over something more dignified, a proper sit-down dinner, candles, the works, but you'll notice we don't get to choose the morning the asking actually happens, only whether we say the true thing when it shows up uninvited." Hesper's needle went in and out of the cuff with the same unhurried rhythm it had kept since she'd picked it back up, three stitches, a small adjustment of the fabric, three more, the kind of motion Wren suspected her mother could have kept going through a flood, a funeral, or the end of the world, because some part of Hesper Wickern had decided decades ago that hands occupied with something useful were hands that couldn't be wrung. "I don't actually know what the third thing looks like, mind. I want to be clear I'm not handing you a finished pattern. I'm handing you the problem and telling you I believe you can solve it, which is a worse gift in the short term and a better one, I think, in the long."

"You could have just told me to be braver. Most people would have just told me to be braver."

"Most people haven't watched you solve eleven careful measurements a day for four years running without once asking anyone for help doing it," Hesper said. "I don't think you have a bravery problem, Wren. I think you have a *direction* problem. You point all that nerve at scales and ledgers because nobody ever taught you it was allowed to point anywhere else. That's not cowardice. That's just where the only door in the house happened to be standing open."

They sat with that a while, in the unhurried way of people who'd said more true things in the last quarter hour than they generally managed in a season, and the channel kept moving below the garden, clean and patient, carrying off whatever the morning had stirred up. A water-bird Wren didn't recognize picked its way along the far bank, prodding at something in the silt with single-minded patience, and she watched it for longer than the bird strictly warranted, grateful, in the specific quiet way she was rarely grateful for anything, for a few minutes in which nobody required her to say one more true thing out loud.

It was Mari who broke it, eventually, surfacing out of sleep the way she did everything, gently and without fuss, blinking against the porch light for a moment before her eyes found Wren and focused.

"You came," she said, voice rough with sleep but unmistakably, infuriatingly warm. "You didn't have to run the whole way again."

"I didn't run. I walked very fast and called it walking."

"That's running with better posture." Mari shifted, wincing slightly at something Wren couldn't see, and reached for her hand without seeming to think about it at all, the same automatic warmth she extended to everyone, which had never once in twenty-three years felt like less than the truth and had also, Wren was beginning to understand, never once felt entirely like enough. Her grip was warmer than it should have been, the fever-heat still riding just under the skin, but steadier than it had been three weeks ago in the Lowmarket sickroom, and Wren let herself notice that small mercy before she let herself notice anything else. "Mama's been telling you stories about me. I can tell by your face. You've got your listening-very-hard face on."

"I don't have a listening-very-hard face."

"You absolutely do. Your eyebrows do a specific thing. They've done it since you were nine and Papa tried to explain the tax ledger to you for fun." Mari attempted to demonstrate, badly, an expression that looked nothing at all like whatever Wren's face had actually been doing, and the attempt was bad enough, and committed enough, that Wren felt the corner of her own mouth give before she'd decided to let it. "There. You're smiling. I haven't lost the touch entirely, then, fever or no fever."

"You've lost several stone and most of your color, but apparently none of the touch, no."

"Rude," Mari said, without any heat in it at all, "but probably accurate, and I'd rather have accurate from you than comfort from anyone else right now, so I'll allow it." She shifted again, finding a more comfortable angle against the chair's worn cushion, and looked at her sister for a long moment with the open, assessing curiosity she usually reserved for patients, which from Mari was its own small act of trust — being looked at that carefully, rather than doing the looking herself, never came naturally to her. "She told me about the cat. I know she told you about the cat, because I can also tell, separately, when somebody's just heard about the cat. You get a particular tender, horrified look. It's nearly as good as the listening-very-hard one."

"She told me about the cat. Mrs. Aldous's cat."

Mari's mouth did something complicated — not quite a wince, not quite a laugh. "I'd forgotten I cried about a cat. I remember being so embarrassed about it afterward. Mama made it sound like I'd done something good, and it didn't feel good, it felt like I'd done something wrong and gotten caught liking the apology too much." She said it lightly, the way she said almost everything, but her eyes had gone somewhere distant for a moment, somewhere Wren didn't have a map for. "I used to think you had the easier end of it, you know. Going quiet. Going careful. Nobody ever expected you to fix anything, so nobody was ever disappointed when you didn't."

It landed exactly where Wren least wanted it to land, true and unkind and entirely unintentional, the way Mari's observations about her always seemed to be — a compliment turned slightly the wrong way, close enough to a wound that it found one anyway. "Nobody was ever disappointed," Wren said, careful to keep her voice as light as her sister's had been, "because nobody noticed enough to have an opinion either way."

"That's not the same thing as easier."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't." And found, saying it, that some of the small ugly thing from a few minutes ago had quietly, of its own accord, gone still — not gone, she suspected it never fully went anywhere, but quiet, the way a feeling went quiet when it had finally, for once, been said out loud in a room where someone was listening, even if the someone hadn't fully understood what she was hearing.

Neither of them said anything for a moment after that. A breeze came up off the channel and moved through the apple tree's lower branches, knocking one small, hard, unripe fruit loose to land somewhere in the grass with a soft thud neither of them turned to look at, and Wren found she was glad of the small ordinary interruption, glad of anything that gave the last minute somewhere to settle before either of them had to decide what came next. Mari's hand was still in hers, still too warm, and Wren made herself simply hold it rather than say anything further, because for once the most useful thing she had to offer wasn't a sentence.

Mari's eyes had already started drifting again, sleep pulling at her with the particular insistence Petra had warned them to expect. "I'm glad you're going," she murmured, before it took her fully. "Wherever it is you're going. I can tell by your face you've already decided. You get a look."

"I don't get a look."

"You get a look," Mari said, with her eyes closed, in the last clear voice she'd manage for the rest of the morning, and was asleep again before Wren could decide whether to argue the point.

For a while after that there was nothing to do but sit with it — the porch, the channel, the particular drowsy, overripe heat of a summer morning sliding toward noon, her sister's breathing settling into the slow, even rhythm of real sleep rather than the fitful, fever-thin kind. Wren stayed where she was on the step, her grandmother's name circling somewhere at the edge of her thinking without quite landing, and found her stomach reminding her, with the same dogged persistence it had kept up all morning, that bread and cheese standing at a porch rail did not constitute an actual meal. She ignored it. There would be a proper lunch eventually, the kind her mother made without seeming to plan it, and in the meantime she had the dragonfly and the water-bird and the long patient work of not thinking too hard about the look on her own face to keep her occupied.

Eventually Hesper went inside, and came back out a few minutes later with something wrapped in oilcloth, which she set, without ceremony, into Wren's hands. The oilcloth was old, gone soft and dark at the folds the way only decades of careful handling could make a fabric go, and it smelled, faintly, of the same cedar chest every important document in this family had apparently spent its whole life waiting inside, as though the Wickerns kept their truths in storage right alongside the good linens.

"Your grandmother's actual journal," she said. "Not the copybook pages you've had since you were sixteen — those were the safe parts, the parts about resonance density and storage humidity, the parts I let you have because I thought you'd grow bored of the subject eventually and no harm would be done. This is the rest of it. Her own hand, her own theories, including the parts about handling live residue near an open wound that I always told myself you didn't need yet, because needing it would have meant something had gone wrong enough to require it." She folded Wren's fingers closed over the bundle. "Something has gone wrong enough. I'd rather you walk into this with everything she ever learned than with half of it and a guildmaster's refusal."

Wren held the journal a moment before she trusted her own voice again. It was heavier than the copybook pages, and bound differently — not the neat, later binding someone had done for the safe excerpts, but the original boards, cracked along the spine from use rather than age, the kind of wear that came from a single person opening the same book at the same desk a thousand separate evenings. She could feel, even through the oilcloth, the specific, dense, slightly electric quality of a thing that had been handled with real attention for forty years, the way she could feel it off Bartholomew from across a room, except this was paper and ink instead of bone, and entirely, properly hers.

"You kept this from me on purpose."

"I kept it from you out of fear," Hesper said, "which I am increasingly suspicious is the actual founding dye in every institution in this city, the Court included. I'd like to stop adding to the vat, if it's all the same to you. Go save your sister. Go careful, like you promised. And when this is over, whatever it costs, come home and tell me the truth about it, the whole truth, the way neither of my daughters has ever quite managed to do with me before."

"I will," Wren said, and this time she meant the promise the way Mari always had — fully, without the private hedge she usually kept folded behind her own good intentions — and was a little frightened, holding her grandmother's whole unguarded life's work in both hands, by how much that small shift already felt like the first real cost of this whole undertaking.

"There's one more thing," Hesper said, already turning back toward the kitchen, in the brisk, businesslike voice she used to keep herself from lingering too long on anything that had cost her to say. "You'll want proper food in that bag of yours, not whatever optimistic nonsense you packed last night believing you'd remember to eat it. I'm putting in the good bread, and the hard cheese that travels, and don't argue with me about the weight of it, because I watched you eat like you hadn't seen a meal in a week not an hour ago, and I intend to address that the only way I know how."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were absolutely going to argue. You argue with everyone about everything, it's a Wickern inheritance same as the dye-stains, you just argue quietly enough that people mistake it for agreeableness." She said it with enough warmth that it landed as the compliment it apparently was, and went inside before Wren could decide how to answer that either.

Wren wandered, while her mother was occupied with the bag, as far as the old dye-shed at the back of the property, mostly to give her legs something to do besides sit. The door stuck the way it always had, swollen slightly in its frame from a season of damp, and when she finally got it open the smell came out to meet her before she was even properly inside — madder-root and lye and something mineral underneath both, faint now, gone mostly to memory rather than active use, since her mother hadn't run a vat in this building in years. She didn't go in. She stood in the doorway a moment, looking at the dim shapes of the old equipment under their dust-sheets, the bench along the far wall, the stone floor that would, she noted without yet knowing why she was noting it, hold up perfectly well under exactly the kind of careful, repetitive testing a person might want to do somewhere nobody could hear her arguing with a piece of pottery. She made a private note of it, nothing more decided than that, pulled the door shut against the damp, and went back to the porch before her mother could wonder where she'd gone.

She stayed another hour after that, long enough to watch the fever-sweat at her sister's hairline dry in the channel breeze, long enough to eat the proper lunch her mother produced without seeming to plan it — a thick soup that had clearly been simmering since well before Wren arrived, the kind of meal that asked nothing of the eater except to sit still and let it work, which felt, given the morning, like the most appropriate metaphor available — long enough to help her mother carry the mending basket in before the afternoon heat set in properly, and did not, in all that time, say one word about the look on her own face that everyone in this family seemed able to read except her.
