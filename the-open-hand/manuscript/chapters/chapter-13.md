# Chapter Thirteen — Wyrmsign

"I want to show you something," Vesper said, the next time she came to the dye-shed, "that has nothing whatsoever to do with your sister, your sister's recovery, or anything resembling an emergency. I have decided, against my own better professional habits, that you have spent three weeks being competent under duress and not one single hour being delighted, and I find I cannot, in good conscience, let that continue."

"I delight in plenty of things," Wren said, mildly affronted. "I delight in resonance theory constantly. I delighted in mordant ratios with Bram for an entire hour last week."

"You delighted in mordant ratios as a form of distraction from being frightened. I am offering you delight as an end in itself, which is a different transaction entirely, and I think you'll know the difference the moment you're standing in it." Vesper held up a small folded map, the kind surveyors used, marked in a hand Wren didn't recognize. "There's confirmed wyrmsign in the old quarries past the third lock, fresher than anything the guild's cabinet has logged in a decade. I have a standing arrangement with the quarrymaster that lets me in without the usual fuss. I'm asking if you'd like to come see it before the guild's slow official channels get around to sending anyone, which at their current pace will be sometime next spring."

Wren did not remember agreeing in words. She remembered, mostly, the specific lightness in her own chest at being asked, the first wholly unburdened invitation anyone had extended her in three weeks that didn't have a sickbed or a ledger or a careful negotiation attached to it, and she was halfway into her coat before she'd consciously decided anything at all, narrating the whole undignified scramble out loud to a faintly disapproving Bartholomew-shaped absence in the corner of the shed, because four years of talking to equipment had apparently left a permanent gap in the room that needed filling even when the equipment in question was thirty miles away in its cradle.

The quarries sat an hour upriver, abandoned stone-cuts gone soft and green at the edges where decades of disuse had let the old channel reclaim them, and the quarrymaster — a broad, unhurried woman named Iva who greeted Vesper with the easy familiarity of long acquaintance and Wren with cheerful, unbothered curiosity — let them through a gate with nothing more than a nod and an instruction not to fall in anywhere obvious.

"Another one," Iva said, looking Wren over with friendly appraisal as she latched the gate behind them. "You bring me the most interesting strays, magistrate, I'll say that for you. Last one stayed three hours and asked me more questions about silt composition than my own apprentice manages in a season."

"Wren asks better questions than most," Vesper said, easily, already moving ahead down the old quarry path, and if there was anything in the remark worth lingering on, she gave Wren no time to do it, which Wren noticed only distantly, filed it nowhere in particular, and forgot within a dozen steps in the sheer pull of the place itself.

The wyrmsign, when they found it, stopped Wren entirely mid-sentence in a way almost nothing had managed in years.

It was a shedding trail — a long, curving scrape through a deep quarry pool's silted edge, scales left behind in a loose, glittering line where something enormous and old had hauled itself partway out of the water to molt, and the resonance coming off even the smallest discarded fragment was denser, richer, more layered than anything Wren had handled in her entire professional life, her own beloved scale included. She crouched at the trail's edge and simply held her hand over it for a long moment, not touching, feeling the accumulated weight of it hum faintly against her palm the way a held note hummed against a glass.

"It's enormous," she said, with the specific breathless reverence she usually reserved for nothing at all in front of other people. "Vesper, this is — do you understand how old something has to be to lay down resonance this dense? This isn't decades. This is centuries, layered so evenly it's barely individuated anymore, it's just — it's just *weight*, patient accumulated weight that never once tipped over into anything dangerous because nothing forced it to discharge all at once. It's the gentlest, most enormous thing I've ever touched in my life and it isn't even alive right next to me."

"It's alive about forty feet down," Vesper said, with the dry amusement of someone watching a private joke land exactly where she'd hoped. "I wouldn't recommend introducing yourself. But yes. I thought you'd say something close to that. I said something almost identical the first time I found a confirmed shedding trail, a very long time ago, to a guild mentor who looked at me as though I'd grown a second head."

"What did you say?"

"That it was the most reassuring thing I'd ever felt, because it proved that carrying everything for centuries didn't have to mean becoming a monster about it. My mentor found that a strange thing for a Person-opener to find reassuring. I have never entirely stopped finding it true."

Wren picked through the trail carefully, cataloguing without quite meaning to — three small fragments, a palm-sized curve, and then, near the very end of the scrape where the silt thickened, one whole scale larger than both her hands together, dark as old bronze, denser than anything she'd ever handled including the journal scale that had started this entire chain of events three weeks ago. She held it up to the light, turning it, and felt something in her chest go entirely, helplessly quiet with wonder.

"This is the densest confirmed wyrm-sign material logged in Aldermere in my lifetime," she said. "I'd bet the guild's whole cabinet on it. Vesper, this could—" She stopped, hearing the shape of her own thought arrive a half-step ahead of her words, the way it always did when something genuinely mattered.

"This could refine the buffer past anything you've built so far," Vesper finished for her, gently, watching her face. "Yes. I had a guess that's what we'd find here, which is, I'll admit, the other reason I brought you. I could have come and collected it myself and handed it to you afterward. I wanted you to be the one who found it. I think you'll trust your own hands on this more than you'd ever trust mine, however generously I gave it to you, and I think, given what's coming, you should."

The quarrymaster let them take it, with the easy generosity of someone who'd long since stopped pretending wyrm-sign material belonged to anyone but whoever was foolish and reverent enough to go looking for it properly, and called after them, as they left, with the cheerful indifference of someone making conversation rather than a point: "Bring this one back too, magistrate, whenever you're done with her. The good ones never seem to last as long as the dull ones, in my experience, but she's better company than most." Wren laughed it off as quarry humor and meant the laugh; only much later would she find herself turning the sentence over and noticing it had never specified what *done with her* was supposed to mean, or how Iva had arrived at a category called *the good ones* in the first place, as though there had been enough of them to sort.

She walked the whole hour back downriver cradling the largest scale she'd ever held against her chest like something that might startle if she jostled it, talking — actually talking, longer and more freely than she had to anyone outside her own family in years — about density gradients and shedding cycles and the three competing theories of why wyrms never seemed to over-saturate the way every other creature alive eventually did, and at one point, entirely without meaning to, doing an enthusiastic and frankly unflattering impression of Corvin's face the first time she'd told him she talked to Bartholomew, which made Vesper laugh outright, a real, startled, unguarded sound that seemed to surprise her at least as much as it delighted Wren.

Vesper listened to all of it without once looking bored, asked the kind of follow-up questions that proved she'd actually understood the previous answer, and somewhere in the second mile Wren realized, with a small, surprised jolt, that she had not once, in over an hour of uninterrupted talking about her favorite subject in the world, watched the other person's eyes glaze or her attention visibly drift toward someone more interesting standing nearby. Bram managed it sometimes, when he was tired. Mari managed it out of love rather than genuine fascination, which Wren had always known and never once held against her. Nobody, in her entire adult life, had simply *wanted* to hear all of it, for its own sake, the way Vesper appeared to.

"You actually find this interesting," Wren said, somewhere near the third lock, half a question.

"I find very little else as interesting," Vesper said. "I spent eleven years of my life being interesting to other people in exactly the way the Court required and not one single hour being allowed to be interested in anything myself. I have had twenty-six years since to discover what I actually like, separate from what anyone needed me to be, and it turns out — somewhat to my own surprise, the first time I noticed it — that I like exactly this. Old, patient, complicated things that took centuries to become what they are and never once had to perform their own worth to anybody to be allowed to keep existing." She glanced at Wren, something unguarded crossing her face for a moment, gone before Wren could fully read it. "I imagine that's not an accident, given everything I've just told you about my own history. I find I don't mind that it isn't."

They reached the upriver house as the light was going gold and long across the water, and Wren stood at her own dye-shed door with the great dark scale still cradled against her chest, laughing — actually laughing, unguarded, the specific helpless laugh of someone who has spent three weeks afraid and has just, for one entire afternoon, simply forgotten to be — at something Vesper had said about a guild assessor's face the first time she'd tried to explain wyrm resonance theory to him using nothing but kitchen analogies.

"I haven't laughed like that in longer than I can remember," she said, when it finally subsided, wiping her eyes, slightly embarrassed by her own volume.

"I noticed," Vesper said, quietly, and there was something in her voice in that moment — warm, careful, almost wistful — that Wren would only recognize much later, looking back, as the sound of someone who had not allowed herself to want a thing this ordinary in a very long time, and had just, for one afternoon, let herself want it anyway.

Wren went to bed that night with the great scale on her workbench and her guard, for the first time since the runner's knock three weeks ago, entirely and contentedly down, and did not once think to ask herself why a woman who claimed to want nothing from her yet had spent an entire careful afternoon making absolutely certain she'd be wanted to come back, or why a stranger at a quarry gate had spoken of *the good ones* the way a woman speaks of a thing she has had occasion, more than once, to count.
