# Chapter Thirteen — Wyrmsign

"I want to show you something," Vesper said, the next time she came to the dye-shed, "that has nothing whatsoever to do with your sister, your sister's recovery, or anything resembling an emergency. I have decided, against my own better professional habits, that you have spent three weeks being competent under duress and not one single hour being delighted, and I find I cannot, in good conscience, let that continue."

Wren had been up since before dawn that morning, mixing a mordant bath that refused to hold its color no matter how carefully she measured it, her sleeves rolled past the elbow and her hands stained faintly blue-black at the knuckles in a way no amount of scrubbing ever entirely lifted between batches. She looked up at the announcement with the particular blinking confusion of someone surfacing from four hours of total absorption in a single, narrow problem.

"I delight in plenty of things," she said, mildly affronted, wiping her hands on her apron and making it marginally worse. "I delight in resonance theory constantly. I delighted in mordant ratios with Bram for an entire hour last week."

"You delighted in mordant ratios as a form of distraction from being frightened. I am offering you delight as an end in itself, which is a different transaction entirely, and I think you'll know the difference the moment you're standing in it." Vesper held up a small folded map, the kind surveyors used, marked in a hand Wren didn't recognize, its creases gone soft and pale from being folded and unfolded along the same lines many times before. "There's confirmed wyrmsign in the old quarries past the third lock, fresher than anything the guild's cabinet has logged in a decade. I have a standing arrangement with the quarrymaster that lets me in without the usual fuss. I'm asking if you'd like to come see it before the guild's slow official channels get around to sending anyone, which at their current pace will be sometime next spring."

"You're serious."

"I am very rarely anything else, which I'd have thought you'd noticed by now."

Wren did not remember agreeing in words. She remembered, mostly, the specific lightness in her own chest at being asked, the first wholly unburdened invitation anyone had extended her in three weeks that didn't have a sickbed or a ledger or a careful negotiation attached to it, and she was halfway into her coat before she'd consciously decided anything at all — coat on inside out the first attempt, corrected with the brisk, practiced economy of someone who'd done this exact thing wrong before — narrating the whole undignified scramble out loud to a faintly disapproving Bartholomew-shaped absence in the corner of the shed, because four years of talking to equipment had apparently left a permanent gap in the room that needed filling even when the equipment in question was thirty miles away in its cradle. She grabbed a second, smaller scale-case on her way out, empty, on the theory that any day involving the words *confirmed wyrmsign* was a day that might require somewhere safe to put something, and was rewarded, an hour later, for the optimism.

The quarries sat an hour upriver, and Wren spent most of the walk doing the thing she always did on unfamiliar ground, half-listening to Vesper and half-cataloguing the changing texture of the riverbank under her own boots — the towpath giving way to packed gravel, the gravel giving way, past the third lock, to the particular crumbling pale stone that meant they'd crossed into old quarry country, stone that had been cut by hand two centuries ago and never fully reclaimed by anything except moss and patience. The air smelled different out here, mineral and faintly sweet, the smell of stone that had spent a long time wet and was only slowly, reluctantly drying.

The quarries themselves were abandoned stone-cuts gone soft and green at the edges where decades of disuse had let the old channel reclaim them, water pooled black and still in the deepest cuts, bright and moving where smaller seeps fed in from the higher ledges. The quarrymaster — a broad, unhurried woman named Iva, sleeves rolled to the shoulder and forearms scarred pale in the particular pattern of someone who'd spent thirty years around stone-cutting tools and had stopped being careful about most of them — greeted Vesper with the easy familiarity of long acquaintance and Wren with cheerful, unbothered curiosity, and let them through a gate with nothing more than a nod and an instruction not to fall in anywhere obvious.

"Another one," Iva said, looking Wren over with friendly appraisal as she latched the gate behind them, the iron hinge complaining in a way that suggested it hadn't been oiled since the last time someone had bothered to walk through it. "You bring me the most interesting strays, magistrate, I'll say that for you. Last one stayed three hours and asked me more questions about silt composition than my own apprentice manages in a season."

"Wren asks better questions than most," Vesper said, easily, already moving ahead down the old quarry path, and if there was anything in the remark worth lingering on, she gave Wren no time to do it, which Wren noticed only distantly and lost within a dozen steps in the sheer pull of the place itself.

The path down was narrower than it looked from the gate, switching back twice along a ledge worn smooth by generations of cart-wheels long since rotted away, and Wren found her hand going, twice, to the low stone wall at her left for balance, the surface of it cold and faintly damp under her palm even in the afternoon sun. Iva walked a few paces ahead of them both, unhurried, pointing out a soft patch of ground here, a half-collapsed ledge there, with the easy, automatic care of someone who had stopped finding the place dangerous decades ago precisely because she never once stopped respecting that it could be.

"You run this whole place alone?" Wren asked her, mostly to have something to say that wasn't *I am extremely afraid of falling into that pool and also extremely excited about what might be living in it.*

"Me and the apprentice, when she bothers to show up on time, which is some days and not others." Iva said it without rancor, the particular fond exasperation of someone who'd long since stopped expecting punctuality and started simply being pleased when it occurred. "Guild used to send a proper crew through twice a year, back when there was coin for it. Now it's mostly just me, the water, and whatever decides to come up out of it to molt every decade or so. I don't mind the company being smaller. I mind the budget being smaller a great deal more, but nobody at Council's asked my opinion on that in longer than I care to count."

"What's the apprentice's specialty?"

"Doesn't have one yet. That's rather the point of being an apprentice, I'd have thought, though try telling her that." Iva glanced back over her shoulder, something warm under the gruffness. "She's got good hands. Doesn't know it yet. Most of them don't, at that age."

The wyrmsign, when they found it, stopped Wren entirely mid-sentence in a way almost nothing had managed in years.

It was a shedding trail — a long, curving scrape through a deep quarry pool's silted edge, scales left behind in a loose, glittering line where something enormous and old had hauled itself partway out of the water to molt, and the resonance coming off even the smallest discarded fragment was denser, richer, more layered than anything Wren had handled in her entire professional life, her own beloved scale included. The silt itself had been disturbed in a long, deliberate sweep, the kind of mark that took real weight to leave, and at its edge the water lay unnaturally still, as though even the quarry pool's modest current had learned, at some point, to give the spot a wide berth.

She crouched at the trail's edge, knees in the damp gravel without a single thought for her own trousers, and simply held her hand over it for a long moment, not touching, feeling the accumulated weight of it hum faintly against her palm the way a held note hummed against a glass. Her other hand had already gone, unconsciously, for the small empty scale-case at her hip, fingers working the clasp before her mind had caught up to the fact that she hadn't decided yet whether she meant to fill it.

"It's enormous," she said, with the specific breathless reverence she usually reserved for nothing at all in front of other people. "Vesper, this is — do you understand how old something has to be to lay down resonance this dense? This isn't decades. This is centuries, layered so evenly it's barely individuated anymore, it's just — it's just *weight*, patient accumulated weight that never once tipped over into anything dangerous because nothing forced it to discharge all at once. It's the gentlest, most enormous thing I've ever touched in my life and it isn't even alive right next to me." She was aware, distantly, that she'd raised her voice without meaning to, that Iva had stopped a respectful distance back up the path with the patient amusement of someone who had clearly witnessed this exact reaction in other people before and found it, each time, a little bit funny and a little bit moving. Wren did not care. She had stopped caring about her own dignity roughly the moment her knees hit the gravel, and found the lack of caring, for once, entirely restful rather than embarrassing.

"It's alive about forty feet down," Vesper said, with the dry amusement of someone watching a private joke land exactly where she'd hoped. "I wouldn't recommend introducing yourself. But yes. I thought you'd say something close to that. I said something almost identical the first time I found a confirmed shedding trail, a very long time ago, to a guild mentor who looked at me as though I'd grown a second head."

"What did you say?"

"That it was the most reassuring thing I'd ever felt, because it proved that carrying everything for centuries didn't have to mean becoming a monster about it. My mentor found that a strange thing for a Person-opener to find reassuring. I have never entirely stopped finding it true."

"What happened to him? The mentor."

"Retired, eventually, to a small house on the coast that I understand has an excellent view of absolutely nothing. We exchange letters twice a year. He still thinks I'm strange. I've stopped minding." Vesper said it lightly, but there was something underneath the lightness Wren couldn't quite place — not grief, exactly, but something adjacent to it, the specific flatness of a subject visited often enough to have worn its own edges smooth.

Wren picked through the trail carefully, cataloguing without quite meaning to — three small fragments, a palm-sized curve, and then, near the very end of the scrape where the silt thickened, one whole scale larger than both her hands together, dark as old bronze, denser than anything she'd ever handled including the journal scale that had started this entire chain of events three weeks ago. She held it up to the light, turning it, watching the way the late sun caught and refracted oddly across its ridged surface, throwing a faint, shifting pattern of gold across her own wrist that had no business looking as beautiful as it did for something that had, in a strictly technical sense, fallen off a reptile. She felt something in her chest go entirely, helplessly quiet with wonder.

"This is the densest confirmed wyrm-sign material logged in Aldermere in my lifetime," she said. "I'd bet the guild's whole cabinet on it. Vesper, this could—" She stopped, hearing the shape of her own thought arrive a half-step ahead of her words, the way it always did when something genuinely mattered, and pressed the back of her wrist briefly to her own mouth, an old, unconscious habit from childhood that surfaced only when she was too excited to trust her own voice not to crack on the next word.

"This could refine the buffer past anything you've built so far," Vesper finished for her, gently, watching her face. "Yes. I had a guess that's what we'd find here, which is, I'll admit, the other reason I brought you. I could have come and collected it myself and handed it to you afterward. I wanted you to be the one who found it. I think you'll trust your own hands on this more than you'd ever trust mine, however generously I gave it to you, and I think, given what's coming, you should."

"You could have just said that. 'I have a hunch there's exactly the material you need an hour upriver, let's go look.' I'd have come for that alone."

"I know. I wanted you to have the delight as well as the discovery, and I wasn't entirely certain you'd let yourself have the first one if I led with the second." Vesper watched her weigh the great scale in both hands, something unreadable and almost fond crossing her face. "Was I wrong?"

"No," Wren admitted. "Which is somehow the most irritating part of this entire afternoon."

The quarrymaster let them take it, with the easy generosity of someone who'd long since stopped pretending wyrm-sign material belonged to anyone but whoever was foolish and reverent enough to go looking for it properly. Iva walked them back up the switchback path herself, unhurried, pointing out a fresh rockfall on the upper ledge that hadn't been there on her last circuit and would need shoring before the autumn rains, the kind of small, ordinary maintenance worry that belonged to nobody's crisis but her own and was, for exactly that reason, strangely restful to listen to. At the gate, latching it again behind them with the same complaining hinge, she called after them, with the cheerful indifference of someone making conversation rather than a point: "Bring this one back too, magistrate, whenever you're done with her. The good ones never seem to last as long as the dull ones, in my experience, but she's better company than most." Wren laughed it off as quarry humor and meant the laugh; only much later would she find herself turning the sentence over and noticing it had never specified what *done with her* was supposed to mean, or how Iva had arrived at a category called *the good ones* in the first place, as though there had been enough of them to sort.

She walked the whole hour back downriver cradling the largest scale she'd ever held against her chest like something that might startle if she jostled it, the small empty case she'd brought now full and latched and tucked carefully inside her coat where nothing could jostle it loose, talking — actually talking, longer and more freely than she had to anyone outside her own family in years — about density gradients and shedding cycles and the three competing theories of why wyrms never seemed to over-saturate the way every other creature alive eventually did. She got, at one point, fully sidetracked into a five-minute digression about a footnote in her grandmother's resonance journal she'd never managed to verify, complete with hand gestures she only half-noticed she was making, sketching the shape of a saturation curve in the air between them as though Vesper could read the invisible line as easily as Wren could.

"Your grandmother sounds like she would have liked me rather a lot," Vesper said, when the digression finally wound down, "or hated me on sight, depending entirely on whether she trusted theory built without a guild seal behind it."

"She would have interrogated you for four hours and then decided you were probably right about everything, which is worse, in its way, because then you'd have had to live up to it for the rest of your life." Wren shifted the great scale to her other arm, her shoulders starting to ache pleasantly with the unfamiliar weight of it, and grinned, unable to help herself. "She did that to my mother once. Never let her live down a single overconfident guess for thirty years."

At another point, entirely without meaning to, she did an enthusiastic and frankly unflattering impression of Corvin's face the first time she'd told him she talked to Bartholomew — eyebrows climbing, mouth working soundlessly, the particular long-suffering silence of a guildmaster deciding whether the conversation was worth having — which made Vesper laugh outright, a real, startled, unguarded sound that seemed to surprise her at least as much as it delighted Wren. It cracked something open in her face for a moment, an entirely different woman visible underneath the composed one, gone again almost as soon as it arrived, the way a held breath goes out and is drawn back in before anyone's quite finished noticing it left.

Vesper listened to all of it without once looking bored, asked the kind of follow-up questions that proved she'd actually understood the previous answer — *but if the discharge never happens all at once, where does the excess actually go, does it simply keep compounding forever, or is there some natural ceiling nobody's measured yet* — questions sharp enough that Wren found herself answering them more carefully than she answered most guild colleagues, aware she was being genuinely tested rather than humored. Somewhere in the second mile Wren realized, with a small, surprised jolt, that she had not once, in over an hour of uninterrupted talking about her favorite subject in the world, watched the other person's eyes glaze or her attention visibly drift toward someone more interesting standing nearby. Bram managed it sometimes, when he was tired, his attention drifting to the middle distance in a way he always apologized for afterward and she always told him not to bother apologizing for. Mari managed it out of love rather than genuine fascination, which Wren had always known and never once held against her — Mari's whole face arranging itself into careful, attentive interest the way Wren imagined she arranged it for every patient who needed to feel heard, generous and entirely performed at the same time. Nobody, in her entire adult life, had simply *wanted* to hear all of it, for its own sake, the way Vesper appeared to.

They stopped, briefly, at a flat shelf of rock overlooking the third lock to rest, and Wren found herself unwrapping the great scale again just to look at it once more in the changed light, turning it slowly so the late sun caught its ridges from a different angle, narrating the new angle's effect out loud the way she'd have narrated it to an empty room if she'd been alone.

"You actually find this interesting," Wren said, finally, half a question, looking up from the scale to find Vesper watching her rather than the view.

"I find very little else as interesting," Vesper said. "I spent eleven years of my life being interesting to other people in exactly the way the Court required and not one single hour being allowed to be interested in anything myself. I have had twenty-six years since to discover what I actually like, separate from what anyone needed me to be, and it turns out — somewhat to my own surprise, the first time I noticed it — that I like exactly this. Old, patient, complicated things that took centuries to become what they are and never once had to perform their own worth to anybody to be allowed to keep existing." She glanced at Wren, something unguarded crossing her face for a moment, gone before Wren could fully read it. "I imagine that's not an accident, given everything I've just told you about my own history. I find I don't mind that it isn't."

"That's a very tidy answer for something that started out sounding like it might not be tidy at all."

"Most of my answers are tidy. It's a professional habit I've never managed to unlearn, even for things that don't strictly require tidiness." Something in her tone suggested she'd noticed the same thing Wren had, and decided, for now, to let the observation stand unexamined between them rather than chase it any further. They sat a while longer on the warm stone, the quarry's particular green-mineral smell fading behind them into the more ordinary smell of riverbank and late-season grass, and Wren found she didn't especially want to be anywhere else.

They reached the upriver house as the light was going gold and long across the water, the dye-shed's crooked little chimney throwing a thin line of shadow clean across the yard, and Wren stood at her own door with the great dark scale still cradled against her chest, laughing — actually laughing, unguarded, the specific helpless laugh of someone who has spent three weeks afraid and has just, for one entire afternoon, simply forgotten to be — at something Vesper had said about a guild assessor's face the first time she'd tried to explain wyrm resonance theory to him using nothing but kitchen analogies. She had to set the scale down on the step to manage the laugh properly, both hands free, head tipped back, and somewhere in the middle of it she caught sight of her own reflection in the dye-shed's single dark window and barely recognized the unguarded, open-mouthed delight looking back at her.

"I haven't laughed like that in longer than I can remember," she said, when it finally subsided, wiping her eyes with the back of one stained wrist, slightly embarrassed by her own volume, glancing instinctively toward the neighboring houses to see if anyone had heard.

"I noticed," Vesper said, quietly, and there was something in her voice in that moment — warm, careful, almost wistful — that Wren would only recognize much later, looking back, as the sound of someone who had not allowed herself to want a thing this ordinary in a very long time, and had just, for one afternoon, let herself want it anyway.

They stood a moment longer in the gathering dusk, neither quite ready to end the day, and Vesper finally took her leave with none of the careful formality she'd used the first time they'd met at the lock-house — simply a nod, an unhurried "until next time," and the particular easy backward glance of someone who fully expected there to be a next time and had no reason to make a ceremony out of saying so.

Wren carried the great scale inside and set it on her workbench in the place of honor usually reserved for whatever she was actively working on, and stood looking at it for a long moment in the lamplight before she finally went to bed, her notebook still open beside it with three new pages of measurements she'd recorded half from memory on the walk home, eager to confirm them properly in the morning. She went to bed that night with her guard, for the first time since the runner's knock three weeks ago, entirely and contentedly down, and did not once think to ask herself why a woman who claimed to want nothing from her yet had spent an entire careful afternoon making absolutely certain she'd be wanted to come back, or why a stranger at a quarry gate had spoken of *the good ones* the way a woman speaks of a thing she has had occasion, more than once, to count.
