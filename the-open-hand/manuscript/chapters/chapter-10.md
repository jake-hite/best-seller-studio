# Chapter Ten — Recovery, As Such

They kept her in the Quiet House for three days, in a small room two doors down from Mari's, on the same principle that had built the whole place: running current, close enough to draw the worst of it, and nothing required of her except to lie there and let the channel water under the floor grate do work no one's hands could do faster.

"You're not a difficult patient," the house's healer told her on the second morning, with the particular dry approval of someone who mostly dealt with people who fought the rest they were prescribed. "You're actually the easiest one I've had this season. You just lie there like a sensible person and let the water do its work."

"I diagnosed myself accurately," Wren said, from under a blanket she hadn't moved in an hour. "I Bled. I'm not Saturated. The recovery's supposed to be straightforward — rest, solitude, current, and not using the gift on purpose for a few days. I read the same texts you did. I'm just following the instructions."

"Most people who Bleed don't follow the instructions. They feel fine after a day and go straight back to whatever flooded them in the first place."

"I'm not most people. I'm extremely lazy when given explicit permission to be."

It wasn't quite true, and the healer's face said she knew it wasn't, but she let it stand, which Wren appreciated more than she said.

The honest version was that lying still was the hardest work she'd done in months. Her body had recovered its edges within a day — the shaking gone, her pulse back to its ordinary unremarkable rhythm, the specific blurred place where she'd stopped being sure where she ended and Mari's exhaustion began closed back over, the way a wound closes, leaving a thin new seam where it hadn't been before. But some quieter part of her kept going back to the four minutes inside her sister's flood the way she went back to a measurement that hadn't matched the rest of the set — not turning it over so much as picking at it, worrying the edges, looking for the exact moment the buffer had been right about the principle and wrong about the scale. She didn't have an answer yet. She had a body that knew, now, in a way her mind hadn't fully caught up to, that *theory holds on a clay vessel* and *theory holds on a person who has loved you since before either of you could talk* were not the same sentence wearing different clothes.

She also had, surfacing at odd unguarded moments — staring at the ceiling, halfway through a meal she wasn't hungry for — a small clear fragment that wasn't hers: a child's fear, brave and lonely and entirely unequipped, standing in a doorway over a stranger's grief. She didn't know yet what to do with it. She put it, for now, in the same place she put most things she didn't know what to do with, and tried not to notice how full that place was getting.

Hesper came on the first day and sat with her without saying very much, which was its own kind of gift from a woman who had spent twenty years saying quite a lot. Corvin sent a note by runner — three lines, gruff even in his own handwriting, informing her that her bench remained hers, that the locked cabinet remained locked, and that he expected a full written account of the buffer's working numbers the moment she was fit to hold a pen, "for the cabinet, unofficially, the way everything else between us seems to be these days." She read it twice and found, underneath the gruffness, something that was almost certainly Corvin's version of *I'm glad you're alive*, filtered through a man who would rather discuss ledger entries than feelings for as long as the option remained available to him.

Bram came on the second day, having heard — guild-houses being what they were — within roughly six hours of it happening, and stood in the doorway for a long moment before he said anything at all.

"I'm not going to ask if you're all right," he said finally, "because you'll say yes, and I won't believe you, and then we'll have wasted the only good chair in the room arguing about a word. So. Are you bored. That's the question I actually want answered."

"Catastrophically," Wren said, and meant it, and felt something in her chest unclench slightly at being asked a question with an honest answer available.

He'd brought a deck of cards and, more usefully, a small jar of dye-shed pigment he'd been having trouble stabilizing for weeks, and the two of them spent an hour doing nothing more dangerous than arguing about mordant ratios, which was, Wren thought, possibly the most restful hour she'd had since the runner first arrived at her workshop door. He lost three hands of cards in a row badly enough that she accused him, with full theatrical outrage, of throwing the game out of pity, which he denied so unconvincingly that she counted it as a win regardless. Somewhere in the second hour, with the cards abandoned and the pigment jar between them on the blanket, he said, with the particular careful casualness of someone who'd clearly rehearsed the sentence on the walk over:

"Sela told me what the recovery actually calls for. Properly, not just the resting part. Said it helps to have someone open back to you, on purpose, in a small contained way — gives the gift something honest to settle against instead of just emptiness. I looked it up myself before I came, because I didn't want to ask you to explain it to me while you're like this. I'm not trying to make this a bigger thing than it is. I just wanted to offer, if it'd actually help, and not if it wouldn't."

It was, Wren thought, an almost perfect sentence — careful in exactly the way that left her room to say no without either of them having to feel foolish about it. She thought about Sela's actual instruction, which she'd half-dismissed at the time as the kind of thing house healers said to everyone: that the closing-over after a Bleed went easier, cleaner, less likely to scar into the kind of permanent over-caution she'd watched in older Material-openers, if the practitioner opened toward something living and benign on purpose, in a small contained way, before they convinced themselves the only safe thing left to open toward was a piece of pottery.

"It's small," she said. "Nothing like what happened with Mari, so don't go bracing for anything dramatic, you'll only be disappointed. Just enough to remember what it feels like when it doesn't cost anything."

"Small is fine. Small is the whole offer."

She did it the way she'd done the dye-runoff vessel, the way she'd done it nine times in the dye-shed before she ever tried it on a person — slow, deliberate, watching her own edges the entire time. What came back, when she opened the smallest possible channel toward Bram Coll sitting in the one good chair with a jar of unstable pigment in his lap, was almost laughably gentle by comparison: a kind of low, steady, unbothered warmth, faint worry about her wrapped around something more constant that she didn't examine too closely because examining it felt like the conversational equivalent of touching a hot pan to see if it was actually hot. It was the first time in her adult life she had opened toward another person entirely on purpose, for no clinical reason, with nothing at stake but the opening itself, and it did not flood her, and it did not cost her anything she couldn't immediately afford, and when she closed it again — slow, careful, the way Sela had taught her in the worst hour of her life and that she now got to practice in one of the easiest — it closed clean, no seam at all.

"There," she said, faintly surprised by her own voice. "That's — that actually helped. I didn't expect it to help that much."

"Good," Bram said, with the studied lightness of a man pretending the moment hadn't meant anything in particular to him either, and failing just enough at it that Wren noticed, and decided, for reasons she didn't examine too closely either, to let him have the pretense.

The reports on Mari arrived through Sela, brief and unsentimental, the way Wren had asked for them to be: stable through the first day, then, on the second, a slow re-warming under the skin that the house's healer logged without alarm but without comfort either, the buffer's bought time visibly running its course faster than the four-day theory had promised. By the third day Sela's note read, in full: *Holding. Not improving. You should know the difference matters now, the way it didn't yesterday.*

Wren read it sitting up in the narrow recovery bed, her own edges fully closed, her own pulse fully ordinary, and felt the quiet grim certainty arrive that she had been circling for three days without quite letting herself land on it: the buffer had been the right theory, built well, tested honestly, and it was never going to be enough on its own. It was a bridge, not a bank. It had bought Mari a handful of days at the cost of Wren's own body, and the days were running out the way bridges always eventually did, and there was exactly one person left in Aldermere who had ever stood where Mari was standing and come back from it with something other than rest and time.

She got up, on the fourth morning, dressed without help, thanked the house's healer with the specific brevity of someone who meant it but had no more words left over for ceremony, and went to find Sela at the front desk.

"I need to know where Vesper Reed actually lives," she said. "Not the rumor. The real address. I think you know it, or know someone who does, the same way Petra Vance seems to know everything that matters in this city three days before anyone else does."

Sela looked at her for a long moment — the same assessing look from the doorway four days ago, recalibrated now around whatever she'd watched happen in that small bright room.

"I know someone who knows," she said. "I'll send word today. I want to say, before I do — most people who go looking for her regret it within the season."

"I expect to," Wren said. "I'm going anyway. I've run out of theories that don't require her."

Sela nodded once, reached for paper, and began writing the note in a small, neat hand that gave away nothing of what she actually thought of the errand. Wren stood at the desk in the early light, her own hands steady for the first time in four days, watching the ink go down line by careful line, and found herself thinking — absurdly, helplessly, the way her mind kept doing this lately at exactly the wrong moments — that Bram was exactly the sort of person who would find the dye-shed's resident beetles genuinely, seriously interesting rather than merely tolerable, the way she did, and that she would like, someday, when there was a day that wasn't this one, to show him the cracks in the old vat-house where they collected and watch him take them as seriously as she had. She folded the thought away with the rest of the things she didn't have room to examine yet, and went to go pack a bag for a mill channel she'd never seen, on a road south, toward a woman she had every reason to be afraid of.
