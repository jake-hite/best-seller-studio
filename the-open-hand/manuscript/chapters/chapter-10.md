# Chapter Ten — Recovery, As Such

They kept her in the Quiet House for three days, in a small room two doors down from Mari's, on the same principle that had built the whole place: running current, close enough to draw the worst of it, and nothing required of her except to lie there and let the channel water under the floor grate do work no one's hands could do faster. The room was smaller than Mari's, with a single narrow window facing the side channel rather than the main one, and Wren spent more of the first day than she'd like to admit simply watching the particular quality of light that came off moving water, pale and restless and faintly green at the edges where it caught the old stone sill, cataloguing without meaning to the exact rhythm of it, brighter, dimmer, brighter again, the way she'd have catalogued any pattern she had nothing better to do than watch.

Her body, in the first hours, behaved like something she was renting rather than something she owned — heavy in a way that had nothing to do with actual weight, her thoughts arriving a half-beat slower than usual, as though they had to travel some greater distance to reach her than they ordinarily did. She slept more in that first day than she remembered sleeping in any single stretch since childhood, woke disoriented each time by how dark or light the window had become without her noticing the change happen, and ate, when food appeared, with the specific mechanical thoroughness of someone obeying an order rather than answering an appetite, because Sela had been entirely clear on the point that recovery ran on calories whether or not the patient currently wanted any.

"You're not a difficult patient," the house's healer told her on the second morning, with the particular dry approval of someone who mostly dealt with people who fought the rest they were prescribed. He was a broad, unhurried man named Tobel who'd introduced himself on the first morning with nothing more than his name and a request to see her hands, and had since visited twice daily with the same unbothered thoroughness, checking pulse and color and the steadiness of her grip around a cup with the air of a man running through a list he'd run through a thousand times before and saw no reason to rush. "You're actually the easiest one I've had this season. You just lie there like a sensible person and let the water do its work."

"I diagnosed myself accurately," Wren said, from under a blanket she hadn't moved in an hour, her voice coming out rougher than she intended, the particular hoarseness of someone who hadn't used it for much beyond ordering tea since the morning before. "I Bled. I'm not Saturated. The recovery's supposed to be straightforward — rest, solitude, current, and not using the gift on purpose for a few days. I read the same texts you did. I'm just following the instructions."

"Most people who Bleed don't follow the instructions. They feel fine after a day and go straight back to whatever flooded them in the first place. I've had to drag grown men back to bed by the collar more than once."

"I'm not most people. I'm extremely lazy when given explicit permission to be."

"You haven't stopped working since I met you. You've just relocated the work to your own head, where I can't see it happening and therefore can't object to it as effectively." Tobel said it without much heat, the observation of a man who'd clearly catalogued this particular type of patient before and made peace with not being able to fully reach them.

It wasn't quite true, the lazy part, and the healer's face said he knew it wasn't, but he let it stand, which Wren appreciated more than she said.

The honest version was that lying still was the hardest work she'd done in months. Her body had recovered its edges within a day — the shaking gone, her pulse back to its ordinary unremarkable rhythm, the specific blurred place where she'd stopped being sure where she ended and Mari's exhaustion began closed back over, the way a wound closes, leaving a thin new seam where it hadn't been before. Her appetite came back unevenly, ravenous one hour and entirely absent the next, and she ate what was put in front of her on the ravenous hours with a single-mindedness that surprised her — a thick barley soup not unlike the kind her mother made, bread still warm enough to fog the inside of its cloth wrapping, an apple she ate down to a core so thin it was barely worth discarding, all of it consumed with the specific gratitude of a body that had spent four days in a dye-shed forgetting it needed anything at all and was now, belatedly, making up the difference.

But some quieter part of her kept going back to the four minutes inside her sister's flood the way she went back to a measurement that hadn't matched the rest of the set — worrying at the edges of it in idle moments, looking for the exact moment the buffer had been right about the principle and wrong about the scale, running the numbers again in her head with nothing but the ceiling to write them on. She didn't have an answer yet. She had a body that knew, now, in a way her mind hadn't fully caught up to, that *theory holds on a clay vessel* and *theory holds on a person who has loved you since before either of you could talk* were not the same sentence wearing different clothes. She found herself, more than once, reaching for a pencil that wasn't there, the old reflex to write the discrepancy down before it slipped, only to remember each time that Tobel had confiscated her ledger on the first morning with the flat observation that resting included her hands as well as the rest of her, and that she would get it back when he judged her actually ready to use it rather than merely desperate to.

She also had, surfacing at odd unguarded moments — staring at the ceiling, halfway through a meal she wasn't hungry for — a small clear fragment that wasn't hers: a child's fear, brave and lonely and entirely unequipped, standing in a doorway over a stranger's grief. She didn't know yet what to do with it, and didn't make herself decide right away, on the grounds that a person recovering from a Bleed was allowed to leave at least one question genuinely unanswered for a few days without it meaning anything in particular.

Hesper came on the first day and sat with her without saying very much, which was its own kind of gift from a woman who had spent twenty years saying quite a lot. She came empty-handed — no basket, no mending, nothing to occupy her — which Wren noted dimly, even through the fog of recovery, as more unsettling than anything Tobel had said about her numbers; her mother without a task in her hands was a thing Wren could count the occurrences of across an entire lifetime, and this was one of perhaps three. Hesper sat in the chair by the window instead, hands loose in her lap, and Wren drifted in and out of a sleep too heavy to properly resist, surfacing twice to find her mother exactly where she'd left her, humming something under her breath that Wren recognized, the second time, as the same wordless tune she used to hum over the dye vats when a batch was taking its time to set. Neither of them mentioned the obvious parallel out loud. Hesper kissed her forehead before leaving, an old gesture neither of them had used much since Wren turned twelve and decided she was too grown for it, and said only, "You'll tell me properly when you're ready, and not before, and I'll wait," which Wren understood, even through the fog of recovery, as her mother's own particular version of a promise.

Corvin sent a note by runner — three lines, gruff even in his own handwriting, the ink slightly blotted in one corner as though he'd written it standing rather than seated, informing her that her bench remained hers, that the locked cabinet remained locked, and that he expected a full written account of the buffer's working numbers the moment she was fit to hold a pen, "for the cabinet, unofficially, the way everything else between us seems to be these days." She read it twice, then a third time, tracing the blotted corner with one finger as though it might tell her something the words themselves hadn't, and found, underneath the gruffness, something that was almost certainly Corvin's version of *I'm glad you're alive*, filtered through a man who would rather discuss ledger entries than feelings for as long as the option remained available to him. She wrote back four lines of her own, mostly numbers, because she suspected he'd find that more reassuring than any actual sentiment, and asked Sela to see it sent before she'd quite finished deciding whether four lines was an adequate reply to three days of silence on his end.

Bram came on the second day, having heard — guild-houses being what they were — within roughly six hours of it happening, and stood in the doorway for a long moment before he said anything at all, the kind of pause Wren recognized, even in her current state, as a man deciding which of several rehearsed openings he'd actually use. He looked, she thought, faintly ridiculous standing there in his work apron, which he'd apparently not bothered to remove before crossing half of Confluence to get to her, a smear of something blue-green still drying along one forearm, and the sheer ordinariness of him standing in that doorway, looking like a man who'd come straight from his bench rather than composed himself for a sickroom visit, did more to settle her than any amount of careful composure would have.

"I'm not going to ask if you're all right," he said finally, "because you'll say yes, and I won't believe you, and then we'll have wasted the only good chair in the room arguing about a word. So. Are you bored. That's the question I actually want answered."

"Catastrophically," Wren said, and meant it, and felt something in her chest unclench slightly at being asked a question with an honest answer available. "I have read the one book Sela considered appropriate for a recovering Bleed patient twice. It is about irrigation. I now know more about irrigation than any reasonable person needs to."

"I can have someone bring you something with actual plot in it, if you'd like."

"Please. I will take anything. I would currently accept a guild primer on tax law if it had so much as one character in it I could form an opinion about."

He'd brought a deck of cards and, more usefully, a small jar of dye-shed pigment he'd been having trouble stabilizing for weeks, holding it up to the window light with the specific apologetic pride of a craftsman showing off a problem rather than a triumph. "It keeps separating overnight. I've tried three different binders and it still settles out into two distinct layers by morning, like it's got opinions about staying mixed." Wren made him describe the exact sequence of additions twice, propped up against her pillows with more genuine engagement than she'd managed for anything since Tobel's irrigation book, and offered, by the end of the second telling, three guesses as to the cause, none of which she could test from a hospital bed and all of which she made him promise to try and report back on, because an unsolved technical problem was, she discovered, considerably more restorative to her own peace of mind than any amount of actual rest had been.

The two of them spent the better part of an hour doing nothing more dangerous than arguing about mordant ratios, which was, Wren thought, possibly the most restful hour she'd had since the runner first arrived at her workshop door. He lost three hands of cards in a row badly enough that she accused him, with full theatrical outrage, of throwing the game out of pity, which he denied so unconvincingly that she counted it as a win regardless. "I'm not letting you win because you nearly died four days ago," he said, with the wounded dignity of a man who had, transparently, been letting her win for exactly that reason, "I'm letting you win because you're better at cards than I am, which is a completely separate and much less flattering fact about my own abilities." Somewhere in the second hour, with the cards abandoned and the pigment jar between them on the blanket, he said, with the particular careful casualness of someone who'd clearly rehearsed the sentence on the walk over:

"Sela told me what the recovery actually calls for. Properly, not just the resting part. Said it helps to have someone open back to you, on purpose, in a small contained way — gives the gift something honest to settle against instead of just emptiness. I looked it up myself before I came, because I didn't want to ask you to explain it to me while you're like this. I'm not trying to make this a bigger thing than it is. I just wanted to offer, if it'd actually help, and not if it wouldn't."

It was, Wren thought, an almost perfect sentence — careful in exactly the way that left her room to say no without either of them having to feel foolish about it. She thought about Sela's actual instruction, which she'd half-dismissed at the time as the kind of thing house healers said to everyone: that the closing-over after a Bleed went easier, cleaner, less likely to scar into the kind of permanent over-caution she'd watched in older Material-openers, if the practitioner opened toward something living and benign on purpose, in a small contained way, before they convinced themselves the only safe thing left to open toward was a piece of pottery. Sela had said it standing in the doorway on the first morning, brisk as ever, the kind of clinical aside dropped into the middle of an otherwise ordinary conversation about Wren's pulse, and Wren had treated it, at the time, the way she treated most unsolicited advice about her own gift — noted, technically correct, probably not for her. She revised that assessment now, lying here with an actual offer sitting in the room in front of her, considerably faster than she revised most things.

"It's small," she said. "Nothing like what happened with Mari, so don't go bracing for anything dramatic, you'll only be disappointed. Just enough to remember what it feels like when it doesn't cost anything."

"Small is fine. Small is the whole offer."

She did it the way she'd done the dye-runoff vessel, the way she'd done it nine times in the dye-shed before she ever tried it on a person — slow, deliberate, watching her own edges the entire time, counting the careful half-seconds the way she counted everything that mattered, one, two, the channel widening only as much as she'd decided in advance it was allowed to widen and not a hair's breadth more. What came back, when she opened the smallest possible channel toward Bram Coll sitting in the one good chair with a jar of unstable pigment in his lap, was almost laughably gentle by comparison: a kind of low, steady, unbothered warmth, faint worry about her wrapped around something more constant that she didn't examine too closely because examining it felt like the conversational equivalent of touching a hot pan to see if it was actually hot. There was no shape to it the way Mari's flood had come with shape, no twenty-six years of unspent grief riding underneath — just a plain, unhurried, entirely undramatic steadiness, the resonant equivalent, she thought, of a man who showed up to a sickroom in his work apron because he hadn't thought to do anything more performative with the visit. It was the first time in her adult life she had opened toward another person entirely on purpose, for no clinical reason, with nothing at stake but the opening itself, and it did not flood her, and it did not cost her anything she couldn't immediately afford, and when she closed it again — slow, careful, the way Sela had taught her in the worst hour of her life and that she now got to practice in one of the easiest — it closed clean, no seam at all, no thin new scar left anywhere she could find one.

"There," she said, faintly surprised by her own voice, which had come out smaller and more honest than she'd planned it. "That's — that actually helped. I didn't expect it to help that much."

"Good," Bram said, with the studied lightness of a man pretending the moment hadn't meant anything in particular to him either, and failing just enough at it that Wren noticed, and decided, for reasons she didn't examine too closely either, to let him have the pretense. He picked the cards back up a moment later, shuffling them with more attention than the action strictly required, the small fidgeting business of someone giving his hands something to do while the rest of him recovered its footing. "Best of five. I want a real chance to lose with dignity this time, none of this throwing-the-game nonsense you're so convinced of."

"You're on. Loser owes the winner one honest opinion about whether that pigment is salvageable."

"That's a cruel stake. You already know my honest opinion."

"I do. I'd just like to hear you say it out loud, in a sickroom, where you can't pretend you didn't."

He lost, in the end, four games out of five, fairly this time by his own loud insistence, and delivered his honest opinion about the pigment — *probably not, but I'm not done trying* — with the particular stubborn good cheer of a man who'd clearly decided long ago that a problem worth solving was worth being wrong about a few more times first.

The reports on Mari arrived through Sela, brief and unsentimental, the way Wren had asked for them to be, delivered each morning on a small folded slip of paper that Wren had come to dread and crave in roughly equal measure, the particular dual feeling of wanting information and wanting, very badly, for the information to simply not exist yet. Stable through the first day, then, on the second, a slow re-warming under the skin that the house's healer logged without alarm but without comfort either, the buffer's bought time visibly running its course faster than the four-day theory had promised. Wren read each note standing rather than sitting, as though standing might somehow let her absorb bad news more efficiently, and found that it didn't, particularly, but kept doing it anyway. By the third day Sela's note read, in full: *Holding. Not improving. You should know the difference matters now, the way it didn't yesterday.*

Wren read it sitting up in the narrow recovery bed, her own edges fully closed, her own pulse fully ordinary, and felt the quiet grim certainty arrive that she had been circling for three days without quite letting herself land on it: the buffer had been the right theory, built well, tested honestly, and it was never going to be enough on its own. It was a bridge, not a bank. It had bought Mari a handful of days at the cost of Wren's own body, and the days were running out the way bridges always eventually did, and there was exactly one person left in Aldermere who had ever stood where Mari was standing and come back from it with something other than rest and time.

She lay there a while longer with the note in her hand, running through, out of old habit, every alternative she could think of before allowing herself to settle on the one she'd clearly already chosen — another buffer attempt, refined further, ruled out within a minute on the grounds that the scale's own saturation had limits she'd already mapped and was not going to discover new room inside; a second Person-opener brought in to share the load, ruled out because there was no one else in Aldermere she trusted with her sister's whole unguarded grief, and she suspected, examining the thought honestly, that this was less a practical objection than a confession about her own willingness to share the job at all; more rest for Mari, ruled out last and most reluctantly, because rest had already been tried, for three years, by a woman who didn't know how to take it properly, and hadn't worked. Each alternative took less time to dismiss than the one before it, which told her, more clearly than any amount of staring at the ceiling had, exactly how settled the decision actually already was.

She got up, on the fourth morning, dressed without help — her hands steadier doing the buttons than she'd expected, though her legs complained, briefly and pointedly, about four days spent mostly horizontal — thanked the house's healer with the specific brevity of someone who meant it but had no more words left over for ceremony, and went to find Sela at the front desk.

"I need to know where Vesper Reed actually lives," she said. "Not the rumor. The real address. I think you know it, or know someone who does, the same way Petra Vance seems to know everything that matters in this city three days before anyone else does."

Sela looked at her for a long moment — the same assessing look from the doorway four days ago, recalibrated now around whatever she'd watched happen in that small bright room.

"I know someone who knows," she said. "I'll send word today. I want to say, before I do — most people who go looking for her regret it within the season."

"I expect to," Wren said. "I'm going anyway. I've run out of theories that don't require her."

Sela nodded once, reached for paper, and began writing the note in a small, neat hand that gave away nothing of what she actually thought of the errand, the pen scratching steadily in the quiet of the early-morning desk, the house around them still mostly asleep, the only other sound the same low constant pull of channel water Wren had spent four days learning to stop noticing as separate from her own pulse. "I'll have an answer by tomorrow, if there's an answer to be had at all," Sela said, without looking up. "She doesn't see people quickly, by all accounts, even people with a genuine reason. You should prepare yourself for a wait you don't currently have the patience for."

"I've gotten considerably better at waiting in the last two weeks. Against my own preference, but considerably better regardless."

"That's not nothing," Sela said, echoing, without meaning to, words Wren had heard twice already in the same building from two different people, and looked up from the paper just long enough to add, in a voice gone a degree warmer than her usual flat economy allowed, "For what it's worth — and I don't say this to many people who come through that door — I think you'll manage her better than most who've tried."

Wren stood at the desk in the early light, her own hands steady for the first time in four days, watching the ink go down line by careful line, and found herself thinking — absurdly, helplessly, the way her mind kept doing this lately at exactly the wrong moments — that Bram was exactly the sort of person who would find the dye-shed's resident beetles genuinely, seriously interesting rather than merely tolerable, the way she did, and that she would like, someday, when there was a day that wasn't this one, to show him the cracks in the old vat-house where they collected and watch him take them as seriously as she had. She didn't have room to examine it properly, not today, and let it go without deciding anything about it, and went to go pack a bag for a mill channel she'd never seen, on a road south, toward a woman she had every reason to be afraid of.
