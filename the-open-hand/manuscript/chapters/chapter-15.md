# Chapter Fifteen — Lowmarket, Again

She spent that morning, before any of it, doing the kind of small, ordinary, hopeful work she'd almost forgotten she was allowed to do: sitting at her own bench with the great dark quarry scale and a borrowed scrap of Cae Rookwood's old coat, sent over by his mother at Iris's suggestion so Wren could rehearse the gentler buffer on something that actually carried his resonance signature before she ever brought the real thing near an actual frightened eleven-year-old. The coat-scrap was a sorry little object on its own merits — a square of brown wool no bigger than her palm, frayed along one edge where someone had cut it free of a larger garment with scissors that hadn't been especially sharp — but it held a thin, recognizable thread of him anyway, the specific loose unguarded permeability she'd felt for half a minute in Iris's office, faint now and settled the way a scent settles into cloth long after whatever made it has moved on. She liked working mornings like this. The light through the dye-shed's one good window came in low and amber at that hour, catching the dust she stirred up every time she shifted on her stool, and Bartholomew had taken up his usual post on the windowsill, unbothered by any of it, tail curled neatly around his own feet, which she'd come to find oddly steadying — proof, of a kind, that not everything in the room needed to be in crisis at the same time. Somewhere outside, the dye-works' morning kettle had just come up to its boil, the smell of warm woad drifting faintly through the gap under the door, and a cart of some kind rattled past on the lane beyond, ordinary and unhurried, the whole small world outside her window apparently still under the impression that this was a day like any other.

She'd practiced the seeding technique on it four times already, slow and careful, narrating the whole process under her breath to a Bartholomew who wasn't even in the room to hear it half the time, a habit she'd never quite shaken from her apprentice years, when talking a procedure aloud had been the only way to catch her own mistakes before a master caught them for her. *Small seed first. Something you can afford to lose. Don't reach for the real flood until the seed's settled, or you're building the lock around a key that hasn't finished forming yet.* She'd just begun, with the specific small satisfaction of someone watching a difficult thing finally become easy, to believe she might actually have something gentle enough to offer a child by the end of the week — had even started, in the margin of her ledger, a short hopeful list of adjustments she meant to make before showing Vesper the working version, *smaller seed, slower draw, test on cloth before scale* — when the morning's quiet ended the way mornings in her life had apparently decided they were no longer permitted to simply end on their own.

The runner came at noon, not from Lowmarket this time but from the Quiet House itself, a boy she didn't recognize, breathing hard from whatever pace he'd set himself across the city, holding out a scrap of paper without breaking stride enough to actually stop walking until she'd taken it from his hand. Three words, scrawled in Sela's own unmistakable economical hand, the letters pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the indentation through the back of the page — *come now, faster* — and Wren was out the dye-shed door with the great dark quarry scale still wrapped in its cloth before she'd consciously finished reading them, the ledger left open on the bench with its hopeful half-written list of adjustments, Cae's coat-scrap left forgotten beside it, the morning's small hopeful project abandoned mid-thought the way everything in her life seemed to get abandoned mid-thought lately. She did not stop to put on a coat. She noticed this only once she was three streets gone and the wind off the river had found every gap in her sleeves, and decided, with the particular flat practicality she reserved for facts she couldn't currently do anything about, that it didn't matter enough to turn back for.

She'd meant to test the seeding technique properly first — slow, controlled, the way she tested everything, on something inert before she ever tried it on a person again. She'd had, by her own private schedule, another four days before she meant to risk it on Mari at all, four days she'd already half-planned out in her head: two more sessions on the coat-scrap, one trial on something larger and less precious than Cae Rookwood's actual nervous system, and only then, if every step held, an attempt on her own sister with Sela standing by and a clear hour set aside for it rather than whatever this was. The schedule, it turned out, belonged to her and not to the thing actually happening in her sister's body, and the walk from the dye-shed to the Quiet House — usually a pleasant enough twenty minutes along the embankment, past the lamplighter's boy and the fish-barrows and the dye-works she'd come to think of, almost fondly, as scenery — passed in a kind of blank, breathless arithmetic, her mind running and re-running the seeding sequence she hadn't finished trusting yet, *seed first, small, contained, then open the second channel only once the first has settled,* as though enough repetition could make four practice sessions on a scrap of wool into something it wasn't.

Mari was awake when Wren reached the small bright room, which was itself the first wrong sign — awake, and talking, fast and disjointed, about patients she hadn't treated in months, about her mother's garden, about a conversation with their grandmother that had not occurred in either woman's lifetime within Wren's hearing, her composure not cracked so much as simply absent, the specific even fluency Wren had learned to read as warning replaced by something looser and far more frightening. The room itself was exactly as it had been every other visit — the same narrow bed, the same single window looking out over a strip of grey rooftop, the same smell of willow-bark tincture that Wren had started, without meaning to, associating permanently with fear — and the sameness of it made the difference in Mari worse somehow, as though the room had simply failed to notice that everything in it had changed.

"How long," Wren said to Sela, already unwrapping the scale, her fingers fumbling once on the cloth's knot in a way they hadn't fumbled in weeks, and registering the fumble with a kind of distant, clinical disgust at herself even as she worked it loose.

"An hour, maybe less. She was lucid at breakfast. The healer's reading says the load's climbed faster in the last six hours than in the previous six days combined." Sela's face had none of its earlier professional composure left in it either, and her hands, usually so still, kept finding small unnecessary tasks to do — straightening a blanket that didn't need straightening, moving a cup an inch to the left — the same kind of restless useless tending Wren had seen on Cae's mother two days before, as though competent women everywhere reached for the same small gestures when there was nothing larger left to be done with their hands. "I sent for you the moment I saw the number. I don't have anything else to send for."

Wren sat on the edge of the bed, the new scale heavy and humming faintly in her lap, its surface cool against her palms in a way that felt, absurdly, like the only steady thing left in the room, and did the thing Vesper had taught her in the dye-shed two evenings ago for the very first time outside a controlled exercise: opened toward the scale alone, before reaching for anything else, and gave it something small and entirely her own to measure against — a thin, deliberate thread of her own steady worry, contained, nothing she couldn't immediately afford to lose. She held the seed there a moment longer than she had in practice, half out of caution and half, she suspected, out of a reluctance to find out yet whether any of this would actually hold. The scale took it the way the theory promised it would, settling around the seed of feeling the way a key bedded into a lock, and for one full breath Wren felt something she hadn't felt the first time at all: readiness, rather than blind hope. She allowed herself exactly that one breath to feel it.

It was not enough.

The flood that met her, the instant she opened the second channel toward Mari, did not arrive with the shape of three years of patient, absorbed grief the way it had the first time. It arrived as something closer to static — fast, formless, the underlying structure of her sister's mind no longer holding its careful accumulated order the way it had even at its worst a week ago, every boundary between one remembered patient and the next dissolving into the others, the whole mass of it pressing forward not with intelligence but with the blind, structureless urgency of water that had simply forgotten it was supposed to be a river at all.

The seeded scale caught the worst of the initial surge — Wren felt it happen, felt the prepared channel do exactly what Vesper had promised it would do, drink the first terrible edge of the flood the way the unprimed scale never could have — and for three or four seconds it was working, genuinely working, better than it ever had, and some small triumphant part of her, even in the middle of it, wanted to laugh, or weep, or both, at the simple fact of a theory she'd built in a dye-shed actually holding against something this large.

Then the second wave came, larger, faster, with none of the structure the first wave had at least loosely possessed, and Wren felt the seeded channel's careful, prepared shape simply not be large enough, the buffer's whole design built around the assumption of a flood with edges, a flood that could be measured against, failing in real time against something that no longer had any edges left to measure. It was, in some distant clinical corner of her mind that kept working even as the rest of her began to panic, almost a useful data point — proof that the seeding technique scaled to a point and then simply stopped scaling, the same way any buffer eventually would against a flood large enough — except that the flood in question was wearing her sister's face, and there was no later afternoon in which to write the observation down calmly and consider what it meant.

"Pull back," Sela said, already moving, already at Wren's shoulder with a hand ready on it, her voice gone flat and fast in the particular way Wren now recognized as Sela's own version of panic, the version that sounded, to anyone who didn't know her, exactly like calm. "Now. Don't wait for the clean stopping point, there isn't going to be one this time."

Wren pulled back — fast, rougher than she would have chosen, the closing technique she'd practiced a hundred times on Bartholomew and once, agonizingly, on her own sister, applied now in a version with none of the careful slowness either previous attempt had allowed — and felt the channel tear shut rather than ease shut, a fast ragged closing that left her gasping but upright, on the chair rather than the floor this time, shaken but not Bled, the scale in her lap gone hot to the touch in a way she'd never felt from any piece of wyrm-sign material before, hot enough that she nearly dropped it before she remembered, distantly, that dropping it now would be its own kind of failure.

Mari, on the bed, had stopped talking.

It was the silence, more than anything Wren had felt in the failed channel, that frightened her most — Mari simply lying still, eyes open, breathing, present in the most technical sense available, but absent in every sense that had ever mattered between them, the loose disjointed talking of an hour ago somehow less alarming in retrospect than this complete, exhausted quiet. Wren had spent three weeks cataloguing every version of her sister's face she could imagine — frightened, furious, even Bled and grey on the cottage floor — and had not once, until this exact moment, imagined this one: simply nobody home behind eyes that were still, technically, open and looking at the ceiling.

"Mari," she said, low, not really expecting an answer and not getting one. She reached for her sister's hand on the blanket anyway, because it was the only useful thing she could think of to do with her own hands in the next several seconds, and found it warm and slack and entirely unresponsive to the small, deliberate squeeze she gave it.

"She's not Bleeding," the house healer said, arriving fast with instruments already in hand, a younger man Wren hadn't met before this exact crisis and would, she suspected, remember for the rest of her life purely for the particular careful reluctance in his voice, checking pulse and pupil and the visible heat under the skin with quick, professional fingers that nonetheless moved with none of Sela's usual unhurried confidence. "She's not Bled. This is past that. This is—" He stopped, choosing his next word with visible, careful reluctance, the way a person chose a word they didn't want to be the one to say first, his hand still resting against Mari's wrist as though the contact itself might give him another half-second to decide. "This is Saturation moving toward something I don't have a gentler name for. I need Vesper Reed here within the hour, or I need to know who else in this city has ever actually pulled someone back from this stage, because I am telling you honestly, as a colleague rather than as someone trying to reassure you, that I have only ever read about what comes after this in old Court records, and never once watched it happen, and I would very much like that to remain true beyond today."

Wren looked down at her sister's open, unseeing eyes, at the scale gone cool again in her lap now that the channel had fully shut, and felt the floor of the last three weeks — every careful theory, every bought day, every fragile, hard-won piece of competence — drop away beneath her all at once. There was no list to make here. No measurement to take, no resonance reading to consult, no careful comparison to a wyrm-sign sample that might tell her what came next. She had built an entire identity, these last three weeks, out of being the person who always had a next careful step, and for one long, terrible moment in that small bright room she simply did not.

"Send for her," she said, and was distantly surprised by how steady her own voice came out, given everything else in her at that moment was not. "Now. Whatever it costs, whatever she asks for after this, send for her now and I'll deal with the rest of it later."

She did not look away from Mari's face while Sela ran for the door, footsteps receding fast down the corridor and then the muffled, urgent sound of the Quiet House's own runner being dispatched somewhere beyond the closed door, voices pitched low and fast in the particular register of people trying not to alarm a patient who, in this case, was well beyond being alarmed by anything. She had promised herself, three weeks ago in a different sickroom, that she would not look away again, and whatever else had failed in the last ten minutes, she intended to keep that one promise for exactly as long as her sister needed her to — for as long as it took, however long that turned out to be, for the sound of someone arriving fast enough to matter.
