# Chapter Fifteen — Lowmarket, Again

The runner came at noon, not from Lowmarket this time but from the Quiet House itself, three words scrawled in Sela's own unmistakable economical hand — *come now, faster* — and Wren was out the dye-shed door with the great dark quarry scale still wrapped in its cloth before she'd consciously finished reading them.

She'd meant to test the seeding technique properly first — slow, controlled, the way she tested everything, on something inert before she ever tried it on a person again. She'd had, by her own private schedule, another four days before she meant to risk it on Mari at all. The schedule, it turned out, belonged to her and not to the thing actually happening in her sister's body.

Mari was awake when Wren reached the small bright room, which was itself the first wrong sign — awake, and talking, fast and disjointed, about patients she hadn't treated in months, about her mother's garden, about a conversation with their grandmother that had not occurred in either woman's lifetime within Wren's hearing, her composure not cracked so much as simply absent, the specific even fluency Wren had learned to read as warning replaced by something looser and far more frightening.

"How long," Wren said to Sela, already unwrapping the scale.

"An hour, maybe less. She was lucid at breakfast. The healer's reading says the load's climbed faster in the last six hours than in the previous six days combined." Sela's face had none of its earlier professional composure left in it either. "I sent for you the moment I saw the number. I don't have anything else to send for."

Wren sat on the edge of the bed, the new scale heavy and humming faintly in her lap, and did the thing Vesper had taught her in the dye-shed two evenings ago for the very first time outside a controlled exercise: opened toward the scale alone, before reaching for anything else, and gave it something small and entirely her own to measure against — a thin, deliberate thread of her own steady worry, contained, nothing she couldn't immediately afford to lose. The scale took it the way the theory promised it would, settling around the seed of feeling the way a key bedded into a lock, and for one full breath Wren felt something she hadn't felt the first time at all: readiness, rather than blind hope.

It was not enough.

The flood that met her, the instant she opened the second channel toward Mari, did not arrive with the shape of three years of patient, absorbed grief the way it had the first time. It arrived as something closer to static — fast, formless, the underlying structure of her sister's mind no longer holding its careful accumulated order the way it had even at its worst a week ago, every boundary between one remembered patient and the next dissolving into the others, the whole mass of it pressing forward not with intelligence but with the blind, structureless urgency of water that had simply forgotten it was supposed to be a river at all.

The seeded scale caught the worst of the initial surge — Wren felt it happen, felt the prepared channel do exactly what Vesper had promised it would do, drink the first terrible edge of the flood the way the unprimed scale never could have — and for three or four seconds it was working, genuinely working, better than it ever had.

Then the second wave came, larger, faster, with none of the structure the first wave had at least loosely possessed, and Wren felt the seeded channel's careful, prepared shape simply not be large enough, the buffer's whole design built around the assumption of a flood with edges, a flood that could be measured against, failing in real time against something that no longer had any edges left to measure.

"Pull back," Sela said, already moving, already at Wren's shoulder with a hand ready on it. "Now. Don't wait for the clean stopping point, there isn't going to be one this time."

Wren pulled back — fast, rougher than she would have chosen, the closing technique she'd practiced a hundred times on Bartholomew and once, agonizingly, on her own sister, applied now in a version with none of the careful slowness either previous attempt had allowed — and felt the channel tear shut rather than ease shut, a fast ragged closing that left her gasping but upright, on the chair rather than the floor this time, shaken but not Bled, the scale in her lap gone hot to the touch in a way she'd never felt from any piece of wyrm-sign material before.

Mari, on the bed, had stopped talking.

It was the silence, more than anything Wren had felt in the failed channel, that frightened her most — Mari simply lying still, eyes open, breathing, present in the most technical sense available, but absent in every sense that had ever mattered between them, the loose disjointed talking of an hour ago somehow less alarming in retrospect than this complete, exhausted quiet.

"She's not Bleeding," the house healer said, arriving fast with instruments already in hand, checking pulse and pupil and the visible heat under the skin with quick, professional fingers. "She's not Bled. This is past that. This is—" He stopped, choosing his next word with visible, careful reluctance, the way a person chose a word they didn't want to be the one to say first. "This is Saturation moving toward something I don't have a gentler name for. I need Vesper Reed here within the hour, or I need to know who else in this city has ever actually pulled someone back from this stage, because I am telling you honestly, as a colleague rather than as someone trying to reassure you, that I have only ever read about what comes after this in old Court records, and never once watched it happen, and I would very much like that to remain true beyond today."

Wren looked down at her sister's open, unseeing eyes, at the scale gone cool again in her lap now that the channel had fully shut, and felt the floor of the last three weeks — every careful theory, every bought day, every fragile, hard-won piece of competence — drop away beneath her all at once.

"Send for her," she said. "Now. Whatever it costs, whatever she asks for after this, send for her now and I'll deal with the rest of it later."

She did not look away from Mari's face while Sela ran for the door. She had promised herself, three weeks ago in a different sickroom, that she would not look away again, and whatever else had failed in the last ten minutes, she intended to keep that one promise for exactly as long as her sister needed her to.
